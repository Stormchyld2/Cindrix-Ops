**First, get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.** See the card "Nuclear blast or radiation". Do not leave shelter to look for care or medicine unless officials say it is safe, or a life is in danger.

**What it is:** radiation sickness happens only after a very high dose of penetrating radiation to the whole body (or most of it) in a short time, such as very close to a blast. Many people near a radiation event will not get it.

**Signs, in stages:**
1. **First (minutes to days after):** nausea, vomiting, headache, diarrhea. This can last minutes to several days and may come and go.
2. **Then a quiet time:** the person may look and feel well for a while.
3. **Then the illness comes back** (hours to months): no appetite, tiredness, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and in the worst cases seizures or coma. The higher the dose, the sicker.
4. **Skin:** itching, tingling, redness and swelling within hours to days, and blisters or sores after a high dose. Hair can fall out for a few weeks.

**What to do:**
1. **Wash off dust.** Take off outer clothing, put it in a bag away from people, then wash skin and hair with plenty of soap and lukewarm water. Keep cuts covered while washing. Radioactive dust on or in a person (not the radiation itself) is what can spread to others.
2. Rest and give small sips of fluids. Write down when each sign started.
3. **Get medical care as soon as officials say it is safe.** Doctors treat infection, dehydration, burns and injuries, and some patients get medicine that helps the bone marrow recover.

**Potassium iodide (KI):** it protects only the thyroid from radioactive iodine. It does nothing against fallout dust or radiation to the body. Take it only if officials or a doctor tell you to, use only an FDA-approved product, and follow the package. Table salt, iodized food and iodine supplements are not substitutes and can be harmful.

**Call 911 for:** repeated vomiting and cannot keep fluids down, seizures, confusion or cannot wake, trouble breathing, bleeding that will not stop, or severe burns or blisters. A life-threatening problem is a reason to call even from a shelter.

Source: CDC, Acute Radiation Syndrome; Contamination versus Exposure; Potassium Iodide (KI); Preventing and Treating Radiation Injuries, checked 2026-10-31.
