**Growing your own flour takes many steps. Each stage below links to the page that covers it.**

**1. Choose seed.** **Winter wheat** is sown in autumn and harvested the next summer; **spring wheat** is sown as soon as the soil can be worked and harvested in late summer. Use seed grown for your area (ask a local farm or Extension office). Store seed cool, dry and sealed (see the seed vault pages).

**2. Prepare the soil and sow.** A firm, weed-free bed. Seed rate is roughly **2 to 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet**, scattered by hand and raked in about 1 to 2 inches deep. Winter wheat goes in about 6 to 8 weeks before the ground freezes.

**3. Care.** Wheat takes nitrogen early; follow a legume or compost. Weed while it is small. Birds take seed: cover it with a light mulch.

**4. Harvest** when the straw is golden and the heads are dry and drooping, and a kernel is hard enough that a fingernail can't dent it. Cut with a sickle or scythe, and bind into bundles (sheaves). Stand them in "shocks" in the field to dry for 1 to 2 weeks, out of the rain.

**5. Thresh** (knock the grain out of the heads): flail the heads on a clean tarp or floor, or beat bundles in a sack.

**6. Winnow** (blow away the chaff): pour the grain from one bucket to another in a breeze or in front of a fan. The light chaff blows off and the grain falls.

**7. Dry and store.** Grain must be **below about 13% moisture**. Spread it out for several days. Store in sealed metal or food-grade containers; see the grain storage page. Keep out insects and rodents.

**8. Mill.** A hand-cranked burr mill gives flour. Grind only what you will use in a week or two, since whole-grain flour goes off quickly. Sifting out the bran gives paler flour.

**9. Bake.** Bread needs flour, water, salt and yeast or a sourdough starter.

**How much?** In a good year a garden plot gives roughly **40 to 60 pounds of grain per 1,000 square feet**. A person eating bread daily uses well over 100 pounds of flour a year, so it takes thousands of square feet. Wheat is an important food crop, not a small one.

**Safety: ergot.** A purple-black fungus replaces some kernels in rye (and sometimes wheat) in wet seasons. It is poisonous. Do not eat it: clean it out of the grain and discard it.

**Mouldy or damp grain** should be thrown out.

**Effort:** the work is mostly by hand. Plan for many hours per 1,000 square feet.

Source: general small-grain practice, as taught by state Extension services; USDA. Yields vary widely with weather, soil and variety. Not yet reviewed.
