**The most rewarding garden crop, and the one most worth canning, drying or saucing.** Tomatoes are a **warm-season** crop: frost kills them.

**Timing:** start seed indoors **6 to 8 weeks before your last spring frost**. Move seedlings out 1 to 2 weeks **after** the last frost, once nights stay above about 50 degrees F. Most varieties need **60 to 85 days from transplanting** to ripe fruit.

**Types:**
- **Determinate (bush):** fruit ripens in one 2 to 3 week burst. Best for canning and sauce.
- **Indeterminate (vine):** keeps growing and fruiting until frost. Needs a tall stake or cage. Best for steady fresh eating.
- **Paste types** (Roma, San Marzano) have less juice and make thick sauce with less boiling.

**Planting:**
1. Harden seedlings off first (see the hardening-off page).
2. Plant **deep**: bury the stem up to the first leaves. New roots form along the buried stem.
3. Space **18 to 24 inches** apart (bush) or **24 to 36 inches** (vine), rows 3 to 4 feet apart.
4. Add a stake or cage **at planting**, not later.

**Care:**
- Water **deeply once or twice a week**, at the soil, not the leaves. Uneven watering causes **blossom-end rot** (a dark, leathery patch on the bottom of the fruit) and cracking.
- Mulch 2 to 3 inches deep once the soil is warm.
- Rotate: do not plant tomatoes, peppers, potatoes or eggplant in the same bed two years running (they share diseases).
- Remove leaves that touch the ground. Leaf spots and blight start there.

**Harvest:** pick when fully coloured and slightly soft. Before a hard frost, pick all green fruit that has started to blush or turned pale green. It will ripen indoors, wrapped in paper, over 2 to 4 weeks.

**Seed saving:** most heirloom tomatoes come true from seed. Scoop out the seeds with their gel, ferment in a cup for 2 to 3 days, rinse, and dry on a plate. Hybrid ("F1") seed will not come true.

Source: general home-gardening practice, as taught by state Extension services (university garden guides). Not yet checked against one named page: confirm planting times and varieties with your own county Extension office.
