**A garden full of vegetables can still leave a family hungry. Vegetables are mostly water and give few calories.**

**What a person needs:** roughly **2,000 to 2,500 calories a day** for an adult (more for heavy work, growing teens and nursing mothers), which is about **730,000 to 900,000 a year.** Children need less.

**Calories per pound, rounded (cooked weight is lower, because of water):**
- **Dry grain, dry beans, rice:** about 1,500 to 1,650.
- **Potatoes:** about 350. **Sweet potatoes:** about 390.
- **Winter squash:** about 150 to 200.
- **Corn on the cob (fresh):** about 400. **Carrots, beets, onions:** about 150 to 200.
- **Leafy greens, cabbage, tomatoes, cucumbers:** about 50 to 120.
- **Eggs:** about 70 each. **Milk:** about 150 per cup. **Nuts and oils:** very high (250 per ounce for nuts).

**Calorie crops matter most:** potatoes, sweet potatoes, dry beans, corn and grains, and winter squash. They are the hardest to do on a small plot and the most important.

**A rough idea of area:** estimates vary, but feeding one person most of the year from a garden can take **several thousand square feet** when a lot of potatoes, beans, corn and squash are grown, and much more when it is mostly vegetables. Eggs, milk and meat from a few animals add calories and protein but also need feed.

**What a vegetable-only diet lacks:**
- **Protein:** dry beans, peas, lentils, eggs, milk, meat and fish. Beans plus a grain make a good protein mix.
- **Fat:** seeds, nuts, eggs, meat, milk, and oils. Fat is needed for a lot of the calories in a winter diet.
- **Vitamin B12:** found only in animal foods (meat, eggs, milk, fish). A person eating no animal foods needs a supplement.
- **Vitamin D** (winter sun), **calcium** and **iodine** (iodised salt) may also run short.
- **Vitamin C** is easy from cabbage, peppers, potatoes and greens.
- Salt, sugar and cooking fat usually have to be stored.

**Plan:** work out your family's needs, add what you can grow, and see the gap. Store food to cover the gap and the bad harvest years. Seasonal food needs storage: most of the year's calories come in the autumn.

**First priorities:** potatoes, beans, winter squash, onions, garlic, cabbage and greens. Add corn or grain when you have the room.

**Be honest about yields:** beginners often get half of a book's figures. Plan for a bad year, and keep records.

Source: USDA food composition data and Extension yield estimates, rounded. These are rough planning figures, not promises. Not yet reviewed.
