**A long-storing staple.** Onions form bulbs in response to **day length**, so choose the right type for where you live.

- **Long-day types** (northern states, roughly north of Virginia and Kansas): bulb up when days pass about 14 to 16 hours.
- **Short-day types** (southern states): bulb at about 10 to 12 hours.
- **Day-neutral (intermediate) types**: do reasonably almost anywhere in between.

**Three ways to start:**
1. **Sets** (small dry bulbs): easiest. Plant as soon as the soil can be worked.
2. **Transplants** (bundles of young plants): plant 4 to 6 weeks before the last frost.
3. **Seed**: start indoors 8 to 10 weeks before the last frost. Slowest, but gives the most varieties.

**Planting:** 1 inch deep, **4 inches apart** in rows 12 to 18 inches apart. Onions have shallow roots and hate competition: **weed by hand and often**. Water about 1 inch a week. Feed with nitrogen early in the season; stop feeding when bulbs start to swell.

**Harvest:** when about half the tops have fallen over, bend the rest down and wait a week. Pull on a dry day.

**Cure (this is what makes them keep):** lay them out in a single layer in a warm, dry, airy spot out of direct sun for **2 to 3 weeks**, until the necks are tight and the outer skins rustle. Cut the tops to 1 inch. Store in mesh bags or braids in a cool (32 to 40 degrees F is ideal), dry, dark place.

**Storage tip:** thick-necked onions rot first. Eat those first. Strongly flavoured, high-sulphur varieties usually keep longest.

**Seed saving:** onions are biennial. They flower in their second year, and need a cool winter between. Save seed only from your best bulbs.

Source: general home-gardening practice, as taught by state Extension services (university garden guides). Not yet checked against one named page: confirm planting times and varieties with your own county Extension office.
