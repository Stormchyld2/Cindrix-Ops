**A large animal gives a lot of meat at once and needs skill, cold weather, and a plan to preserve it fast. This page is an outline, not a substitute for learning from someone who has done it.**

**Plan first:** Pick a cold spell, 20 to 40 degrees F, with a few days of cold ahead so the meat can hang. Gather help, sharp knives, a hoist or strong beam, clean water, a tub or table, and containers. Arrange where the meat will go: salting, curing, canning, freezing or sharing with neighbours.

**Keeping it clean:** Meat spoils from the gut contents and dirty hands. Work clean, avoid cutting the gut, and cool the carcass fast: below **40 degrees F** within a day.

**Basic steps:** Humane kill and bleeding, hanging the carcass, skinning (or scalding and scraping a pig), opening the belly and removing the organs without cutting the gut, splitting the carcass or cutting it into quarters, and cooling. A carcass hangs a few days (sheep 3 to 5, pig 1 to 3, cattle 7 to 14) in near-freezing cold.

**Using everything:** Fat to lard or tallow, bones to stock, organs fresh within days, skin to leather where you have the skill, blood into sausage, and trimmings into sausage or dried meat.

**Preserving:** Salting and curing (ham, bacon), smoking, canning meat in a **pressure canner**, drying (jerky), and freezing. Follow tested times. Never can meat in a boiling-water bath: botulism.

**Safety:** Cook pork to **145 degrees F** with a rest, ground meat to 160. Wash hands, knives and surfaces often. Wear cut-resistant gloves. See the butchering safety page.

Source: general livestock practice as taught by state Extension services and the USDA (for example Penn State, Oregon State and Cornell small-farm guides).
