**Pigs turn garden waste, whey and grain into the most calories per day of any farm animal. They are clever, strong and escape artists, and need a good fence and clean water.**

**Timing:** Buy weaner pigs (8 weeks, 40 pounds) in **spring**; they reach butcher weight (about 250 pounds) in 5 to 7 months. Butcher in cool weather for easier meat cooling.

**Housing:** A pig needs a dry draught-free shelter and **shade and a wallow or sprinkler in heat**: pigs cannot sweat and overheat above about 85 degrees F. Bedding must stay dry in cold. Allow 8 to 10 square feet of shelter and 20 or more of outdoor run per pig; more if you want grass left.

**Fencing:** Two strands of electric wire (6 and 12 inches off the ground) around strong posts, with a charger that works. Train pigs to it with a small pen first. A weak fence is the usual failure.

**Feeding:** A growing pig eats about **4 to 8 pounds of feed a day**, rising as it grows. Grain (corn, barley, wheat), peas, garden surplus, acorns and whey all work. Pigs need protein: a pig fed only corn grows poorly. Fresh water always (3 to 5 gallons a day).

**Do not feed:** **Never feed raw or undercooked meat or meat scraps** (disease), or fruit pits, rotten or mouldy feed, or raw potato peel and green potatoes. In peacetime, feeding meat scraps is regulated by law; if you must feed table waste, cook it well.

**Breeding:** A sow comes into heat every 21 days; gestation is **114 days** (3 months, 3 weeks, 3 days). A sow may farrow 8 to 14 piglets. A boar is dangerous: use artificial insemination or borrow one. Give a farrowing sow a clean, deep-bedded pen and keep piglets out of the way of her feet; heat lamps are a fire risk, so use a covered warm creep corner.

**Cutting up:** Scald and scrape or skin; hang and chill the carcass quickly. Salt, smoke, cure and render fat (lard is a calorie store). See the jerky and meat safety page.

**Health:** Watch for a pig that stops eating, has a cough, a swollen face, or a fever. Isolate and call a vet. Treatments are for a vet to give you.

Source: general livestock practice as taught by state Extension services and the USDA (for example Penn State, Oregon State and Cornell small-farm guides).
