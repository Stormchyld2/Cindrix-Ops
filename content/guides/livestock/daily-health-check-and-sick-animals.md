**Most illness shows first as a change in how an animal looks and acts. A quick look twice a day finds problems early.**

**Every day look at:** Does it come to feed? Is it standing with the others? Is its coat smooth? Are the eyes bright and nose dry? Is the dung normal? Is it breathing easily? Is it walking evenly? Is the udder normal? An animal that hangs back, hides or goes off feed is sick until proven otherwise.

**Normal rest temperatures:** Cattle 100.5 to 102.5 degrees F; horses 99 to 101; sheep and goats 102 to 103; pigs 101 to 103; rabbits 101 to 104; chickens 105 to 107. A fever is a degree or two above the top of the range.

**Isolate:** Move a sick animal to a separate pen, away from the flock or herd, with clean water and feed. Wash your hands and boots after handling sick animals before touching healthy ones.

**Emergencies:** Call a vet at once for: swollen left side in a ruminant (bloat), difficulty giving birth, a bone sticking out, heavy bleeding, an animal that cannot stand, collapse, sudden death of several animals, fits, or choking.

**Medicines:** This program does not give animal medicine amounts. For worming, antibiotics, pain relief and vaccines, use your vet or Extension office. Some medicines have **withdrawal times** before milk or meat is safe to eat.

**If no vet can be reached:** Keep the animal warm, dry and drinking, and give good feed. Rest, shelter and water do more than anything else. Keep records of what you tried.

**Notifiable disease:** Anthrax, rabies, brucellosis and some other diseases spread to people. Do not handle or open an animal that died suddenly, with blood from the nose or mouth, without protective gloves and advice. Wash hands thoroughly.

Source: general livestock practice as taught by state Extension services and the USDA (for example Penn State, Oregon State and Cornell small-farm guides).
