**A shortwave receiver picks up radio stations from around the world, with no internet, no phone, and no licence.** It is a way to hear news in a long outage.

**What to buy:** a portable receiver covering **AM, FM and shortwave (about 2 to 30 MHz).** Many small models also have NOAA weather channels, a clock, and a hand crank or solar panel. An external wire antenna (20 to 30 feet of wire, outdoors) improves reception a great deal.

**Where to listen:**
- **International broadcasters** use bands near 5.9-6.2, 7.2-7.6, 9.4-9.9, 11.6-12.1, 13.57-13.87, 15.1-15.8, 17.5-17.9 and 21.45-21.85 MHz.
- **Time signals:** WWV and WWVH in the United States broadcast exact time on 2.5, 5, 10, 15 and 20 MHz.
- **Amateur radio:** you can listen (but not transmit) to hams on 3.5-4.0, 7.0-7.3 and 14.0-14.35 MHz.
- **Numbers stations and utility stations:** in the news, but not useful to a beginner.

**When:**
- **Lower frequencies (5-7 MHz)** are better at night.
- **Higher frequencies (15-21 MHz)** are better by day.
- Reception changes with the season and the 11-year solar cycle.

**Tips:**
1. Listen away from computers, chargers and LED lights: they produce radio noise.
2. Use a long wire antenna outside, away from the house.
3. Turn the tuning slowly. Use the signal-strength meter.
4. Write down the best stations and times in a notebook.

**Advice:** broadcasters change frequencies each season. Keep a printed schedule from the broadcaster's website.
