**Anyone with a radio on the same channel can hear you. Anyone with a scanner or a cheap receiver can hear you from farther away.** Treat the radio like a loudspeaker in the street.

**What not to say:**
- Your full name and address together.
- That nobody is home, or that you have supplies to spare.
- Where valuable things are stored.
- Medical details of other people.
- Anything you would not say in front of a stranger.

**Good practice:**
1. **Listen first.** Wait until the channel is clear before pressing the button.
2. **Be short.** Say who you want, who you are, and what you need. "Base, this is Scout, over."
3. **Pause before you talk,** so urgent traffic can break in.
4. **Plain words.** Avoid "10-codes" unless everyone knows them. Use plain language in an emergency.
5. **Spell hard words** with the phonetic alphabet (see that page).
6. **Repeat numbers and names** when they matter. Write them down.
7. **Say "break break" or "emergency"** only for a real emergency. Stop talking when someone does.
8. **End clearly.** "Over" means you expect a reply. "Out" means you are finished. (Do not say "over and out", which means both.)
9. **Check in on schedule** and keep the transmissions short. It saves batteries and lets others use the channel.

**Codes and privacy:** The FCC does not allow codes meant to hide the meaning on amateur, FRS, GMRS and CB radio, with limited exceptions. Agreed short phrases for an alarm or a meeting ("Red barn at 5") are fine as plain code words for plans. **Do not rely on them to be secret.** The privacy codes on a radio do not make a conversation private.

**Direction finding:** a transmitting radio can be located. In a situation where staying hidden matters, keep transmissions few and brief, and move afterwards.

Source: Standard radio-operating practice taught in amateur radio, public safety and military training; FCC rules. Not yet reviewed.
