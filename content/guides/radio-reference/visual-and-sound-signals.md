**When a radio fails or does not exist, you can still signal. The universal sign for distress is three of anything.**

**Sound:**
- **Three blasts on a whistle,** repeated every minute or so. A whistle carries much farther than a shout and costs far less energy. Keep one on every bag and on every child's coat.
- **Three shots** in the air (only in the right circumstances and place).
- **Three horn blasts** from a vehicle.
- **The reply:** two blasts is the traditional answer to say "I hear you" (some places use three).

**Light:**
- **Three flashes of a torch/flashlight,** a pause, then three more. At night a flashlight can be seen for miles.
- **A signal mirror** is the best daytime signal: it can be seen for **ten miles or more** on a clear day, and by aircraft far beyond that. Hold it near your face, make a "V" with two fingers, and flash the reflection across the target. Even a CD or shiny phone screen works.

**Fire and smoke:**
- **Three fires** in a triangle or line, about 100 feet apart.
- **Smoke:** green leaves or damp material on a fire make white smoke by day; rubber or oil makes black smoke (take care: toxic fumes and fire danger).
- Keep dry wood under cover ready to light a signal fire when you hear an aircraft.

**Colour and shape:**
- **Bright orange or red** cloth, tarps and clothing spread in the open. Tie them to a pole as a flag.
- **Straight lines and right angles** do not occur in nature: use them in ground signals.
- **Signal near a clearing** where searchers can see you, not under tree cover.

**Stay put.** If you are lost or stranded, staying where you are is usually the best way to be found. Tell someone your plan before you leave.

See also the ground-to-air signals page.

Source: US Coast Guard, National Park Service and wilderness survival guidance on distress signalling; the rule of three is widely taught. Not yet reviewed.
