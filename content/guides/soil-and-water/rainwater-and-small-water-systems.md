**Rain from a roof can water a garden for little or no cost. It can also become drinking water, but only after treating it.**

**Check the law first:** some states limit or regulate collecting rain. Look up yours.

**Collecting:**
1. **Roof:** metal, tile or slate roofs give cleaner water. Asphalt shingles may add chemicals; use the water only for the garden. Roofs coated with lead flashing or treated wood shingles are not suitable even for gardens if edible leaves are watered by hand.
2. **Gutters and screens** keep leaves out. Add a **first-flush diverter**, which sends the first few gallons (the dirtiest) away.
3. **Barrel or tank:** food-grade, opaque (to stop algae), with a tight lid and mesh on the inlet and overflow to keep out mosquitoes. Raise it on blocks so a bucket fits under the tap.
4. A roof of 1,000 square feet yields about **600 gallons from 1 inch of rain.**

**For the garden:** apply to the soil, not onto leaves of vegetables you will eat raw. Collect in barrels and use within a few weeks; stagnant water breeds mosquitoes.

**For drinking (emergency only):**
1. Strain through a cloth.
2. **Filter** (a quality filter) and then **disinfect**: boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet) or use treatment tablets or unscented household bleach as directed on a water card. See the water cards in this program.
3. Do not drink water from a barrel that was not food-grade.

**Other sources:** snow melt, dew, streams, ponds. All need treatment.

**Watering by hand:** a watering can or bucket with a rose spout gives deep, gentle water. A clay pot buried in the bed ("olla") releases water slowly to roots. A plastic bottle with small holes upside down in the soil does the same.

Source: EPA and state Extension guidance on rainwater harvesting, paraphrased. Not yet reviewed: check local rules on collection, and treat water before drinking.
