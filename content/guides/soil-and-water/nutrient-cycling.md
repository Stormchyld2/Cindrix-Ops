**Every harvest removes nutrients from the soil. If you take and never give back, the soil runs down and yields fall.**

**What crops take (relative):**
- **Heavy feeders:** corn, cabbage family, squash, tomatoes, potatoes, leafy greens. They need compost or manure each year.
- **Medium:** carrots, beets, onions, peppers.
- **Light feeders and givers:** beans and peas (they add nitrogen), most herbs, root crops in rich soil.

**Ways to give back (cheapest first):**
1. **Compost** of all your garden waste, kitchen scraps and animal bedding, returned to the bed it came from, or the one that needs it.
2. **Leave roots and stalks** of beans, peas and cover crops in the soil.
3. **Legumes and cover crops:** clover, vetch, peas, and beans fix nitrogen from the air.
4. **Manure:** from your own animals, aged or composted (see sanitation page).
5. **Ash from clean wood** (a little, as a source of potassium and lime).
6. **Bones, shells and eggshells**, crushed, are slow sources of calcium and phosphorus.
7. **Mulch** of leaves and straw, which rots into the soil.

**Keep the cycle closed:** what leaves the garden as food has to come back as compost. If you sell or give away crops, or import food and compost the scraps elsewhere, you are mining the soil.

**A rough yearly plan:** 1 to 2 inches of compost on the beds, plus a legume or cover crop in each bed in a three or four year rotation.

**Watch the signs:** smaller plants, pale leaves, and falling yields tell you nutrients are running low. A soil test every 2 to 3 years confirms which ones.

**Plan for long emergencies:** commercial fertiliser may not be available. Soil fertility from compost, legumes and manure builds slowly (years), so start early, and expect smaller yields than with fertiliser until the soil builds up.

Source: general gardening and farm practice, as taught by state Extension services, USDA and the EPA. Not yet reviewed: confirm with your county Extension office.
