**Fruit is easy to keep because it is naturally acidic.** The tested times for boiling-water canning are in the USDA guide (GUIDE02 and GUIDE07). Use those; this page explains the ideas.

**Ways to keep fruit:**
- **Dry** (apples, pears, apricots, berries, grapes as raisins): see the drying page.
- **Freeze** on trays, then bag. Add lemon juice to apples and peaches to stop browning.
- **Boiling-water canning** for fruit, jam, jelly, and tomatoes with added acid.
- **Cellar** apples and pears, wrapped separately.
- **Fruit leather:** puree, spread thin on a tray, dry until no longer sticky.
- **Vinegar and fruit ferments** (cider, vinegar) store for a long time.

**Jam and jelly basics:**
1. Fruit, sugar and **acid** (lemon juice) with **pectin** (naturally in apples, citrus peel, and under-ripe berries) set to a gel.
2. Pectin needs the right balance. Do not change the sugar in a recipe made for a regular pectin. Use **low-sugar pectin** recipes if you want less sugar.
3. **Never double a batch.** Large batches don't set well.
4. **Use tested recipes** and the processing times in the USDA guide. Process in a boiling-water canner; the old "turn the jar upside down" method is no longer approved.
5. Skim foam, use clean jars with new flat lids, wipe the rim, and leave headspace of 1/4 inch.

**Test the set:** put a spoonful on a plate chilled in the freezer; if it wrinkles when pushed after a minute, it's done.

**Honey and sugar** are natural preservatives, but **honey must not be fed to babies under 1 year**. Honey kept sealed and dry lasts indefinitely.

**Mould on jam:** throw out the whole jar. Do not scrape it off.

Source: the National Center for Home Food Preservation (nchfp.uga.edu) and USDA guidance, paraphrased; state Extension services. Not yet reviewed: check against the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and the Shelf manuals.
