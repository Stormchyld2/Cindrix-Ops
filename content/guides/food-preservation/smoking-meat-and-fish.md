**Smoke dries the surface, adds flavor and slows spoilage, but it does not make unsafe meat safe.** Cure or salt first, then smoke, and keep it cold.

**Cold smoking (under about 90 degrees F)** flavors and slowly dries cured meat and fish. It does not cook them. It needs a **proper cure first** and good control. It is **risky** without experience.

**Hot smoking (about 165 to 250 degrees F)** cooks the food. Meat should reach a **safe internal temperature** (pork and beef 145 degrees F, poultry 165, fish 145). Hot-smoked food is not shelf stable unless dried a lot, so keep it cold or freeze it.

**Steps:**
1. Cure or brine the meat or fish first, with a tested recipe.
2. Rinse and air dry until a **tacky skin (pellicle)** forms. This helps smoke stick.
3. Use **hardwood** (hickory, oak, apple, maple). **Never use** softwood like pine or any treated wood: it is bitter and can be toxic.
4. Keep smoke thin and blue, not thick and white.
5. Use a thermometer in the meat and in the smoker.
6. After smoking, cool and wrap. Store cold. Dried, hard-smoked meat keeps longer, but check it often.

**Safety:** smoking happens **outdoors only.** Smoke contains carbon monoxide. Keep fire in a safe place with water and a shovel nearby.

**Do not** smoke at too low a temperature for a long time without curing. That is how bacteria grow.

Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service and National Center for Home Food Preservation guidance, paraphrased.
