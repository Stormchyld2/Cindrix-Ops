**Water-bath canning is for high-acid foods only:** most fruit, jam and jelly, pickles, and tomatoes with added acid. Low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, beans, soup) are **never** safe in a water bath. Use pressure canning for those.

**What you need:** a large pot with a rack and lid, canning jars, new flat lids and bands, a jar lifter, a funnel, a ladle, and a **tested recipe.**

**Steps:**
1. **Use a tested recipe** from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or the National Center for Home Food Preservation. Do not change the amount of acid, sugar or vegetables in a recipe that is not meant for it.
2. Wash jars and keep them hot. Keep the water in the pot hot, not boiling.
3. Fill hot jars with the food, leaving the **headspace** the recipe says. Remove air bubbles with a clean spatula. Wipe the rim.
4. Put on the lid and band **fingertip tight.**
5. Lower jars onto the rack in hot water, with at least an inch of water over the tops. Bring to a rolling boil and **start timing then.**
6. Process for the time in your recipe. **Add time for altitude:** the recipe's chart tells you how much. West Virginia and eastern Tennessee hills often need extra time.
7. Turn off the heat, wait 5 minutes, then lift jars straight up onto a towel. Do not tilt or tighten. Let them sit 12 to 24 hours.
8. **Check the seal:** the lid should not flex when pressed. Remove the bands, wash and label with the date. Store in a cool, dark place. Use within a year for best quality.

**If a jar does not seal** in 24 hours, refrigerate it and use soon, or reprocess with a new lid within 24 hours.

**Throw away without tasting:** a bulging lid, a leak, spurting liquid, cloudy or moldy food, or a bad smell. **Never taste to test.**

**Without power or a stove:** a camp stove or fire with a big enough pot works. Keep a thermometer or watch, and a good ladle.

Source: USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and National Center for Home Food Preservation, paraphrased.
