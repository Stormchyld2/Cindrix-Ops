This lesson is a careful walkthrough. Read it, then practice on a manikin in a class.

## Before you begin
- **Is the scene safe?**
- **Gloves and a face shield** if you have them. Do not delay for them.
- **The person should be on a firm, flat surface,** on their back. If they are on a bed or sofa, move them to the floor if you can.

## Step by step
1. **Tap and shout.** Tap their shoulders firmly and shout, "Are you okay?"
2. **Call 911.** Point at a specific person: "You, call 911 and bring an AED." If alone, use speakerphone, and put the phone beside you.
3. **Check breathing.** Look at the chest for up to **10 seconds.** No breathing or only gasping means start CPR.
4. **Expose the chest** if possible, to place your hands. Do not delay for this.
5. **Hand position:** place the **heel of one hand in the center of the chest,** on the lower half of the breastbone. Put your other hand on top and lace your fingers. Keep your fingers off the ribs.
6. **Body position:** kneel beside the person. Put your shoulders directly over your hands. **Lock your elbows** and keep your arms straight.
7. **Compress:** push **straight down hard and fast.**
   - **Rate:** **100 to 120 per minute.** This is the beat of "Stayin' Alive" or "Baby Shark."
   - **Depth:** **at least 2 inches** (5 cm), but not more than about 2.4 inches (6 cm).
   - **Recoil:** let the chest **rise fully** after every push. Do not lean on it.
8. **Minimize pauses.** Pause no more than 10 seconds.
9. **Count out loud** to keep pace and so others can hear: "one and two and three and four ..."
10. **Switch** with another rescuer every **2 minutes** (about five cycles), or sooner if you tire. Make the swap in under 5 seconds.

## Adding breaths (if trained and willing)
- After **30 compressions,** open the airway: **tilt the head back and lift the chin.**
- **Pinch the nose,** seal your mouth over theirs (or use a face shield) and give **2 breaths,** each about **one second,** enough to make the chest visibly rise.
- **Return to compressions** at once. The whole breath pause should be under 10 seconds.
- Use a **ratio of 30 compressions to 2 breaths.**

If breaths do not go in, re-tilt the head and try once more. Then go straight back to compressions. **Do not spend time on repeated attempts.**

## Hands-only CPR
If you are **not trained, unsure, or unwilling to give breaths,** do **continuous chest compressions** at 100 to 120 per minute. This is effective for adults who collapse suddenly. Keep going until help or an AED arrives.

## Use the AED
As soon as an AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice prompts (see the AED lesson).

## When to stop
- **A trained professional takes over.**
- **The person starts to breathe or move.** Put them on their side (recovery position) and watch.
- **An AED tells you to stop** (it will say when it is analyzing).
- **You are too exhausted** to continue.
- **The scene becomes unsafe.**

## Special situations
- **Pregnancy:** do CPR as usual. Help the pregnant person on their back. If a second rescuer is there, they can gently push the belly to the left. Use an AED as usual.
- **Larger bodies, people with breast implants, people with pacemakers:** do CPR as usual.
- **Water:** remove from water and begin. If drowning, rescue breaths are especially important (see the drowning lesson).
- **Opioid overdose:** give naloxone if you have it, and CPR with breaths if possible.
- **Hypothermia:** CPR and warming. Do not stop.
- **Electrocution:** make sure the power is off before touching the person.

## Common mistakes
- **Pushing too shallow.** Push hard.
- **Pushing too slow or too fast.**
- **Not letting the chest rise.**
- **Pausing too long** to check the pulse or give breaths.
- **Hesitating** for fear of hurting them. Broken ribs happen and are not a reason to stop.
- **Stopping too soon.**

## Checklist
- Know the steps: tap and shout, call, check breathing, compress
- Rate 100 to 120, depth at least 2 inches, full recoil
- Switch every 2 minutes
- Know when hands-only is acceptable
- Practiced on a manikin

> This is education, not a substitute for a hands-on CPR and first aid class from the American Red Cross, American Heart Association or similar. It never gives medicine amounts. Follow product labels and your doctor, and call 911 in an emergency.
