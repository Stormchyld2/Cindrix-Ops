Maintain a minimum level of physical functionality under load. Your body is your **primary** tool. If you are severely overweight, unable to walk more than a few hundred feet, or unable to do physical labor, your chances in a serious emergency go down. And even if you are not worried for yourself, think about the family members or group mates who count on you.

Being physically fit also improves your quality of life now: more energy, better sleep, better mood and fewer illnesses.

## A simple plan
There is no secret. It comes down to two things: **eat real food** and **exercise.** Consistency beats intensity.

## The baseline test (healthy adults, 18 to 65)
Check with your doctor before you start if you have a health condition, are pregnant, or have not exercised for a long time. Then measure where you are:

- **Walk 1 mile with your 3-day go-bag in under 20 minutes.**
- **Complete 25 push-ups.**
- **Complete 5 pull-ups.**
- **Complete 25 air squats.**
- **Document your current baseline.**

If you cannot do these yet, that is your starting point, not a failure. Write your numbers down and aim to improve them.

## What the guidelines say
The US Physical Activity Guidelines recommend that adults get at least:

- **150 minutes a week** of moderate aerobic activity (such as brisk walking), or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, and
- **Muscle-strengthening activity** for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.

## What to train
**Walking and endurance.** The most useful skill in many emergencies is the ability to walk for hours carrying a load.

- Start with 30 minutes of brisk walking most days.
- Add distance and a light pack over weeks.
- Work up to carrying your go-bag for several miles.

**Strength.** Carrying, lifting, pulling and pushing.

- Push-ups, squats, lunges, rows, planks and carries.
- Add weight gradually.
- Learn good form from a qualified coach or a trusted program.

**Mobility and balance.** Stretching and balance work reduce the risk of injury. Yoga, mobility drills and walking on uneven ground help.

**Rest and sleep.** Muscles build and mend during rest. Adults generally need seven or more hours of sleep.

## Build up gradually
1. **Week 1 to 2:** 20 to 30 minutes of walking, and two short strength sessions.
2. **Week 3 to 6:** Add a light pack and distance. Add a third strength session.
3. **Week 7 to 12:** Work toward the baseline test.
4. **Then:** Retest every three months, and keep improving.

## Avoid injury
- Warm up for five minutes before exercise.
- Increase load slowly. A common guide is no more than about 10 percent more per week.
- Use good form, and stop if something hurts sharply.
- Wear good shoes.
- Drink water.

## For people with physical limits, older adults and others
You do not need to meet the baseline to benefit. Your goal is to **do something within your ability each day.**

- Walk, swim, use a stationary bike or do chair exercises.
- Work on balance and leg strength to prevent falls.
- Talk to your doctor or a physical therapist about a safe plan.
- Set goals that fit you and measure your own progress.

Everyone has a part to play in a group. Strength comes in many forms.

## Checklist
- Get your doctor's approval if you need it.
- Record your baseline: walk, push-ups, pull-ups, squats.
- Walk most days and build up to carrying your go-bag.
- Do strength work on two or more days a week.
- Sleep seven hours or more.
- Retest every three months.

> You do not need to be an athlete. You need to be fitter than you were last month.
