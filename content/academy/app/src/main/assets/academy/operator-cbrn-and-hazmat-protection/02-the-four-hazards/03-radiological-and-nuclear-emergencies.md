Radiation events include accidents at nuclear plants, lost or stolen radioactive sources, a "dirty bomb" (explosives plus radioactive material) and, though unlikely, a nuclear detonation. You can greatly reduce risk with simple, well-studied steps.

## The basics
- **Radiation** is energy given off by radioactive material. You cannot see, taste or smell it. Instruments detect it.
- **Contamination** is radioactive material on or in things or people. **Exposure** is being in the path of the energy.
- **Dose** depends on **time, distance and shielding.**
- **Fallout** is radioactive dust and debris that falls after a detonation or major release. It is most dangerous in the first hours and fades fast.
- **Half-life:** radioactivity falls off over time. After a nuclear detonation the "7-10 rule" says: for every sevenfold increase in time, the radiation rate drops by about a factor of ten. So after 7 hours it is about one tenth of the 1-hour level, and after 49 hours about one hundredth.

## Nuclear power plant accidents
Plants have emergency zones, usually ten miles for the plume. Local alerts, sirens and radio will say whether to shelter or evacuate. If you live near a plant, know the plan, and keep the information handy.

## If a nuclear detonation happens: Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned
This is the official guidance (FEMA and Ready.gov).
1. **GET INSIDE** the nearest sturdy building, **right away.** Do not look at the flash. If you see a flash, take cover, and stay away from windows.
2. **Go to the middle** of a large building or a **basement.** Concrete, brick and earth shield well. A larger distance from outside walls and roof helps.
3. **STAY INSIDE** for at least **24 hours** unless officials give other instructions. The danger drops quickly, and early exit often kills more than staying put.
4. **STAY TUNED:** use battery or hand-crank radio and phone alerts for instructions.
5. **If you were outside** when fallout may have come: **remove outer clothing** (this removes most of the dust), **wash** exposed skin and hair, then go inside.
6. If in a car, go into a sturdy building if you can. A car gives little shielding.

## In your shelter
- Keep family together. Bring pets.
- Use stored food and water. Food and water in sealed containers are safe. Wash the outside.
- Stay in the **lowest, most central** area.
- Keep the radio on.
- Set up a toilet bucket.
- Do not go out to look or to get others unless you must.

## Decontamination at home
After an event, officials may tell people to remove outer clothing and wash. Cutting off or removing clothing carefully, without pulling it over the face, takes away 80 to 90 percent of contamination. Bag and set aside. Wash with soap and water. Do not scrub hard, and do not use hair conditioner (it binds material to hair).

## Dirty bomb
The main danger is the explosion. Move away from the area, go inside, close windows, listen to officials and follow decontamination steps.

## Potassium iodide (KI)
KI protects the **thyroid** from one kind of radioactive iodine, if taken at the right time and amount. It does **not** protect from other radiation. Take it **only if public health officials tell you to,** and follow their dose instructions for each age. Many people should not take it without advice.

## Food, water and animals
Officials will say what is safe. Food in sealed containers, canned goods and covered water are protected. Wash fresh produce.

## When can you go out?
Follow the radio. Early leaving, if directed, may be done in stages and by instructed routes.

## Health effects
High doses cause radiation sickness: nausea, vomiting, fatigue, hair loss, and more. Seek medical care after high exposures. Low doses carry small, long-term risk. Fear should not stop you from protecting others and acting wisely.

> This course is protective only. Follow instructions from emergency officials and call 911 for any real incident. Medicines for radiation or chemical exposure are given only on the direction of public health officials or a doctor.
