Protective gear helps only if it is the right type, fits, and is used correctly. Wrong gear gives false confidence.

## Respirators
- **N95 (and similar P100 or FFP2)** filters at least 95 percent of small particles. Good against dust, smoke, many germs and fallout particles. **Not** against gases.
- **P100** filters at least 99.97 percent of particles.
- **Cartridge respirators** with organic vapor, acid gas or multi-gas cartridges can help against some gases, but only for the gas listed and with a good seal.
- **Escape hoods** are for short escape use.
- **Fit:** the mask must seal. Facial hair prevents a seal. A fit test is best.
- **Check:** do a seal check each time: cover the filter and breathe in, and the mask should pull to the face.
- **Limits:** filters load up and cartridges expire. People with lung or heart disease should ask a doctor before using a respirator.
- Choose **NIOSH approved** products. Beware of counterfeits.

## Eye and skin
- **Goggles** that seal against the face.
- **Gloves** of nitrile or chemical-resistant material.
- **Coveralls** or long clothing that can be discarded.
- **Boots or shoe covers.**
- **Tape** wrist and ankle joins when decontaminating or cleaning up, if needed.
These help with dust and splashes, not with serious chemical or radiological hazards. There is no home suit that makes you safe in a toxic cloud.

## Gas masks
Military-style masks need proper filters for the hazard, and training. Check expiry dates. A filter is not good for all hazards.

## Air filtration at home
- **HEPA filter** removes particles, including many germs, smoke and dust. It does not remove gases.
- **Activated carbon** captures some gases and odors.
- **Do-it-yourself box fan filters** (a box fan with a MERV 13 filter attached) can clean room air of smoke and particles. Follow safety guidance and do not leave unattended.
- **Positive pressure** in a safe room with filtered air is a professional design. Do not rely on improvised systems for toxic gases.

## Detection
- **Carbon monoxide alarms** and smoke alarms.
- **Radiation detectors** (Geiger counters, dosimeters) show radiation levels. They require training and are not essential for most families, but a simple dosimeter may be useful. Beware of cheap units that give false readings.
- **Chemical detectors** are specialist devices.
- **Your senses** and official alerts remain the main warning.

## Storage
Keep gear in a labelled bag with a list. Check expiry and condition twice a year. Practice putting it on.

## Honest limits
For serious chemical or radiological events, distance and shelter are better protection than home equipment.

> This course is protective only. Follow instructions from emergency officials and call 911 for any real incident. Medicines for radiation or chemical exposure are given only on the direction of public health officials or a doctor.
