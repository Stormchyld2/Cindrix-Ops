This is where most people start, and it is a good place to start. You want light you can grab in the dark without hunting for it, and a way to keep your phone and radio alive for several days.

## Light: skip the candles
The CDC recommends battery flashlights and lanterns instead of candles, gas lanterns or torches because of the fire risk. Candles are a leading cause of home fires during outages.

If you must use a candle:
- Put it in a sturdy holder on a flat, non-flammable surface.
- Keep it away from curtains, paper, bedding and anything that can burn.
- **Never leave it unattended,** and put it out when you leave the room or go to sleep.
- Never use candles around a person who is using oxygen.

Keep a **fire extinguisher** handy and make sure everyone old enough knows how to use it (see the lesson on safety).

## Build a lighting system
- **A headlamp for each person.** Hands-free light beats a flashlight for cooking, first aid and repairs.
- **A flashlight in every bedroom,** within reach of the bed, for getting up in the dark. A drawer by the bed works.
- **One battery lantern** for the main living space, and one in the kitchen.
- **Glow sticks** for children and for marking stairs and doors.
- **Solar yard lights** can be brought in at night and recharged by day.
- **Spare batteries** in the sizes your gear actually uses, stored together and labeled. Store them away from heat, and avoid mixing old and new.
- Prefer **rechargeable** batteries with a charger that can run from a power bank, car or solar panel, and keep some single-use lithium cells as a reserve.

## Charging and power banks
- **Power banks.** Two or more, charged, one per adult at least. Recharge them on a monthly reminder. Most phones need about 2 to 4 full charges' worth of power bank to last several days. Check the capacity on the label.
- **Car charger.** Keep one in each vehicle. A car can charge devices while running outdoors, but never run it in a garage.
- **Solar charger.** A folding panel can recharge power banks in daylight.
- **Hand-crank radio with USB output.** Gives an emergency trickle.
- **Lower your use.** Turn on low-power mode, dim the screen, close apps and use airplane mode when you do not need signal. Text instead of calling.

## Portable power stations
A portable power station is a big rechargeable battery with outlets. It can run a phone, a lamp, a laptop, a CPAP or a small fridge for a while, and many can be recharged with a folding solar panel.

How to size one:
1. **List what you want to run** and each item's **watts** (on the label).
2. **Estimate hours per day** for each.
3. **Multiply watts by hours** to get **watt-hours** per day.
4. **Add 20 to 30 percent** for losses.
5. **Compare with the battery's watt-hours** (the label states it).

Example: a lamp at 10 watts for 5 hours (50 Wh), a phone charge (15 Wh), a laptop at 50 watts for 3 hours (150 Wh), and a CPAP at 40 watts for 8 hours (320 Wh) total about 535 Wh, or about 650 Wh with losses. A 1,000 Wh unit would cover one night plus extras.

Safe use:
- Use indoors in a dry, ventilated place, away from heat.
- Do not use if it or its cords are wet or damaged.
- Keep the unit charged to the level the maker recommends and recharge after use.
- Test it every three months.

## Medical power
If a medical device depends on power, **ask your device provider or doctor** what backup it supports (internal battery, power station, generator) and how long that will run. Never guess.

## Gear you might need
- Headlamps and battery lanterns (one per person is a good goal)
- Extra batteries in the sizes your gear uses
- Two or more charged power banks
- Car phone charger
- Portable power station and solar panel (the extra mile option)

## Checklist
- A flashlight or headlamp in every bedroom
- At least one battery lantern for the main living area
- Spare batteries stored together and labeled
- Power banks charged and on a monthly recharge reminder
- Car charger kept in the vehicle
- Power station tested every three months

> Light and charge are cheap to prepare and a big comfort when the grid is down.
