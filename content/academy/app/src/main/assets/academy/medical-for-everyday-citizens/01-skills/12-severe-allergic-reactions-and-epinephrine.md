Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction. It can come on within minutes of exposure to a food, an insect sting, a medicine or latex. It can block the airway and drop blood pressure. Epinephrine is the first-line emergency treatment.

## Signs
Anaphylaxis involves more than one body system. Look for:
- **Skin:** hives, itching, flushing, swelling of the lips, face or tongue.
- **Breathing:** wheezing, shortness of breath, cough, a tight throat, hoarse voice, trouble swallowing, **swelling of the throat.**
- **Circulation:** dizziness, fainting, pale or blue skin, weak fast pulse.
- **Stomach:** vomiting, cramps, diarrhea.
- **Mind:** sense of doom, anxiety, confusion.

A person who has had a **known allergen** exposure and shows **any breathing problem, throat swelling or fainting** should be treated as anaphylaxis. Hives alone is milder, but watch closely.

## Common triggers
- Foods: peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, shellfish, fish, wheat, soy, sesame.
- Insect stings: bees, wasps, hornets, fire ants.
- Medicines: antibiotics, pain relievers.
- Latex.
- Exercise (rare).

## What to do
1. **Call 911.** Tell them it is a severe allergic reaction.
2. **Use an epinephrine auto-injector right away** if the person has one, or if one has been prescribed for them. **Do not wait** for symptoms to get worse. Follow the instructions printed on the device and your doctor's plan. **Never use someone else's medicine unless trained and allowed by local rules, and never give a dose from memory.** The device labels and your prescriber give the correct strength for the person's size.
3. **Auto-injector steps in general:** remove the safety cap, firmly press the tip against the **outer middle thigh** (it can go through clothing), hold it in place for the time the device says, and then remove and massage the area. Note the time. Take the used device with the person.
4. **Lay the person down** with legs raised, unless they have trouble breathing (then let them sit up) or are vomiting (then on their side). **Do not let them stand or walk,** because it can drop blood pressure.
5. **Stay with them** and keep them calm and warm.
6. **A second device** may be needed if symptoms do not improve or return. Follow your doctor's action plan and the dispatcher's advice.
7. **Be ready to start CPR** if they stop breathing and have no pulse.
8. **Go to the hospital** even if they feel better. Symptoms can return hours later (a second wave), so they need monitoring.
9. **Hand the used device** to paramedics and tell them what happened and when.

## Antihistamines
Antihistamines may relieve mild symptoms like itching and hives but **do not treat anaphylaxis** and must **not replace epinephrine.** Follow the label.

## If you have known allergies
- **Carry two auto-injectors** at all times, as your doctor advises.
- **Check expiration dates** and the liquid color (it should be clear) and replace on time.
- **Store** at room temperature, away from heat and cold. Never leave them in a hot car or the freezer.
- **Have a written allergy action plan** and share it with family, school and your group.
- **Wear medical ID.**
- **Teach** family and friends how to use the device.
- **Read labels** and ask about ingredients.
- **Practice** with a trainer device.

## In a disaster
- Stock **extra** auto-injectors if your doctor agrees.
- Know what supplies will be affected by heat or cold.
- Keep **antihistamines** and an allergy action plan in the go bag.
- Plan for how to reach care.

## Checklist
- Know the signs of anaphylaxis
- Know where epinephrine is and how to use it (trained)
- Allergy action plan written and shared
- Expiration dates checked
- Know to call 911 and go to the hospital

> This lesson is first aid education, not medical advice or a substitute for hands-on training from the American Red Cross, American Heart Association or a Stop the Bleed class. Call 911 for any life-threatening emergency.
