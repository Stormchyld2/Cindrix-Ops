A wound in the chest can let air into the space around the lung, and a lung can collapse. This is why the chest is checked early.

## Look and listen
- Is the chest rising on **both** sides?
- Is there a hole, a sucking noise, or bubbling blood? This is an **open chest wound**.
- Is the person struggling harder and harder to breathe, with blue lips?

## Treating an open chest wound
1. Wipe the wound if you can, and ask the person to breathe out.
2. Cover the hole with a **chest seal** (a sticky plastic dressing with a valve), pressed on firmly. If you do not have one, use a piece of plastic and tape it down on **three sides**, leaving one side free so air can escape.
3. Let the person sit up if they are more comfortable, or lie on the injured side.
4. Check them often. If they keep getting worse, a build-up of air in the chest may be pressing on the lung. This needs a medical professional. Take the seal off for a moment to let the air out only if you have been taught how.

## Other chest injuries
- Do not remove anything stuck in the chest. Pad round it.
- A rib injury can be very painful. Support the chest with an arm or a cushion.

> A person who finds it harder and harder to breathe is in danger. Call for help and keep them calm.

## Going deeper: serious chest injuries
- **Open (sucking) chest wound:** air enters through the hole. Cover it with a **vented chest seal** (or any occlusive dressing taped on three sides if no vented seal is available, though current teaching favors vented seals on the front and back). Wipe the skin dry first, apply on exhale, and check for a second wound at the exit point, often on the back.
- **Tension pneumothorax:** air trapped under pressure squeezes the lung and heart. Signs: severe, worsening breathlessness, a rapid pulse, blue lips and a swollen neck, with a chest injury. Burp the seal by lifting a corner, which may let air escape. Only trained personnel can decompress the chest with a needle. Get urgent help.
- **Flail chest:** a section of ribs moves opposite to the rest of the chest. Support it gently with padding or the person's hand and arm, and monitor.
- **Rib fractures:** painful, shallow breathing risks pneumonia. Help the person breathe deeply and cough while holding a pillow on the ribs, and seek care.
- **Blunt trauma:** bruising, tenderness, crackling under the skin and breath sounds that differ side to side suggest internal injury.

## Position
A person with chest trauma usually breathes easiest sitting up or lying on the injured side, so a wound is down and the good lung is free. Let them choose, unless they are unconscious.

## Count breathing
Normal adult breathing is about 12 to 20 breaths a minute. Over 30 or under 10 is a danger sign. Watch for use of neck muscles, flaring nostrils and an inability to speak full sentences.

## Reassess
Chest injuries can worsen over minutes. Keep watching rate, color and effort, and keep the person calm.
