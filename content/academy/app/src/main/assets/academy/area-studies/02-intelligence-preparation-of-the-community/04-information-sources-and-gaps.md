During an emergency, the information you act on decides what you do. Plan **how** you will get information and **how** you will fill in what you are missing.

## Where news travels
Find out how people in your area get news, and which sources are reliable:

- **Official alerts.** Wireless Emergency Alerts on phones, NOAA Weather Radio, the Emergency Alert System on radio and TV, and your county's alert system. Sign up for your county or city text and email alerts.
- **Local radio.** In many places the local station gives the best emergency information. Note the frequencies of two or three stations, and keep a battery or hand-crank radio.
- **Television and newspapers.** Slower, but good for background.
- **Official websites and social media** of your county, city, utility, fire department and schools.
- **Neighbors and community groups.** Fast, but can be wrong. Treat as a lead, not a fact.
- **Radio networks.** Amateur radio operators and local nets often relay official information.

## Judge the source
Ask of every piece of information:

1. **Who said it?** An official or a rumor?
2. **How do they know?** First-hand or second-hand?
3. **When was it?** Is it still true?
4. **Does a second source agree?** Look for two independent sources.
5. **What do they want?** Is anyone selling something or stirring up fear?

Label what you hear as **confirmed**, **likely** or **rumor**. Do not pass rumors on as facts.

## Intelligence gaps
An **information gap** is something you need to know and do not. List them, rank them, and plan to close them.

Examples:
- "I do not know where my well water comes from or how deep it is."
- "I do not know if the bridge on Mill Road is rated for heavy trucks."
- "I do not know which neighbors have medical training."
- "I do not know how long the power company says it takes to restore my area."

For each gap, write:

1. The question.
2. Why it matters.
3. Who or what can answer it.
4. Who will find out, and by when.

## Collect information the right way
- Use **public** sources: maps, websites, public meetings, your own observation.
- **Ask** people, politely, about community resources and how they would like to help.
- **Do not** spy on or follow people, photograph private property, or build records about individuals. It is unlawful in many places, hurts relationships, and will not make you safer.
- Share what you learn with your group, and with the neighbors who would benefit.

## Keep a simple log
During an event, keep a log of what you hear and when:

- The time.
- The source.
- What was said.
- How reliable it is.

A log helps you see patterns and decide.

## Checklist
- Sign up for county or city emergency alerts.
- Note the frequencies of two local radio stations.
- Keep a battery or hand-crank radio in the go-bag.
- Write down five information gaps and who will close each.
- Practice labeling information as confirmed, likely or rumor.

> Good decisions need good information. Know where yours comes from and how much to trust it.
