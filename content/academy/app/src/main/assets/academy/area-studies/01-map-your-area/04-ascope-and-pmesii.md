ASCOPE is a framework planners use to study the **civil considerations** of an area: the human side of the place. It is a six-part checklist, and it works well for studying your own neighborhood.

## The six parts
**Areas.** Important places and boundaries. Population centers, neighborhoods, farmland, parks, flood plains, industrial zones, school districts.

**Structures.** Important buildings and facilities. Hospitals, schools, churches and halls, bridges, power substations, water towers and treatment plants, cell towers, warehouses, shopping centers, apartment buildings.

**Capabilities.** What the community can do or provide, and what it needs. Medical services, water supply, sewage, public safety, food supply, fuel, repair and building skills, transport.

**Organizations.** Groups that operate in your area. Local government, fire and police, utilities, churches and charities, clubs, schools, neighborhood associations, scouts, volunteer response teams, local businesses.

**People.** The roles that matter, not private files on individuals. Think of the offices and jobs: the mayor, the fire chief, the emergency manager, the nurse down the street, the plumber, the shop owner. Ask who is a **resource** and who would need **help** (the elderly, people with disabilities, families with infants).

**Events.** Things that happen on a schedule or without warning. Holidays, elections, festivals, harvests, school terms, storm and fire seasons, floods, strikes, big sporting events. Events change traffic, services and crowds.

## A bigger picture: PMESII-PT
The military pairs ASCOPE with eight **operational variables**. They give a wider view of any place:

- **P**olitical: who governs, and how decisions are made.
- **M**ilitary or security: police, fire, emergency services.
- **E**conomic: employers, banks, markets, supply chains.
- **S**ocial: how the community is organized and gets along.
- **I**nformation: how news travels, local media, and the networks that carry communication.
- **I**nfrastructure: roads, power, water, fuel, telecommunications.
- **P**hysical environment: terrain, climate, hazards.
- **T**ime: seasons, schedules, how long things take.

You do not need all eight for a household study. Use them as a prompt so you do not miss a category.

## FEMA's community lifelines
FEMA uses eight **lifelines**, the services a community must have to function. If one fails, everything else struggles. They fit nicely beside ASCOPE:

1. Safety and security: police, fire, rescue.
2. Food, water and shelter.
3. Health and medical.
4. Energy: power and fuel.
5. Communications.
6. Transportation.
7. Hazardous materials.
8. Water systems: drinking water and wastewater.

For each one, ask: what serves my area, where does it come from, and what happens to me if it fails for a day, a week or a month?

## How to fill it in
Use a table with ASCOPE across the top, or one page for each letter:

1. List what you know today.
2. Mark each item on the map.
3. Write what you do not know.
4. Visit, look it up, or call to fill each gap.
5. Date the page.

## Sources
- County and city websites.
- Your local emergency management office.
- Maps (online and paper).
- Local news, community groups, neighborhood meetings.
- Your own eyes: walk or drive your area with a notebook.

## Checklist
- Draw a six-column table for A, S, C, O, P and E.
- Add at least five entries to each column.
- Mark each place on your map.
- Rate each community lifeline for your area: strong, weak or unknown.
- List your information gaps.

> ASCOPE asks one question six ways: what is here, who is here, and what will happen to them in a crisis?
