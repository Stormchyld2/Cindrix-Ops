In most disasters, the people who help you first are your neighbors, not professionals. Responders may take hours or days to reach you. Strong neighborhoods recover faster and more safely. Know your community, and let your community know you.

## Why this matters
- **Skills multiply.** One neighbor is a nurse, another has a chainsaw, another has a truck. Together you can do what none of you can do alone.
- **Eyes and ears.** Neighbors notice the smoke, the leak, the elderly person who has not come out.
- **Shared supplies.** Tools, generators, water and food can be shared.
- **Safety.** A neighborhood that talks to itself is harder to hurt and quicker to respond.

## Start with a friendly conversation
You do not have to tell anyone what you store. Start simply:

1. Introduce yourself to the households next to you and across the street.
2. Exchange phone numbers and emergency contacts.
3. Ask: "If the power was out for a week, what would worry you?"
4. Ask if anyone would need help in an emergency (older people, people with disabilities, families with babies) and how you could help.
5. Ask what skills and tools people are happy to share.

## Respect privacy
- Keep a list only of **what people choose to offer** and **what help they ask for**. Ask first.
- Do not keep notes about people's religion, politics, wealth, background or private lives.
- Do not tell outsiders what your neighbors have.
- Do not share a neighbor's information without permission.

## Neighborhood skills and resources list
Ask neighbors to fill in a short form if they wish:

- Name and address (optional).
- Skills: medical, building, mechanical, electrical, radio, cooking, child care, languages.
- Tools and equipment people are willing to lend.
- Whether they have a generator, a wood stove, a well or a vehicle that can go off-road.
- Anyone who needs extra help in an emergency and has agreed to be on the list.

## Join organized efforts
- **CERT (Community Emergency Response Team).** Free training in basic disaster response from your local emergency management office.
- **Neighborhood watch or association.** Where one exists.
- **Local radio clubs.** The people who keep radio communications going.
- **Churches, charities and service clubs.** They often have shelters, kitchens and volunteers.
- **Red Cross and local volunteer groups.**

## Plan together
- Agree on **one gathering point** for the neighborhood.
- Agree on a **simple signal** that says "we are OK" or "we need help," such as a card in the window.
- Check on the **older and more vulnerable** neighbors first.
- Hold a **neighborhood meeting** once a year to review the plan.

## Checklist
- Meet the neighbors on both sides and across the street.
- Exchange phone numbers and emergency contacts.
- Find out who needs extra help and how you can assist.
- Start a voluntary skills and tools list.
- Look up your local CERT program.
- Agree on a signal.

> Preparedness spreads from house to house. A prepared neighbor is a gift to you and to the whole street.
