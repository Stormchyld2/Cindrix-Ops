Water is the first system to fail in most disasters and the one people die without fastest. The five principles of emergency management give you a way to build it in layers.

## 1. Prevention and hazard assessment
Find out what could go wrong with your water **before** it does.
- Where does your tap water come from: a city system, a private well, a spring or a cistern?
- What could stop it: a power cut that stops the pumps, a main break, a flood, a drought, a chemical spill, a boil-water notice, a frozen pipe, an earthquake?
- What could spoil it: sewage, farm runoff, fuel, industrial waste, dead animals, floodwater.
Write the top three threats for your place at the top of a page.

## 2. Preparedness
- Store water (see the storage lesson). Aim first for **a gallon per person per day for two weeks**, then raise it.
- Own the tools to treat water: a filter, chlorine or tablets, a pot, a stove and fuel.
- Know the **second source** you would use, and have what it needs: containers, a hose, a pump, a way to carry.
- Practice once a year.

## 3. Response
When the water stops or is not safe:
1. Stop and think. Do not drink or cook with tap water if there is a boil or "do not use" notice.
2. Use stored water first.
3. Switch on the second source and treat it.
4. Ration sensibly: drinking first, then cooking, then hygiene.

## 4. Mitigation
Make the system less likely to fail or less harmful if it does:
- Add a **second supply** (rain catchment, a hand pump, a spring).
- Keep several smaller containers rather than one big one.
- Protect pipes from freezing, and know your shutoff valve.
- Raise stored water above flood level.

## 5. Recovery
After the emergency: test or disinfect wells and tanks, flush the pipes, replace water you used, and write down what worked.

## How this course is laid out
1. **Assess and plan:** judge sources, work out how much you need.
2. **Collect and treat:** rainwater, filters, disinfection.
3. **Store and use:** containers and long-term plans.
4. **Sanitation:** toilets, waste and handwashing.
5. **Recover:** wells, floods, greywater and the long-term plan.

> Water that looks clear can still be unsafe, and water that tastes bad can be safe. Treat first, judge later.

> Local rules on rainwater, wells, greywater and waste differ. Check with your health department before you build anything permanent.
