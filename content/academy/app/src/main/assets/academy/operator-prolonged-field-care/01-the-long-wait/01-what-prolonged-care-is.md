In a city, a seriously hurt person is usually in an emergency room within the hour. In a large disaster, a remote area or an unrest situation, help can be delayed for **hours or days.** Prolonged field care is the work of keeping a person alive and stable through that delay.

## Why it is different
- **Time:** the casualty needs reassessment, warmth, fluid, food, comfort and care across many hours.
- **Resources:** you have only what you carry, and what you can improvise.
- **People:** the carer tires, so teams and shifts matter.
- **Decisions:** you may have to decide whether to wait, move, or ask for help with little information.

## The five principles in this field
1. **Prevention and hazard assessment:** avoid injury, and know which hazards cause the injuries you may see (falls, tools, vehicles, fire, cold).
2. **Preparedness:** skills, kits, plans, communications, evacuation routes.
3. **Response:** stop the killers fast, then stabilize.
4. **Mitigation:** prevent the secondary harms: cold, shock, infection, pressure sores, pain, panic.
5. **Recovery:** hand over, document and care for the carers.

## The killers, in order
The **MARCH** sequence (massive bleeding, airway, respiration, circulation, head injury and hypothermia) still comes first. After that, prolonged care adds what keeps a stable person stable: **PAWS** from the military's PCC model, adapted here as:
- **P**ain relief and comfort
- **A**ntibiotics and infection control (by trained clinicians; you control wound care and cleanliness)
- **W**ound care and dressings
- **S**plinting and position

## The stages
1. **Care under threat:** the scene is unsafe. Stop massive bleeding, get to cover.
2. **Tactical field care:** the scene is safer. Do the full assessment and treatment.
3. **Prolonged care:** you wait or evacuate with ongoing monitoring.
4. **Evacuation and handover.**

## What this course gives you
- A routine to **reassess** a casualty every 15 minutes.
- How to **keep a person warm, dry and fed.**
- Safe handling of **wounds, burns and fractures.**
- How to **write a record** a clinician can trust.
- How to **plan evacuation** and when to go.
- How to **look after yourself and your team.**

## Know your limits
You are not a surgeon. Your aim is to **do no harm, buy time and get the person to higher care.**

> Most people who die waiting die from bleeding, cold, a blocked airway or breathing trouble. Cover those four, and you have done most of the work.

> This course teaches the thinking and the hands-on steps a layperson can do. Medicines, needles, fluids and surgery belong to trained clinicians. Take an in-person Wilderness First Responder, Wilderness EMT or Tactical Casualty Care course. Always call for help if you can.
