Good care is lost if nobody knows what was done. A clear record helps clinicians treat the right problem and avoid repeating dangerous steps.

## What to record
- **Patient:** name, age, sex, weight if known, allergies, medicines, medical history, last food and drink.
- **Event:** what happened, when and where.
- **Findings:** injuries and signs.
- **Vital signs:** time, pulse, breathing rate, skin, alertness, temperature and oxygen readings.
- **Treatments:** what was done and when: tourniquet time, dressings, splints, medicines (as directed by a clinician), fluids.
- **Plan:** what you intend to do next.

## The SOAP format
- **S – Subjective:** what the patient says: complaints, pain, history.
- **O – Objective:** what you see and measure: vitals, exam findings.
- **A – Assessment:** your best understanding of the problem.
- **P – Plan:** the next steps and triggers to escalate.
Use it for each entry. It forces you to think.

## The casualty card
Military teams use a casualty card on which they mark injuries on a body diagram, record vital signs, and the time of tourniquets and treatments. Make your own: a body outline, spaces for times, a vital signs table, and boxes for treatments. Tie it to the person.

## Write down the time of a tourniquet
Write "TQ" and the time on the tourniquet itself (use tape and a marker), on the casualty card, and in your notes. It is the single most critical entry.

## Be factual
- Use clear words and numbers, not guesses.
- Use the 24-hour clock.
- Do not interpret more than you saw. "Pulse 120, weak" is better than "he's in shock."
- Correct mistakes by crossing out, not erasing.

## Handover: a short, standard report
When help arrives, report in under a minute using **MIST:**
- **M**echanism: what happened.
- **I**njuries: found or suspected.
- **S**igns: vital signs and trends.
- **T**reatment: what was done, with times.
Add allergies, medicines, and anything that changed. Hand over the card and notes.

## Keep a copy
Photograph the record. Keep the original with the patient, and your own copy for review.

## Privacy and consent
Keep the record confidential. Share it with those who need it for care.

> This course teaches the thinking and the hands-on steps a layperson can do. Medicines, needles, fluids and surgery belong to trained clinicians. Take an in-person Wilderness First Responder, Wilderness EMT or Tactical Casualty Care course. Always call for help if you can.
