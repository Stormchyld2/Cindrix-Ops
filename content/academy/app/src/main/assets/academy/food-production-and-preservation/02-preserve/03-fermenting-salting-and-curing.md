Salt, acid and time have preserved food for thousands of years.

## Lacto-fermentation (vegetables)
Salt draws water out of vegetables. Friendly bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid. The acid keeps harmful bacteria away.
1. Use fresh, clean vegetables (cabbage, cucumbers, carrots, beets, green beans).
2. Weigh the food, and use **about 2 to 3 percent salt by weight** (for example, 20 to 30 grams per kilogram). Use plain salt with no anti-caking agents.
3. Pack tightly into a clean jar or crock, so liquid covers everything. Weight the vegetables under the brine.
4. Leave a little headspace, cover loosely so gas can escape and keep at room temperature (about 65 to 75 °F) for days to weeks.
5. Taste and check: it should smell pleasantly sour. Move to a cool place or a fridge when done.
- A white film (kahm yeast) is usually harmless. **Fuzzy, colored mold, a slimy texture or a bad smell** means discard.
- Fermented food is not shelf-stable at room temperature forever, but lasts months in a cool cellar or fridge.

## Pickling with vinegar
Use tested recipes with vinegar of at least 5 percent acidity. Process in a boiling-water canner for shelf stability. Do not dilute the vinegar beyond what the recipe says.

## Salting and curing meat
- **Dry salting** and **brining** reduce water and microbes. Curing salt (with nitrite) is needed for many sausages and hams to prevent botulism. Follow tested recipes exactly and use accurate scales.
- **Smoking** adds flavor and some preservation, but cold smoking needs proper curing first.
- Fish and meat preserved by salt need careful, tested methods.
- Do not improvise ratios for curing salts, since too much is toxic and too little is unsafe.

## Other ferments
Sourdough, yogurt, kefir, cheese, wine and vinegar can add food, nutrition and variety. Learn them in good times from tested sources.

## Safety
- Keep everything clean.
- Do not ferment in reactive metal. Use glass, food-grade plastic or ceramic.
- Do not eat anything with moldy fuzz.
- Keep records of salt amounts and dates.

## Why it matters
Fermented foods keep without power, add vitamins and give variety to a stored diet.

> Always use tested recipes and current guidance for preserving food. Local laws govern livestock, hunting, fishing and selling food.
