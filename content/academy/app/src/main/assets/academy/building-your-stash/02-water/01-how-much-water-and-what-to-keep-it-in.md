> Basic planning guidance. Prices change, so this course names no prices. Check anything about chemicals or food storage against the sources named in the lesson before you rely on it.

## How much
- At least **one gallon (about four litres) per person per day**: half to drink, half for cooking and washing.
- Store more for hot weather, children, nursing mothers and anyone ill.
- Do not forget pets.

## What to keep it in
- **Store-bought sealed bottles** or jugs carry a date. They are the easiest.
- **Clean, food-grade containers** made for drinking water. Look for a food-safe mark.
- Do not use old milk or juice jugs. Residue feeds germs and they crack.
- Never use containers that held anything but food or drink.
- Large drums made for water are fine if food grade.

## Rules
- Keep containers closed, in a cool dark place, away from fuel and chemicals.
- Write the date filled on each container. Swap tap water for fresh every so often.
- A hardware-store bucket is **not** automatically safe for food or water. See module 3.

## Extra sources in your house
- The water in your **water heater tank** and in pipes after supply stops
- Ice in the freezer
- The tub, filled early before a storm, for washing and flushing (not drinking, unless treated)

> Count water first. If you only get one thing right, get this right.

## Going deeper: more than drinking
The rule of one gallon per person per day covers drinking and a little basic cleaning. Hot weather, illness, pregnancy and nursing all raise the need. Children, nursing mothers and sick people need more, and pets need roughly an ounce of water per pound of body weight each day, a little less for cats. If you can store more, use the extra for washing hands, cooking and cleaning dishes.

## Container choices
- **Commercially bottled water** in sealed bottles is the simplest. Keep it in its original container, store it cool and replace it by the date printed on it.
- **Food-grade water containers** (marked for water, BPA-free, with a screw cap) in sizes you can lift. A full 5-gallon jug weighs over 40 pounds, so choose sizes people can carry.
- **Cleaned two-liter soda bottles** are a cheap choice. Do not use milk or juice jugs, because sugars and proteins are hard to wash out and grow bacteria. Avoid containers that held anything but food or drink.
- **Large barrels and tanks** hold a lot but need a pump or siphon and care in filling. Use only containers made for drinking water.

## Cleaning and filling a container
1. Wash with dish soap and rinse well.
2. Sanitize with a solution of one teaspoon of plain, unscented household bleach in a quart of water. Swish it so it touches every surface, wait a couple of minutes, then rinse.
3. Fill with safe tap water. Treated municipal water is generally already safe and needs nothing added. If your supply is untreated, such as a private well, follow your health department's advice.
4. Seal tight, label with the date and store in a cool, dark place away from fuel and chemicals.

## Extra water already in your home
Your water heater tank holds many gallons, and you can drain it from the bottom valve once the power and gas are off and the supply is shut. Melted ice from the freezer and the water in pipes are further sources. Toilet **tanks** (not bowls) are only for flushing or cleaning unless the water is clean and free of cleaning tablets. Do not drink from a pool, a waterbed or a hot tub.
