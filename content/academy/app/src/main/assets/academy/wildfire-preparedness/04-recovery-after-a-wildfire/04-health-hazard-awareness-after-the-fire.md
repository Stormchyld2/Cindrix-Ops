Wildfire effects on health do not end when the flames do. Smoke, ash, contaminated water, stress and the risk of flooding on burned slopes all matter.

## Smoke
Smoke irritates the eyes, nose, throat and lungs. It can make breathing hard and cause coughing and wheezing. Smoke can linger for days after a fire ends.

**Higher risk:** children, adults 65 and older, pregnant people, and people with asthma, COPD, heart disease, diabetes or chronic kidney disease. Exposure adds up over time.

**Check the air:** use AirNow.gov or the AirNow app, and the Fire and Smoke Map, for current and forecast air quality.

**Reduce exposure**
- **Stay indoors** with windows and doors closed when air quality is poor.
- Run the air conditioner on **recirculate** with a **MERV 13 or higher** filter, and check it often during smoke events.
- Use a **portable HEPA air cleaner,** or build a **Corsi-Rosenthal box** (a box fan with filters).
- Make a **clean room:** one room with a HEPA cleaner and closed doors and windows, for rest.
- **Avoid indoor pollution:** smoking, vaping, frying or broiling, gas or propane use, wood stoves and vacuuming without a HEPA filter.
- Go to an air-conditioned public space such as a library if you cannot clean your air.
- **Limit outdoor activity,** especially exercise, in unhealthy air.
- **In a car,** close the windows and set the air to recirculate.
- **Wear a well-fitted N95 or KN95** if you must be outside. Dust, cloth and surgical masks do not protect against smoke particles.
- If you have asthma or COPD, **keep your quick-relief inhaler** with you and follow your action plan.
- **Check on neighbors,** especially older adults and people who live alone.

**Seek medical care** for difficulty breathing, chest pain, a persistent cough, dizziness or confusion.

## Ash and debris
- Wear **gloves, long sleeves, long pants, closed shoes and socks, goggles** and a **fit-tested NIOSH-approved N95.** It must fit tightly and be worn correctly.
- **Wash ash off** your skin, eyes and mouth as soon as you can.
- **Do not stir up ash.** Wet it lightly before sweeping, and use a HEPA vacuum rather than a regular one or a broom.
- **Avoid blowing ash** with a leaf blower or compressed air.
- Keep **children and pets** away from ash.
- Dispose of ash and debris as local rules require. Burned homes can release asbestos, lead and other toxic materials. Ask your county health or environmental agency before handling or disposing of debris.

## Water
- **Private wells can be contaminated** after a wildfire and may be unsafe for drinking and other uses. Test the well and treat the water before use. Contact your health department.
- **City water:** follow boil-water or do-not-use advice. Burned pipes can release chemicals such as benzene into the system after a fire. Wait for your utility to say the water is safe.
- Use **bottled or treated water** until cleared.

## Food
- **Throw away** food that was exposed to heat, smoke, soot or ash, and food from a refrigerator or freezer that lost power for too long. See the Power Outage course.
- **Canned food** exposed to heat may be unsafe if the can is bulged, leaking or damaged.
- **Wash dishes and utensils** in safe water.

## Cleaning indoors
- **Soot and smoke residue** are hard to remove. Wear gloves and a respirator.
- **Clean surfaces** with a damp cloth, then a detergent solution. Avoid stirring up dust.
- **Replace HVAC filters** after a smoke event, and consider a professional duct cleaning.
- **Smell** can linger. Ventilate when outside air is clean.
- **Mold** can follow water used to fight fires or leaks. Dry quickly and clean small areas with detergent.

## Burned slopes: flash floods and debris flows
Burned hillsides carry a real risk of **flash flooding and debris flows** for years afterward, since the vegetation that normally absorbs rainfall is gone. Even a modest storm can send mud, rocks and debris downhill.
- **Check** the forecast, and **know your flood and debris-flow risk.**
- **Sign up** for flash flood warnings.
- **Do not camp or park** in drainages or below burned slopes during rain.
- **Leave early** if a flash flood warning is issued. Never drive through floodwater.
- Consider **sandbags, barriers** and diversions. Ask your county.

## Emotional well-being
Many people feel strong emotions after a wildfire: shock, grief, anger, numbness, anxiety, trouble sleeping. These are common reactions.
- **Stay connected** with family, friends and your community.
- **Keep routines,** sleep, food and movement.
- **Talk about it.** Share your feelings with someone you trust.
- **Limit media and social media.**
- **Take breaks** from recovery tasks.
- **Ask for help.** The **SAMHSA Disaster Distress Helpline** is available by call or text at **1-800-985-5990** (press 2 for Spanish). The **988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline** is available around the clock.
- **Watch children,** who may show fear, clinginess or changes in behavior. Give them extra time and reassurance.
- **Look after responders and helpers.**

## Gear you might need
- N95 or P100 respirator
- HEPA air purifier for indoor recovery
- Gloves, goggles and protective clothing

## Checklist
- Monitors the local air quality index before resuming outdoor activity
- Uses a respirator when handling ash or debris
- Watches for delayed hazards such as flash flooding and debris flows on burned slopes
- Knows the well and tap water guidance
- Knows where to find emotional support

> A respirator filters out smoke and ash before you can breathe it in. Wear one.
