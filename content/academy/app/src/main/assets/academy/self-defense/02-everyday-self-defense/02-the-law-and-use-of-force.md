**Everyone needs to know their local laws about self-defense and the use of force.** This lesson explains the general ideas. It is **not legal advice.** Laws differ by state and country, and they change. Read your own state's statutes, take a lawful-use-of-force class and, if you carry a weapon, learn the rules for carrying where you live and travel.

## The core ideas
Most places in the United States share these ideas about when force is justified in self-defense. Check how your state words them.

1. **Imminent threat.** You may use force only to stop a threat that is happening **right now** or about to. Not something that happened earlier, and not something that may happen tomorrow.
2. **Reasonable belief.** Your fear must be one a reasonable person would have in the same situation. A person who shouts but cannot reach you is different from one coming at you with a weapon.
3. **Proportional force.** The force you use must match the threat. You may use **deadly force** only to stop a threat of **death or serious bodily harm** (or certain violent crimes, depending on the state). Using deadly force against a minor push or an argument is not justified.
4. **Stop when the threat stops.** Once the person has stopped, run away or been disabled, the right to use force ends. Continuing is an attack.
5. **You must not be the aggressor.** If you start or provoke the fight, your right to claim self-defense is usually lost or limited.

## Other people and property
- **Defense of others.** Many states allow you to use force to protect another person under the same rules as defending yourself.
- **Defense of property.** The law allows far less force to protect property than to protect people. Deadly force to protect property alone is generally **not** allowed.

## Duty to retreat, stand your ground and the castle doctrine
States differ on whether you must try to escape before using deadly force:

- **Duty to retreat.** You must retreat safely, if you can do so, before using deadly force (outside your home).
- **Stand your ground.** You have no duty to retreat if you are somewhere you may lawfully be.
- **Castle doctrine.** In your own home, many states presume that you do not have to retreat and that you feared for your life if someone unlawfully and forcibly enters.

Even where there is no duty to retreat, **leaving safely is still the smartest and safest choice.**

## Less-lethal tools and the law
Pepper spray, stun devices, batons, alarms and other tools are regulated differently in each state and city. Some are banned or limited. Use of any tool is still judged by the same rules: imminent threat, reasonable belief and proportional force.

## Carrying a firearm
Rules about carrying firearms, in the open or concealed, in vehicles, in certain places (schools, courts, government buildings, bars, airports), and across state lines, vary widely. Know them. If you carry, get proper training and any required permits, and learn how permits are recognized when you travel.

## Know the consequences
Even a justified use of force can lead to an investigation, an arrest, a trial, lawsuits and heavy costs. Everything you do and say matters. This is another reason avoidance and retreat come first.

## What to do
1. Learn your state's self-defense and weapons laws. Read the actual statutes or a trusted state summary.
2. Take a lawful use-of-force class, taught by a qualified instructor.
3. Consider self-defense legal protection or insurance, and read the terms carefully.
4. Review the rules each year, because laws change.
5. Teach every adult in the household.

## Checklist
- Look up your state's self-defense statute and write a one-page summary.
- Look up the rules for carrying weapons in your state and the states you travel to.
- Take a lawful use-of-force class.
- Review the rules every year.
- Discuss with every adult in the household what you would do and what you would not.

> If you can safely leave, leave. The law, and your future, are on the side of the person who avoided the fight.
