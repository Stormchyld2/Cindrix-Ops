There are two broad categories of defensive equipment: **less-lethal** and **lethal.** The right tool depends on you, your training and the law. The best equipment is the equipment you have practiced with, understand and are allowed to carry.

> Always check and respect your local laws on self-defense equipment. Laws differ by state, city and place, and they change.

## Less-lethal tools
These are not called "non-lethal," because there is some chance they could cause serious injury or death depending on the situation. They are meant to give you a **chance to escape.**

**Personal alarm.** A small device that makes a very loud sound. It draws attention and may scare off an attacker. Test the battery regularly. It is legal almost everywhere and needs little training.

**Pepper spray (OC spray).** A canister that sprays oleoresin capsicum, which causes burning of the eyes, skin and airways, and makes it hard to see. The effect lasts for a short time, often 30 to 45 minutes, and then wears off with no permanent damage in most cases.

How to use it:
1. **Learn the safety.** Many canisters have a lock or flip cap. Practice undoing it **before** you need it.
2. **Carry it ready,** in your hand or an easy pocket, not at the bottom of a bag.
3. **Aim at the face,** from several feet away. Spray in a short burst, sweeping side to side.
4. **Mind the wind.** Spray into the wind and you will hit yourself.
5. **Do not rely on it to stop someone.** It may not work on every person, and in some cases the person keeps coming.
6. **Move away at once** and call for help.
7. **Check the expiry date.** Sprays lose pressure and potency over time. Replace them every two to four years, or as the maker advises.
8. **Practice** with an inert training canister.

If you are sprayed, or someone else is: move to fresh air, do not rub the eyes, flush with plenty of cool water, remove contact lenses and get medical help if trouble breathing continues.

**Stun devices (electronic control devices).** Devices that deliver an electric shock. They have limited range, need good contact or well-placed probes, can fail, and are restricted in some places. Training matters.

**Impact and control tools (batons, kubotan).** Legal in some places and not others. They need training to be effective and to avoid injury.

**Pepper-ball and similar launchers.** Launch projectiles that release irritant. They are heavier than a spray and regulated in some places.

## Lethal tools
**Firearms** (pistols, rifles and shotguns) and **knives** can cause death or serious injury. They carry the heaviest legal and moral responsibility.

- A firearm demands strict safety handling, safe storage, training in use and in the law of use of force.
- A knife is a close-range tool that demands skill and carries legal limits.
- Both can be taken from you and used against you if you are not trained.

See the firearm safety lesson for the rules.

## A critical element: training
Owning equipment is not the same as being able to use it. Many people pull out their pepper spray for the first time in an emergency and find they cannot undo the safety. You must know:

- **What it can and cannot do.**
- **Its range** and limits.
- **How to use it safely,** and how to stop using it.
- **The law** for owning, carrying and using it.

## Layered capability
Tools can fail, be forgotten or be taken. Skills take time to build. The strongest approach is **layered capability:** awareness first, then skills, then tools.

## Choosing equipment
Ask yourself:

1. Is it legal where I live, work and travel?
2. Am I trained to use it?
3. Can I carry it comfortably every day?
4. Is it reliable and simple?
5. Can I maintain it?
6. Does it suit everyone who might need to use it?

## Checklist
- Look up the laws on each tool you consider.
- Buy a practice (inert) canister of pepper spray and practice.
- Check expiry dates on all sprays.
- Take a training class before carrying any tool.
- Write down the rules for traveling with each tool.

> A tool you have not trained with is a risk to you.
