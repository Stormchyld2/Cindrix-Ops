Create time and space, have escape routes, and make yourself a **hard target**: someone who looks aware, confident and not worth the trouble.

## Make yourself a hard target
People who commit crimes look for **easy** victims: distracted, alone, unaware, carrying visible valuables. You do not need to be tough. You need to be **not easy.**

- Head up, relaxed shoulders, steady walk.
- Eye contact, a nod or a "hello" shows you have noticed someone.
- Keep valuables out of sight.
- Stay in lit, busy places.
- Walk with other people when you can.
- Trust your gut and leave early.

## Create distance and know your exits
- **Distance is time.** The farther away a threat is, the longer you have to react. Keep at least an arm's length, and more if you can.
- **Know two exits** in every room or place you spend time.
- **Stay near the door** when you can, and do not let anyone block it.
- **Walk away early.** There is no prize for staying.

## De-escalation: calming a conflict
Most conflicts begin with words and can be ended with words. When a person is angry, upset or afraid:

1. **Watch your body language.** Do not stand in a confrontational way, and do not cower. Keep a neutral, stable stance, a bit sideways, with your **hands up and visible** at chest height, open and relaxed. This looks calm and also protects you.
2. **Keep distance.** Give them enough room that they cannot surprise you with a punch.
3. **Speak slowly and softly.** Do not raise your voice. A quiet, steady voice often lowers theirs.
4. **Let them vent.** Do not interrupt. Listen.
5. **Show you understand.** "I can see you are upset. That sounds frustrating."
6. **Do not argue, insult or threaten.** Do not tell them to "calm down." That often makes things worse.
7. **Offer a way out.** Let them save face: "Let's sort this out." "I do not want trouble. I am going to leave."
8. **Be ready to leave.** If the person's voice rises, fists clench or they move toward you, end the talk and go.

## Verbal boundary statements
Practice clear, firm statements with another person:

- "Stop. Stay back."
- "I do not want any trouble."
- "I am leaving now."
- "Back off."

Use a loud, clear voice. This does two things: it tells the other person you are not an easy target, and it alerts people nearby to what is going on.

## Controlled disengagement
**Controlled disengagement** means getting out of a conflict calmly and safely:

1. Keep facing the person, or glance behind you so you do not trip.
2. Step back, hands up.
3. Keep talking calmly: "I am going now."
4. Move toward people, light and an exit.
5. Do not turn your back until you are clear.
6. Get inside a safe place and call for help if needed.

## Decide in advance
Decide **now** what level of force you would use in response to an attack, and under what law. It is always best to avoid a physical confrontation. But be ready to respond **appropriately** if you cannot avoid it. The next lesson explains the law.

## Checklist
- Practice a firm verbal boundary statement with a partner.
- Practice controlled disengagement with a partner, walking backward with hands up.
- Learn two exits in every place you spend time.
- Identify your household safe room or fallback space.
- Write down emergency contacts.
- Review home lighting and lock weaknesses.

> The goal is to go home safe. Walking away is not losing.
