There may be a time when 911 does not respond fast enough, or does not respond at all. Seconds matter in an attack, and police response times are measured in minutes. In a major disaster or a breakdown of order, responders may not be able to reach you.

**Everyone needs to know their local laws regarding self-defense and the use of force.** See the law lesson first.

## Thinking about layers of protection
When avoidance and words fail, you have layers of response. Think of them in order from least to most force:

1. **Leave and get to safety.** Always the first choice.
2. **Alarms and noise.** A personal alarm or a loud shout alerts others and may scare an attacker off.
3. **Barriers.** A locked door, a vehicle, a table between you and the person.
4. **Less-lethal tools.** Pepper spray and similar devices that may let you escape. (See the equipment lesson.)
5. **Empty-hand skills.** Basic strikes and escapes from grabs, to create a chance to get away.
6. **Lethal force.** Only to stop an imminent threat of death or serious harm, within the law.

At every level the goal is the same: **stop the threat and get away.** You are not trying to win. You are trying to go home.

## When help may not come
In a serious emergency, such as a long power outage, flood, earthquake or civil disorder, you may be on your own for days. A sensible household thinks through how it would protect itself in a **breakdown of normal order** (sometimes called "without rule of law," or WROL) or a **failure of civility.**

Protecting a household in that situation is mostly **not** about weapons. It is about:

- **Being unattractive and unseen.** Do not advertise your supplies, light, noise or generator.
- **Staying with a group.** Neighbors who look after each other are safer.
- **Securing your home** with good locks, lights and a plan.
- **Keeping communication.** A radio, a whistle and a signal for help.
- **Being ready to leave** if the situation worsens.
- **Staying legal.** The law still applies, and a justified response still has to meet the legal tests.

## Choosing tools for home defense
Some households choose to keep a firearm for home defense. If you do, the choice is personal and legal. Think about:

- **Your training.** A tool you have not practiced with is a risk to you. Choose something you can handle, shoot accurately and maintain.
- **Your household.** Children, visitors and anyone with mental health concerns affect how you store and handle it.
- **Your home.** Hallway length, walls and neighbors matter. Bullets go through walls.
- **The law.** Know the rules for owning, storing and carrying.
- **Reliability and simplicity.** Common, well-supported designs are easier to learn, maintain and repair.
- **Storage.** See the firearm safety lesson. A weapon that a child or a thief can reach is worse than none.

Many different tools are used for home defense: handguns, shotguns and rifles. Which is best depends on the person, the home and the law. A qualified instructor can help you choose.

## What to do in the moment
1. **Move your family to the safe room** and call for help.
2. **Make noise.** Shouting "I'm calling the police!" and "Get out!" may be enough.
3. **Identify your target and what is behind it** before any use of force.
4. **Stop when the threat stops.**
5. **Call emergency services** and follow their instructions.

## Checklist
- Write down your household's layers of response from least to most force.
- Discuss what you will do if help does not come.
- Take a basic self-defense class.
- If you keep a firearm, take a certified safety and use-of-force class.
- Practice moving the family to the safe room.

> The aim is not to win a fight. It is to protect life and get to safety.
