When a threat appears, your body reacts instantly. Understanding the reaction lets you manage it instead of being run by it.

## What happens in the body
The "fight, flight or freeze" response floods your body with adrenaline. You may notice:

- A fast heartbeat and fast breathing.
- Tunnel vision: your view narrows to the threat.
- Hearing that fades or seems far away (auditory exclusion).
- Shaking hands and loss of fine motor skills, such as dialing a phone, working a zipper or aiming.
- Time seeming slower, or faster.
- Freezing, confusion and a blank mind.

This is normal. It is your body trying to protect you. The problem comes when it overwhelms your ability to think and act.

## Why training matters
Under high stress, **complicated skills break down first**, and **simple, practiced skills survive.** That is why training builds simple actions you can do without thinking: step away, shout "stop," run, call out, block. It is also why you should choose simple tools you have practiced with.

## Tools to keep calm
**1. Tactical breathing (box breathing).** A calm breath calms the body. Practice it when you are not stressed so it comes naturally when you are.

1. Breathe in through the nose for a slow count of four.
2. Hold for four.
3. Breathe out through the mouth for four.
4. Hold for four.
5. Repeat four times.

**2. Mental rehearsal.** Picture a threat clearly and walk through your response in your mind. See yourself noticing it, staying calm, speaking, moving and getting away. Athletes and emergency services use this because the brain treats vivid practice almost like the real thing.

**3. Self-talk.** Use short calm instructions to yourself: "Breathe. Look. Move." Avoid "I can't."

**4. Simple plans.** The fewer steps in the plan, the more likely it works. "If X happens, I do Y."

**5. Stress inoculation.** Practice in realistic conditions, a little at a time: fatigue, noise, time limits, a partner playing a role. Each time you handle a bit of stress, you grow more comfortable with it.

## After the stress
Once the danger has passed, your body may shake, feel weak or feel emotional. That is the adrenaline wearing off. Sit down, drink water, breathe slowly and wait. Look after others. Later, talk it over with a friend or counselor. If strong feelings, trouble sleeping or flashbacks last more than a few weeks, ask a doctor or counselor for help. That is a normal part of recovering, not a weakness.

## Fitness helps
A fit body handles stress better. Regular exercise and sleep improve your ability to stay calm and think clearly.

## Checklist
- Practice box breathing for two minutes a day.
- Mentally rehearse two scenarios a week: a car park and a home intruder.
- Choose three calm phrases to say to yourself.
- Run a practice drill with a family member and talk it over afterward.

> Under stress, simple practiced skills survive. Practice the simple things.
