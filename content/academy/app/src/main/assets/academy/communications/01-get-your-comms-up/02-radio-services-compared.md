Not all radios are the same, and the rules are different for each one. Picking the right service saves you money, keeps you legal, and decides how far you can talk.

## The five services you will hear about
**FRS (Family Radio Service).** The blister-pack walkie-talkies in the store. You do not need a license. Power is low (about 2 watts on most channels, half a watt on a few), and the antenna is fixed to the radio and cannot be changed. Realistic range is a mile or two in open ground and less in buildings or forest. FRS has no repeaters.

**GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service).** The best first step for a household. You need an FCC license, but there is no exam. The radios can use more power and a better antenna, and you can use **repeaters**, which are tall stations that take your signal and send it much farther. One license covers your whole immediate family.

**MURS (Multi-Use Radio Service).** Five channels in the VHF band. You do not need a license. Power is up to 2 watts, and you can use an outside antenna. There are no repeaters. It is a quiet, simple choice for farms, camps and short links between buildings.

**CB (Citizens Band).** A very old service with 40 channels. No license is needed. Power is low, and the radios are large, so it is mostly used in vehicles. CB channel 9 is the traditional emergency channel. It is useful in a convoy or for listening to road traffic.

**Amateur (ham) radio.** The most capable service, and it needs a license that you earn by passing an exam. Hams can use more power, many more frequencies, and long-distance bands that can reach across the country or the world. There are three license levels: Technician (local and regional), General (adds the long-distance bands) and Amateur Extra (everything).

## How far will each one reach?
These are planning figures, not promises. Height and a clear line of sight matter far more than the power rating:

- FRS handheld: about 1 to 3 miles in the open, much less in dense buildings or forest.
- GMRS handheld: about 1 to 4 miles in the open, less in forest.
- GMRS between two vehicles with roof antennas: 3 to 10 miles.
- GMRS through a hilltop repeater: often 10 to 40 miles or more.
- MURS handheld: about 1 to 3 miles in the open.

A hill, a ridge or a hillside home can block a signal completely, even at a short distance. Power does not beat terrain.

## Comparing them side by side
- **License needed?** FRS: no. MURS: no. CB: no. GMRS: yes, no exam. Ham: yes, with an exam.
- **Repeaters?** GMRS: yes. Ham: yes. FRS, MURS and CB: no.
- **Change the antenna?** GMRS, MURS, ham: yes. FRS: no.
- **Best for:** FRS: kids, quick short talk. GMRS: family and group communication. MURS: property and camp. CB: vehicles. Ham: wide-area and long-distance emergency communication.

## What to choose
- **A household that wants to be ready fast:** buy GMRS radios and get the license. This is the plan this course follows.
- **A group that wants long range:** have one or two people earn a ham license. The group's long-range link is then in trained hands.
- **People who just want to listen:** you do not need any license to listen. A scanner or a radio that receives the emergency bands lets you hear what the authorities and your neighbors are saying.

## Two rules that apply to every service
1. **Certification matters.** The FCC approves specific radios for specific services. A radio approved for one service is not automatically legal for another. Check the label or the box for the FCC rule part (for GMRS, Part 95E).
2. **Never block an emergency.** Messages about the safety of life take priority on any of these channels. If you hear someone in trouble, stop talking and help.

## Checklist
- Decide which service your household will use first.
- Check that any radio you buy is certified for that service.
- Choose one person in your group to consider a ham license.
- Keep a scanner or receiver in your kit so you can listen without a license.
