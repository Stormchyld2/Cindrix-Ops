A scenario shows exactly why you need a backup plan.

You are home on a Monday morning. The kids are at school and everyone else is at work. Then you hear a huge rumble and the ground shakes. It seems to go on forever, but it is really only a few minutes. Things fall out of the cabinets, and the shelf with your emergency food tips over, shattering jars and sending cans across the floor. You can hear alarms all over the neighborhood. The power goes out.

As the shaking stops, you reach for your phone to call somebody, anybody.

"All circuits are busy..."

What is your plan to reach your loved ones? How will you get information?

## Why phones fail exactly when you need them
A phone network is built for ordinary days. When a disaster hits, four things tend to go wrong at once:

- **Too many people at once.** Everyone in the area calls family in the same minutes. The network cannot carry that much traffic, so calls get "all circuits are busy" or simply drop.
- **No power.** Cell towers, the equipment that links them together, and your home internet all need electricity. Many cell sites have batteries or a generator, but those last hours to a day or two, not weeks, and a generator needs fuel delivered.
- **Damage.** Storms, floods, fires and earthquakes can break towers, cut the fiber lines that join them to the rest of the network, or knock down poles.
- **Shut-offs on purpose.** During wildfire danger or other emergencies, utilities sometimes cut power on purpose, and that takes the local network down with it.

None of this is a surprise to the people who study it. Government guidance for years has said the same thing: expect the normal network to be overloaded or down, and have a plan that does not depend on it.

## What helps right away
These habits help before you ever buy a radio:

1. **Text instead of calling.** A text message uses far less network capacity than a voice call, and it will often get through when a call will not. It may arrive late, so send it once and wait.
2. **Keep calls short.** If you do reach someone, say the one thing that matters and hang up so the line is free for others.
3. **Use an out-of-area contact.** It is often easier to reach a person far away than someone across town. Pick one relative or friend outside your region and give everyone their number. Each family member checks in with that person, and that person tells the others who has called.
4. **Save power.** Turn down the screen brightness, close apps, and use low-power mode. Keep a charged power bank in every bag.
5. **Write the numbers down.** Your phone may be dead or lost. Keep a paper list of the phone numbers that matter, in your go-bag and in your wallet.

## Why you still need a real plan
Texting and an out-of-area contact only work while the phone network is up in some form. If it is truly down, you need a way to talk that does not depend on towers or the internet. That is radio, and a plan for which method you try first, second, third and fourth. That plan is called PACE, and the rest of this course teaches you how to build one and how to use the radios in it.

## Checklist
- Write down the phone numbers of every family member, your out-of-area contact, and two neighbors on paper.
- Pick an out-of-area contact and tell everyone who it is.
- Practice the text-first habit with your family once this week.
- Keep a charged power bank in each go-bag.
- Agree where you will meet if you cannot reach each other at all.

> The goal of this course is simple: by the end you can still talk to your people when the phones are dead.

? Why does a text message often get through when a phone call will not?
- Texts are always sent first by the network
+ It needs far less network capacity than a voice call
- It uses a separate radio tower for texts only
- It does not need any power at the tower
> A text takes very little capacity, so it can squeeze through an overloaded network. It can still arrive late.

? Why is an out-of-area contact useful?
- They can turn the local network back on
+ It is often easier to reach someone far away than someone across a busy local network
- They do not need a phone
- They can drive to you quickly
> A far-away person can pass news between family members who cannot reach each other directly.

? Which of these is NOT a common reason phones fail in a disaster?
- Towers and fiber lines lose power or are damaged
+ Phone batteries all fail at the same moment
- Too many people calling at once
- Utilities sometimes cut power on purpose
> The network fails because of overload, power loss, damage or planned shut-offs, not because every phone battery dies at once.

? You can reach a family member for a few seconds. What should you do?
- Talk as long as you can in case it drops
+ Say the one thing that matters and hang up so the line is free
- Call back every minute to check
- Ask them to hold while you find a pen
> Short calls leave capacity for other people and give you more chances to get through.
