PACE stands for **Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency**. It is a tiered plan for backup communications. You use the Primary method until it fails, then move to the Alternate, then the Contingency, then the Emergency. Each level is a different way of reaching people, and each one is built so that it does not depend on the one before it.

## What each level means
- **Primary.** The everyday method. It is the one everyone already uses.
- **Alternate.** The backup to the Primary.
- **Contingency.** Used when both the Primary and the Alternate have failed.
- **Emergency.** The final level, used when everything else is gone. At this level the plan may be as simple as meeting at an agreed place.

As you move down the list, the methods get simpler and depend on less technology. That makes them more reliable, but you can send less information through them. A Primary method may let you send a photo. An Emergency method may let you send only a few words.

## Five tests for every level
Check each level against these five questions:

1. **Feasible.** Do enough working devices and trained people exist for both sending and receiving?
2. **Acceptable.** Does using it interfere with other things you need to do?
3. **Suitable.** Does it carry enough information for the job?
4. **Distinguishable.** Does it work without the method that failed?
5. **Complete.** Is every level written down, along with the trigger that moves you to it?

## Build the plan in order
1. **List what you need to communicate.** For a household: "everyone is safe," "where we meet," "someone needs help." For a group: check-ins, orders, requests for help.
2. **Inventory what you have.** List every phone, radio, laptop, power source and skill you can use, along with its limits.
3. **Check the people.** Do you have the people and equipment to run each method? Who is trained?
4. **Draft the four levels.** Choose a method for each level and test it against the five questions above.
5. **Find the failure points.** For each method, ask what could break it. Pick methods that survive the conditions you expect in your area: storms, outages, earthquakes, fires.
6. **Remove shared dependencies.** Never use the same device or path twice. Email, a messaging app and texting may look like three methods, but if all three depend on cell data, you have one method, not three. A voice-over-internet call is a poor backup when the internet is down.
7. **Plan for both directions.** Think about sending and receiving. Choose an out-of-area contact people can call or text.
8. **Write the triggers.** Decide the exact condition that makes you move to the next level, such as "no answer to two texts and one call within 15 minutes, so go to the Alternate." Confirm the failure before you switch.
9. **Keep it simple.** A plan that is easy to run under stress is more reliable than a clever one.
10. **Involve the people who will use it.** The plan must make sense to the people who carry it out, not only the person who wrote it.

## An example, for a family
- **Primary:** Call, then text, using normal phone service. A group chat in a messaging app.
- **Alternate:** Messaging over Wi-Fi if cell service is down but the internet works. Email to the group address.
- **Contingency:** GMRS radios on the agreed channel at the agreed times, then a repeater if one is in range.
- **Emergency:** Meet at the agreed place. Leave a written note if you cannot wait.

## Common mistakes
- Using several methods that all share one device or path.
- Writing a plan that is too complicated to carry out under stress.
- Leaving the triggers vague.
- Not planning for incoming messages and for people who live in different places.
- Never practicing the plan, so nobody knows how to run the lower levels.
- Forgetting a "no communications" case.

## Checklist
- List what you need to communicate.
- Inventory your equipment and skills.
- Write all four levels.
- Check every level against the five tests.
- Make sure no two levels share a device or path.
- Write the trigger that moves you from each level to the next.
- Name an out-of-area contact.
- Print the plan and give a copy to every member.
- Test the plan, including a case where several methods fail one after another.
