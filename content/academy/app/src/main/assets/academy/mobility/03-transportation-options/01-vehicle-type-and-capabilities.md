Think beyond style and cool factor. The prepared person thinks about practical capability.

## Working with what you have
Most of us will use the vehicle we already own. Make the most of it.

**Prepare to be able to drive about 300 miles without getting fuel from another source.**
- Keep your vehicle above half a tank at all times.
- Carry extra fuel in approved containers you can put in the back of your car or in the truck bed.
- Follow the routine maintenance in the previous lessons.

## Fuel safety
Gasoline is dangerous. Follow these rules:
- Use **approved fuel containers** only, with spill-proof spouts and the right color (red for gasoline).
- Never carry fuel in the passenger compartment. Carry it outside, in the bed or on a rack, or in an open trailer. If you must use the trunk, keep the lid vented and the container upright and secured.
- Fill containers outdoors, **on the ground,** not in the vehicle bed, to avoid static sparks.
- Leave air space in the container for expansion. Do not fill past about 95 percent.
- Keep fuel away from ignition sources, including pilot lights and water heaters.
- Store fuel in a cool, shaded, ventilated place away from the house. Check local limits on the amount.
- Use a **fuel stabilizer** for long storage and rotate fuel every six to twelve months into the vehicle.
- Know your local laws on fuel storage and transport.

## Choosing a vehicle for preparedness
If you want to be serious about mobility, pick a vehicle that is capable, reliable and fixable.

- **Reliability first.** A plain, well-maintained, common vehicle you can repair beats an exotic one.
- **Four-wheel or all-wheel drive** with extra ground clearance helps in snow, mud and washouts.
- **Tires.** Good quality tires with a suitable tread pattern for your region. Carry a full-size spare. Consider winter tires where it snows.
- **A high-capacity winch** can be very helpful. Know how to use it safely, with recovery straps, shackles and gloves. Use rated recovery points only.
- **Auxiliary lighting** can help during disasters or emergencies.
- **A top-quality battery** with high cold-cranking amps, and a jump box as a backup.
- **A pickup truck** has the added benefit of an exterior bed, where fuel cans and supplies can be kept outside the passenger compartment.
- **Cargo space** for your family, gear and supplies. A roof rack or hitch carrier adds room.
- **Fuel type and range.** Diesel and gasoline are easy to store. Consider range, fuel economy and what is available.
- **Fixability.** Older or simpler vehicles with fewer electronics can be easier to repair in the field. Newer vehicles are safer and more reliable. Weigh them honestly.
- **Visibility and stealth.** A plain, quiet vehicle draws less attention than a heavily modified one.

Beware of over-building. Gear you cannot afford to maintain, and modifications that make the car less reliable, are not preparedness.

## Electric and hybrid vehicles
- Charging needs power. In an outage, charge before, not after.
- Know your range in cold and with a full load.
- Know where the 12-volt battery and manual releases are.
- Carry a charging cable and adapters.
- Plan routes with charging, or plan a backup vehicle.

## Other transportation
- **Bicycles.** Quiet, efficient and independent of fuel. A bike with panniers or a trailer can carry a lot. Keep a repair kit and pump.
- **Foot.** Good boots, a pack and fitness. Every other option may fail.
- **Motorcycles, scooters, e-bikes.** Efficient in traffic and fuel. Limited cargo.
- **Trailers.** Extend capacity but reduce maneuverability. Check the hitch, brakes and lights.
- **Boats.** Useful near water. Plan for weather, licensing and safety gear.
- **Animals.** Horses and pack animals have a place in rural areas, with care and feed.

## Share the load
In a group, **not everyone needs the same vehicle.** Mix: a truck to haul, a fuel-efficient car for scouting, a bicycle for local runs. Agree who carries what, and who drives which route.

## Know the legal and safe limits
- Never exceed your vehicle's rated payload or towing capacity.
- Secure loads so nothing shifts in a stop.
- Follow traffic laws.

## Checklist
- Range of 300 miles without refueling elsewhere
- Fuel stored in approved containers and rotated
- Spare tire, jack and jump box tested
- Tires suited to your climate
- A plan if your main vehicle is out of action
- Group vehicle roles written down

> The best vehicle for preparedness is the one that runs reliably when you need it.
