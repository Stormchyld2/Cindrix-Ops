Know when to move, where to go and how to get there.

Most people who get hurt in evacuations did not leave too slowly. They decided too late. This lesson helps you make the hard decisions now, while it is calm: what will make you leave, where you will go, how you will get there and who is counting on you.

## Ready, Set, Go
Many fire and sheriff agencies use a three-step model that works for any hazard.

- **Ready: prepare before you need to leave.** Make an evacuation plan for your family and animals. Pack a go kit. Make a list of valuables. Sign up for public warning messages.
- **Set: watch conditions.** Know how you will receive alerts and where to find updates from your sheriff and emergency management office. Gather valuables so you can leave quickly.
- **Go: leave early.** Do not wait for an official order if your home is threatened or you feel unsafe. Emergency services may not be able to help if you stay.

## Step 1: Know your risks and your zone
Identify the hazards where you live (wildfire, flood, hurricane, chemical release, earthquake aftermath). Find your evacuation zone number if your county uses zones. Sign up for your county's emergency alerts and buy a NOAA weather radio.

## Step 2: Set your trigger
Learn the difference between a **warning** (be ready and watching, or leave if you are in a vulnerable group) and an **order** (go now). Decide in advance what makes your household leave. If you have children, pets, livestock, or anyone with limited mobility, **leave at the warning,** not the order, because you need more time.

Write your triggers down. Examples:
- "If a wildfire is within 10 miles and the wind is toward us, we go."
- "If the river reaches the marker on the bridge, we go."
- "If our zone is named in a hurricane evacuation notice, we go that day."

## Step 3: Pick your meeting places and destination
- **Neighborhood meeting spot.** A specific landmark close enough for children to walk to, safely away from the house, reachable without crossing hazards, visible to responders and not dependent on anyone being home.
- **Out-of-neighborhood meeting spot.** A public landmark such as a library or school parking lot that everyone can reach from work or school, outside likely hazard zones, with room to wait. Pick a backup spot too.
- **Evacuation destination.** Line up two options in different directions, far enough out to be clear of the hazard (50 miles is a common planning figure) and reachable on one tank of fuel. Confirm they will take you, pets and all. Write down the address and phone number. Keep a hotel as a backup.
- **For any destination:** every family member, including children, should know how to get there and have it written down.

## Step 4: Map at least two routes out
Plan routes in different directions and print them with **written** directions. GPS can send you toward closed roads or into the hazard, and cell networks often fail. Learn the back roads. Follow recommended evacuation routes on the day and avoid shortcuts, which may be blocked.

## Step 5: Plan for everyone who depends on you
- What if the children are at school and you are at work? Learn the school's plan and who may pick up.
- What about elderly relatives, pets, livestock, medicines and medical equipment?
- If you do not own a vehicle, arrange a ride **now** with family, friends or neighbors.
- Take **one car per family** when possible to reduce congestion.

## The six Ps
When you are rushed, remember the six Ps:
1. **People** (and pets).
2. **Prescriptions** and personal needs.
3. **Papers:** identification, insurance, deeds and key documents (copies or a scan).
4. **Photos and valuables,** including photos or video of your home and belongings for insurance.
5. **Plan and supplies:** your go bag and emergency kit.
6. **Pets and animals,** with carriers, food and records.

## If time allows
- Tell your out-of-area contact where you are going.
- Leave a note on the door saying who you are, when you left and where you are going.
- Lock doors and windows. Unplug small appliances. Leave refrigerators and freezers on unless flooding is likely.
- If told to by officials, shut off water, gas and electricity.
- Wear sturdy shoes and protective clothing.
- Check on neighbors who may need a ride.

## Step 6: Practice it
This is where an idea becomes a plan. Do a timed drill: can everyone be in the car with go bags in 10 minutes? Drive your routes once. Afterward hold a short review and fix what went wrong (see the Admin and Leadership course on after action reviews).

## Checklist
- Primary rendezvous location chosen
- Secondary rendezvous location chosen
- Out-of-area contact plan complete
- Go bag packed and ready for each family member
- Evacuation zone recorded
- Signed up for local emergency alerts
- Two routes printed with written directions
- Timed drill completed

> Check the internet, local radio or television, or a NOAA weather radio to find out what is needed in your area.
