You need to set this up **in advance.** Trying to "bug out" with no destination just makes you a refugee: you arrive with no welcome, no supplies and no plan.

## Why a destination matters
- Shelters can fill quickly, rarely take pets other than service animals, and are a last resort.
- Hotels near a disaster book out within hours.
- Roads and fuel stations are crowded, so wandering burns time and fuel.
- A named destination lets your group and your out-of-area contact know where you are.

## Build relationships with people who could host you
Think in rings:

1. **Outside your local area** (a neighboring town).
2. **Outside your region** (a neighboring county).
3. **Outside your state.** If you live in Washington, it helps to have a friend in Idaho.

Talk to them about **mutual shelter:** if they ever needed a place to stay they could count on you, and you could count on them. Agree on:
- How many people and pets you could bring.
- How long you could stay.
- What you will bring (food, bedding, your own medicines).
- How you would contact each other if phones fail.
- What the house rules are.

Treat it as a favor you will return, not a right. Offer to pay your way and to help with chores.

## Research locations
Look at places you could go if no one can host you:
- Campgrounds, including state park and national forest sites.
- Dispersed camping areas on public land.
- Small towns with large parking lots where you could park an RV overnight in a pinch (always ask permission).
- Hotels and motels outside the hazard area, with the phone number saved.
- Churches and community groups that may offer help.

**Tip:** check in advance some remote short-term rentals along your planned route. In a last-resort emergency, an owner may be glad to have you there, and you will already know the address.

## Match the destination to the hazard
- **Wildfire:** away from fuel and wind, in the opposite direction from the fire, in a town with services.
- **Flood:** higher ground, away from rivers and low roads.
- **Hurricane:** inland and out of the surge zone, far enough that wind is survivable.
- **Winter storm or outage:** a place with heat and power. Often a short trip.
- **Chemical release or other sudden hazard:** often sheltering in place is correct. Follow officials.

## Write it down
For each destination keep a card in the go bag with:
- Name, address, phone number.
- Route A and route B.
- Who is expecting you.
- Hours or gate codes.
- A note on pets and medical needs.

## Test it
At least once a year, call the people you would stay with, confirm the details are still true and update the card. If possible, drive there once.

## Checklist
- Three hosts identified (local, regional, out of state)
- Mutual shelter agreement discussed with each
- Two campgrounds or alternates researched
- Destination cards in each go bag
- Annual confirmation call on the calendar

> A destination you have visited and agreed in advance is worth more than any amount of gear.

- A hotel booking
- A tent
+ An agreement that each household can stay with the other in an emergency
- A public shelter
