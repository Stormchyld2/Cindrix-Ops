You cannot predict an earthquake, but you can know your risk and get ready.

## What an earthquake is
The Earth's crust is broken into huge plates that slowly move. Along the edges and cracks (**faults**), they stick, build strain and then slip. The slip sends **seismic waves** through the ground. That is the shaking.

- **Magnitude** measures the energy released at the source. Each whole number is about 32 times more energy.
- **Intensity** measures how strongly you feel it at a place. It depends on distance, ground type and building quality.
- **Epicenter** is the spot on the surface above where the slip began.
- **Aftershocks** are smaller quakes that follow, for days, weeks or months. Some are strong enough to bring down damaged buildings.
- **Foreshocks** are small quakes before a bigger one, but you only know afterward which was which.

## No warning, no prediction
- No one can say when and where a particular earthquake will strike. Be wary of anyone who claims to predict dates.
- Some regions have **earthquake early warning** systems that can give seconds of notice. Check if yours does and turn on alerts on your phone.
- Shaking can last a few seconds to a minute or more.

## Where earthquakes happen
- Most are along plate edges: the Pacific coast (California, Oregon, Washington, Alaska, Hawaii).
- **They also happen in other places.** Large earthquakes have struck the central US (New Madrid zone, in Missouri, Arkansas, Tennessee, Kentucky) and the east coast. Induced earthquakes from wastewater injection have happened in Oklahoma, Texas and elsewhere.
- The USGS publishes **hazard maps** that show shaking potential for every part of the US. **Look yours up** and note the risk.

## What causes the damage
- **Shaking** damages buildings, bridges, roads and utility lines.
- **Falling objects:** most injuries come from things that fall, such as furniture, shelves, ceilings and glass.
- **Ground failure:** **landslides,** **liquefaction** (wet soil acting like liquid) and cracking.
- **Fires** from broken gas lines and electrical damage.
- **Tsunamis** along coasts.
- **Dam and levee failures.**
- **Loss of utilities,** including water, power, gas, sewer, phones and internet, for days or weeks.
- **Blocked roads** and bridges.

## Know your local risks
Ask or look up:
- Are you near a fault?
- Is your ground firm rock or soft soil, fill or a reclaimed area? Soft ground shakes harder.
- Is your home **built before modern codes?** Older unreinforced masonry, soft-story buildings (garage or open space on the ground floor) and un-bolted houses are at higher risk.
- Are you in a **tsunami zone,** below a dam or on a steep slope?
- What will the neighborhood look like with roads and bridges out?

## The Seven Steps
Earthquake safety follows seven steps. The first four are before, and the last three are during and after:
1. Secure your space
2. Plan to be safe
3. Organize disaster supplies
4. Minimize financial hardship
5. Drop, cover and hold on
6. Improve safety after shaking
7. Reconnect and restore

The five principles in this Academy line up: **prevention and hazard assessment** (steps 1 and 4), **preparedness** (steps 2 and 3), **response** (steps 5 and 6), **mitigation** (retrofits) and **recovery** (step 7).

## Checklist
- Looked up your area's hazard on the USGS map
- Know whether your area has early warning alerts
- Know your ground type and building age
- Know your local secondary hazards (tsunami, landslide, dam)

> Earthquake guidance is the same across the US, but local hazards differ. Check your state geological survey and emergency management office.
