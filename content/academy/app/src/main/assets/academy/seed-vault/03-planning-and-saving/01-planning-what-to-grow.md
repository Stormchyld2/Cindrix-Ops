A plan makes the difference between a pile of seed and a harvest.

## Know your site
- **Sun:** most vegetables need 6 to 8 hours of direct sun. Leafy greens tolerate less.
- **Soil:** test it (a kit or your Extension office). Know the pH (6.0 to 7.0 suits most vegetables) and nutrients.
- **Water:** is there a reliable source near the garden?
- **Drainage:** avoid low spots that flood.
- **Wind and frost pockets:** use fences or hedges, and avoid the bottom of a slope where cold air collects.
- **Access:** near the house is better. A garden you see gets care.

## Know your season
- **Last spring frost and first fall frost** give your growing window.
- **Cool-season crops** (peas, spinach, lettuce, kale, radishes, carrots, cabbage, potatoes) go in early spring or late summer.
- **Warm-season crops** (beans, corn, squash, tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, melons, sweet potatoes) go in after the last frost.
- Count back from your first frost: "days to maturity" must fit.

## Make a garden map
1. **Draw** your plot to scale.
2. **Mark** sun, shade and water.
3. **Divide** into beds or rows about 3 to 4 feet wide, so you can reach the middle.
4. **Assign** crops, grouping tall ones (corn, pole beans) on the north side so they do not shade the others.
5. **Rotate** crops: do not plant the same family in the same spot two years in a row. Rotation reduces disease and pests. Use a 3 or 4 year cycle: legumes, leafy crops, fruiting crops, root crops.
6. **Plan** succession planting.
7. **Add** companion plants: marigolds, herbs and flowers that attract pollinators.

## The three sisters
Corn, beans and squash grown together: corn provides a pole for beans, beans add nitrogen, and squash shades the soil. It is a traditional Native American method that works well in many places.

## Write a planting calendar
List each crop with: when to start seeds indoors, when to sow outdoors, when to transplant, and when to harvest. Use frost dates as anchors. Keep it in your binder.

## Count your seed
For each crop, work out how many plants you need and how many seeds that is (including extra for thinning and failures). Compare with the seed you have. Make a shopping list.

## Start where you are
- **Too little space?** Use containers, raised beds, vertical trellises and window boxes (see the next lessons).
- **Too little time?** Plant fewer things, and plant well.
- **New to gardening?** Start with 100 to 200 square feet and a handful of easy crops: beans, lettuce, radishes, tomatoes, zucchini and herbs.

## Keep records
Note planting dates, weather, pests, yield and what you would do differently. A garden journal is a seed vault of knowledge.

## Checklist
- A garden map and a planting calendar
- Soil tested and improved
- A seed list matched to the plan
- A crop rotation plan

> Growing seasons, frost dates and pests vary by place. Ask your local Cooperative Extension office (find yours at nifa.usda.gov) for advice that fits your area.
