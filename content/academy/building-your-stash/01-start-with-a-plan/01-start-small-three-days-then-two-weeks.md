> Basic planning guidance. Prices change, so this course names no prices. Check anything about chemicals or food storage against the sources named in the lesson before you rely on it.

Most people never start because the job feels huge. Make it small and keep going.

## The steps
1. **Three days.** Water, food that needs no cooking, a light, a radio, a first aid kit, your medicines and papers. Ready.gov starts here.
2. **Two weeks.** Many real emergencies last longer than three days. Storms, ice, floods and long power cuts can cut off shops and roads for a week or more.
3. **A month and beyond.** Add as money and space allow.

## Count people and pets
Plan for **each person and each animal in the house**. Add extra for guests. Babies, older people, pregnant women and anyone ill need special items.

## Write the goal down
"Two weeks for four people and a dog." A written goal is easier to buy towards than a feeling.

## Buy a little at every shop
Add a few extra items each time you shop. Over a few months it grows without hurting the budget.

> A small stash you actually have beats a perfect one you keep planning.

## Going deeper: why three days, then two weeks
FEMA and the Red Cross advise at least three days of supplies for each person, because that is how long it usually takes for roads to reopen, power crews to start and relief to arrive after a local emergency. Many storms, floods and ice events run longer, so Ready.gov now says to aim for **two weeks** where you can. Three days is a floor, not a finish line.

Build in layers:
1. **Layer one, the grab-and-go bag:** water, snacks, light, a radio, a first aid kit and copies of papers, small enough to carry out the door.
2. **Layer two, three days at home:** food you can eat without power, a gallon of water per person per day, and a way to see, stay warm and keep clean.
3. **Layer three, two weeks:** more of the same, plus things that make life steadier: a second water source, a stove and fuel, spare batteries, sanitation supplies.
4. **Layer four, longer than a month:** only after the first three work. Bulk staples, a garden and skills.

## Match the plan to your risks
Look at the hazards where you live: hurricane, flood, wildfire, earthquake, blizzard, heat, long outage, job loss. Each one changes what you need first. A flood or fire may mean leaving in minutes, so the bag matters most. A winter storm means staying put, so heat and food matter most. Write your top three risks at the top of your list and check every purchase against them.

## Budget and pace
Spend a small, fixed amount each pay period. Many families add one or two items at each shop. A plan that fits your budget and is finished beats a grand plan that stalls. Keep receipts in a folder so you know what you bought and when.
