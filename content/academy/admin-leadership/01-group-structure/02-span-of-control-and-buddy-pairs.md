People can only manage so many others at one time. This idea is called **span of control.** Emergency services that run big incidents use it to keep order, and it works just as well for a preparedness group.

## What the experts say
The Incident Command System (ICS), used by fire, police and emergency management teams across the United States, says that one person can effectively supervise between **three and seven** people, and that **five** is the ideal. Beyond that, communication slows, mistakes grow and people feel unseen.

## Adding layers makes things easier
Instead of one leader communicating with every member, break the group into manageable teams.

If you have 16 people, you might form two teams of eight, for example "Blue" and "Gold," or "North" and "South." Then the leader only needs to talk to two team leaders, and those people talk to their teams.

When a group grows past seven or so, split it. Many groups find a good number is **eight per team,** and **no more than about four teams** under one overall leader.

## The building block: buddy pairs
Give every member a **buddy.**

- The Army has its Ranger buddies. Navy divers have their swim buddies. Your team needs buddy pairs too.
- A buddy is the person who **has your back.** They check on you, notice when something is wrong, share your load and share your risks.
- A buddy pair makes sure no one is ever alone.

Two buddy pairs can make a four-person **support team** (the military calls it a fire team). Several support teams make up an **area group.**

## A simple structure
- **Pair:** two people, who look out for each other.
- **Team:** two pairs, four people with a team leader.
- **Section:** two teams, eight people.
- **Group:** two to four sections, 16 to 32 people, with an overall leader, a deputy and a few role holders.

## Buddy rules
1. **Everyone has a buddy** and everyone knows who it is.
2. **Buddies check in** on each other at set times in training and in an emergency.
3. **Buddies go out together.** No one goes out of camp or home area alone.
4. **A buddy reports** if the other is hurt, late, missing or struggling.
5. **Rotate buddies** now and then so everyone knows everyone.
6. **Match skills where possible,** so each pair has some medical, radio and navigation ability.

## Delegate
The leader will have a lot on their plate. Delegating spreads the load and helps every member contribute. We cover roles in a later lesson.

## Checklist
- Count your members and decide whether you need to split into teams.
- Assign every member a buddy and write down the pairs.
- Name a team leader for each team.
- Practice a buddy check at your next meet-up.

> No one should ever be alone, and no leader should have more than about seven direct reports.
