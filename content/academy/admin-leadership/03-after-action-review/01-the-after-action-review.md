An **After Action Review (AAR)** is a short, structured discussion held after an event. The military and first responders use it to record what went well, what did **not**, and how to do better next time.

You can use an AAR after:

- A **training event** or drill.
- An **emergency response**, real or practiced.
- An **accident or near miss.**
- A **group meeting** or big project.

By analyzing an event this way, you improve every time and everyone hears the lessons.

## What an AAR is, and is not
It **is:**
- A discussion among the people who took part.
- Focused on **what happened and why.**
- Aimed at **learning and improving.**
- Honest and respectful, with every voice heard.

It is **not:**
- A search for someone to blame.
- A lecture from the leader.
- A grade or a performance review.
- A long meeting.

## The four questions
The Army's method asks four simple questions:

1. **What did we plan or expect to happen?**
2. **What actually happened?**
3. **Why was there a difference?**
4. **What will we do differently next time? (What do we sustain and what do we improve?)**

## Holding an AAR step by step
1. **Hold it soon.** Within a day or two, while memories are fresh. For a small drill, do it on the spot.
2. **Gather the people** who took part. Include the youngest and newest.
3. **Choose a facilitator.** Often not the leader, so the leader can take part honestly.
4. **Set the tone.** "We are here to learn, not to blame. Everyone's view counts."
5. **Restate the plan or purpose.** What was the goal?
6. **Walk through what happened,** in order, with each person adding their view. Use a timeline on a board.
7. **Ask why.** Dig into causes. Keep asking until you reach something you can change.
8. **List sustains.** What should we **keep** doing, because it worked?
9. **List improves.** What should we **change?**
10. **Assign actions.** Each improvement needs an **owner** and a **date.**
11. **Record it.** Write a short summary and store it in the group binder.
12. **Follow up.** Review the actions at the next meeting.

## A simple AAR template
- Date, event and place.
- Who took part.
- The goal or plan.
- What happened (timeline).
- What went well (sustain).
- What did not go well (improve).
- Why (root causes).
- Actions: what, who, by when.
- Follow-up date.

## Tips for a good AAR
- **Ask open questions:** "What happened when we lost the radio signal?"
- **Be specific.** "The battery was dead" is more useful than "communications were poor."
- **Look for causes, not people.** Ask "what in our process allowed this?"
- **Let junior members speak first.** Seniors can shape what others say.
- **Praise what worked.** Recording successes is as useful as listing failures.
- **Keep it short.** Thirty to sixty minutes is enough for most events.
- **Follow through.** An AAR that leads to no change teaches people not to bother.

## After a real emergency or accident
If someone was hurt, take extra care. Support anyone upset. Report as required by law and the group's rules. Use the AAR for learning and keep legal and medical matters separate.

## Checklist
- Set up a standard AAR template.
- Review a recent event or drill using the four questions.
- Document gaps.
- Assign corrective actions with owners and dates.
- Store each AAR in the group binder.
- Review the actions at the next meeting.

> The aim is not to find who is at fault. It is to find what to fix.
