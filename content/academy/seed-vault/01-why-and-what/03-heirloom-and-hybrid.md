Some seed comes true when you save it. Some does not. Knowing the difference decides what you can keep growing.

## Open-pollinated seed
Plants that are pollinated by wind, insects or themselves and **produce seed that grows into plants much like the parents.** If you save seed from a good plant, you get similar plants next year.
- **Heirloom:** an open-pollinated variety that has been passed down, often for 50 years or more. Many have excellent flavor and local adaptation.
- **Open-pollinated (non-heirloom):** modern varieties that also come true.
These are the best choice for a seed vault, because you can keep saving them.

## Hybrid seed (F1)
Created by crossing two different parent lines. The seed packet gives **vigorous, uniform plants** with good yield or disease resistance. But **seed saved from them does not grow true.** The next generation can be different, weak or unproductive.
- **Hybrids are not bad.** Many are excellent producers, and in a short season or with disease pressure, they can out-yield other varieties.
- **You must buy** new hybrid seed each year.
- A vault can hold some hybrid seed for planting, but not for saving.

## GMO seed
Genetically engineered seed is mostly commercial field crops (corn, soybeans, cotton, canola, sugar beets, and a few others). Seed packets sold for home gardens are generally not GMO. Some GMO seed is patented, with rules on saving. Read the seed label if this matters to you.

## Treated and coated seed
Some seed is coated with fungicide, insecticide or a pelleting clay. It is marked on the packet. **Do not eat treated seed.** Keep it away from children and pets.

## How to tell
- Look for **"F1"** or **"hybrid"** on the packet. That means it will not come true.
- Look for **"heirloom"** or **"open-pollinated."**
- If unsure, ask the seller.

## Which to choose
- **For the vault:** mostly open-pollinated and heirloom seed, in a mix that suits your climate. They let you become independent.
- **For production:** a few reliable hybrids where they truly help, such as in a short or disease-prone season.
- **For flavor and fun:** try a few unusual heirlooms.

## Saving true seed
To keep a variety pure, you need to prevent crossing with other varieties of the same species (more in the saving lesson). Self-pollinating crops such as beans, peas, lettuce and tomatoes are the easiest for beginners.

## Crops that are easiest to save
- **Beans and peas**
- **Lettuce**
- **Tomatoes**
- **Peppers** (with some isolation)
- **Herbs** such as cilantro and dill
