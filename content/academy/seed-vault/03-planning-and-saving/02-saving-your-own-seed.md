Saving seed completes the cycle. Each year you can keep your best plants' seed and have no need to buy more.

## Before you start
- **Use open-pollinated or heirloom varieties.** Hybrid seed will not come true.
- **Choose healthy, productive plants** with the traits you want. Mark them early in the season. Do not save seed from weak or diseased plants.
- **Save from several plants,** at least 5 to 10 if you can, to keep the variety strong (more genetic variety).
- **Let the seed mature on the plant.** For most crops, that means leaving the fruit or pod until it is past the eating stage.

## Easiest first: self-pollinating crops
These cross rarely, so you can grow different varieties near each other.
- **Beans and peas:** let pods dry on the plant until brown and rattling. Shell, dry further for two weeks, and store.
- **Lettuce:** let a plant "bolt" and flower. Collect seed when the fluffy heads form. Shake into a bag.
- **Tomatoes:** pick fully ripe fruit, scoop out the seeds and gel into a jar with a little water, ferment for 2 to 3 days (stir daily) until a mold forms on top and the seeds settle, rinse, and dry on a plate or screen.
- **Peppers and eggplant:** let fruit ripen fully (peppers go red or orange), scrape out the seed, and dry. Isolate hot peppers from sweet ones, since insects can cross them.
- **Herbs:** let cilantro, dill, basil and others flower and dry on the stalk.

## Crops that cross easily (cross-pollinators)
Insects and wind move pollen between plants, so different varieties of the same species can mix.
- **Squash and pumpkin** (same species cross): grow only one variety of each species, or **hand-pollinate** and tape the flower closed. Squash have separate male and female flowers.
- **Corn:** wind-pollinated. Separate varieties by at least 250 to 1,000 feet, or plant at different times so they do not flower together.
- **Cucumbers and melons.**
- **Cabbage family** (broccoli, cabbage, kale, cauliflower) cross freely.
Learn which species each crop belongs to. Squash includes several species (Cucurbita pepo, maxima, moschata), and varieties of different species do not cross.

## Biennials: two-year crops
These produce seed in the second year.
- **Carrots, beets, onions, cabbage, parsnips and kale.**
- **Overwinter** the plants in the garden (in mild climates), or store roots in a cool cellar and replant in spring. They then flower and set seed.
- **Plan** for the extra year and the space.

## Wet seed and dry seed
- **Dry seed** (beans, peas, lettuce, corn, herbs): dry on the plant, then finish drying indoors.
- **Wet seed** (tomatoes, cucumbers, melons, squash, peppers): wash and ferment as needed, then dry.

## Cleaning seed
- **Winnow:** pour seed from one container to another in a light breeze, so chaff blows away.
- **Screens:** sift through fine screens.
- **Water:** for wet seeds, good seed sinks and floats are discarded.

## Drying
Dry seed on a screen or plate in a warm, shady, airy place for a week or two. Seed should be hard and break rather than bend. Do not dry in strong sun or in an oven.

## Store and label
Follow the storage lesson: airtight, dry, cool and dark. Label with crop, variety, year, where it grew and traits.

## Keep it pure
- **Isolation distances,** timing or barriers prevent crossing.
- **Remove** off-type plants before they flower ("rogueing").
- **Keep records.**

## Share
Swap seed with neighbors and seed libraries. Sharing spreads local genetics and builds resilience. Check local laws about selling seed.

## Checklist
- Chose open-pollinated varieties to save
- Marked the best plants
- Dried, cleaned and labeled seed
- A plan for isolation or hand-pollination

> Growing seasons, frost dates and pests vary by place. Ask your local Cooperative Extension office (find yours at nifa.usda.gov) for advice that fits your area.
