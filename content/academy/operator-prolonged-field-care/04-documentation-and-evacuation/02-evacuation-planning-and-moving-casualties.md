At some point, the casualty must be moved to higher care. When and how to go are major decisions.

## Decide: wait or go?
**Go early if:**
- The condition is serious and getting worse (airway, breathing, circulation, head injury, large burns).
- You cannot control bleeding or breathing.
- The person has a major injury that needs surgery.
- Weather or darkness will make the move harder later.
- Help is not coming.
**Wait if:**
- The person is stable and moving would harm them.
- A rescue is on its way and the route is dangerous.
- The weather or terrain makes the move riskier than staying.
Make the decision with your team, and write down your reasoning.

## Plan the evacuation
- **Where to:** the closest facility that can treat the injury. Check hours and capabilities.
- **How:** vehicle, carry, litter, boat, helicopter (via 911).
- **Route:** choose the safest, not just the shortest. Have alternates.
- **Who:** enough people to carry, rest and care.
- **Gear:** casualty supplies, water, food, communication, light.
- **When:** time of day and weather.
- **Alert** the receiving facility if you can.

## Call for help: the 9-line concept
Emergency services ask for a standard set of information. A civilian version for a dispatcher or radio:
1. Your location (coordinates, address, landmarks)
2. Your call sign or name and contact number
3. Number of casualties and their urgency
4. Special equipment needed
5. Injuries and condition
6. Hazards or security issues
7. Landing zone or access details and marking
8. Patient nationality or status if relevant
9. Terrain or obstacles

## Landing zone (helicopter)
- Clear, flat area about 100 feet by 100 feet, away from wires, poles, and trees.
- Mark with bright cloth at the corners; do not use flares in dry vegetation.
- Remove loose objects. Protect the casualty's eyes and ears.
- Approach only when the pilot signals, from the front, never behind.
- Follow the crew's instructions.

## Moving the casualty
- **Litter:** a blanket, a door, a backboard, a tarp or poles through jackets. Four to six bearers for a distance, rotated often.
- **Carry techniques:** two-person seat carry, fireman's carry, drag for short distances.
- **Package the casualty:** warm, padded, tied securely, head slightly raised unless a spine concern, with the airway visible.
- **Keep care going** during the move: check breathing and bleeding often.
- **Protect bearers:** rest, water, and rotation. Back injuries are common.

## On the road
Drive smoothly. Have a helper in the back. Do not speed so much that you cause a crash.

## At arrival
Hand over with MIST, give the record and answer questions.

## After
Stay to give information. Care for yourselves. Return and review.

> This course teaches the thinking and the hands-on steps a layperson can do. Medicines, needles, fluids and surgery belong to trained clinicians. Take an in-person Wilderness First Responder, Wilderness EMT or Tactical Casualty Care course. Always call for help if you can.
