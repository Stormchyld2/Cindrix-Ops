The United States has several radio services, each with its own rules. Knowing which is which keeps you legal and keeps your radios useful.

## FRS: Family Radio Service
- **No license needed.**
- Small handheld radios sold in stores, often in pairs.
- Channels 1 to 22 (shared with GMRS on many channels).
- Power limits are low on some channels, and the antenna must be built into the radio.
- Range is usually under a mile or two.

## GMRS: General Mobile Radio Service
- **A license is required** (no exam).
- One license covers the licensee and their **immediate family**.
- Allows **higher power** (up to 50 watts on the main channels), **detachable and outside antennas** and **repeaters.**
- Real-world range runs from a few miles on handhelds to many miles through a repeater.
- The license lasts **10 years.**
- Many radios sold for FRS also work on GMRS channels. If a radio can transmit on the GMRS-only channels or above the FRS power limits, a GMRS license is needed.

## MURS: Multi-Use Radio Service
- **No license needed.**
- Five VHF channels.
- Low power (2 watts), and any antenna is allowed.
- Often used for property and farm use. Fewer radios are made for it.

## CB: Citizens Band
- **No license needed.**
- 40 channels on the 27 MHz band.
- Long-wavelength signals, so range can be a few miles. Skip conditions sometimes carry far farther, and that can bring unwanted noise and distant stations.
- Truckers use it. Channel 9 is a traditional emergency channel and channel 19 is popular on highways.

## Amateur (ham) radio
- **A license is required** and you must pass an exam.
- Many bands, from low-frequency worldwide (HF) to local VHF and UHF.
- Much higher power allowed (up to 1,500 watts on many bands), many kinds of antennas and modes.
- You can build and modify your own equipment.
- You cannot use it for business, you cannot encrypt, and you must identify with your callsign.
- Licenses last **10 years.**

## Weather radio and listening
- **NOAA Weather Radio** broadcasts continuous forecasts and alerts on seven channels near 162 MHz. Receive-only. No license needed. Many handhelds receive it.
- **Scanners and receivers** can listen to public safety and other traffic. **Listening** to most things is legal in the US, with some limits, such as private phone calls and some encrypted traffic. Check local law about using scanners in vehicles.

## Which should you start with?
- **A household of a few people, a few miles, no studying:** GMRS.
- **Cheap short-range for kids or errands:** FRS.
- **A farm or property:** MURS.
- **Regional or national messages, tinkering, a hobby community:** ham.

## Important rules in all services
- **No false or misleading messages** and no calls for help that are not real.
- **No obscene language.**
- **Do not interfere** deliberately with others.
- **Do not use radios for criminal purposes.**
- **Know the equipment rules:** certain radios are certified for certain services. Using a ham-only radio on GMRS or FRS channels without authorization is not allowed.

## A note about cheap imported radios
Many inexpensive handhelds can transmit on any frequency, but they are not certified for GMRS or FRS and may not meet the rules. **Transmitting outside your license or the rules can lead to fines.** Listening is always allowed. If you hold a ham license, you may use such a radio on ham bands, within the rules for ham equipment.

> Rules and fees change. Check fcc.gov, your exam team and the radio's own manual before you rely on any number here.
