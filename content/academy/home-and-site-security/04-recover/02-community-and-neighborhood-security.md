A lone household is weaker than a street of neighbors who know each other. Community is the strongest layer of security.

## Start with a conversation
Knock on doors. Introduce yourself. Exchange phone numbers. Learn who has children, who is elderly and alone, who has medical skills, tools or generators.

## Neighborhood Watch
- The National Neighborhood Watch program teaches residents to observe and report, **not to confront.**
- Hold regular meetings, share contact lists and use a group message or a call tree.
- Agree on what to report: unfamiliar vehicles, door-to-door strangers, repeated cars, signs of break-ins.
- Work with your local police, who can train and support you.
- Put up signs only if the watch is real.

## CERT: Community Emergency Response Team
FEMA's CERT program trains residents in basic disaster response: fire safety, light search and rescue, first aid and triage. Take the course if offered in your area.

## Group plans
- Map the street: who lives where, special needs, resources, hazards, water, shelter spots.
- Agree on meeting places and a simple signal for help.
- Share tools and skills: chainsaw, first aid, generator, radios, garden.
- Plan for the vulnerable.

## After a disaster
Neighbors check on each other first. Share information, organize a watch, pool resources, and use common sense and courtesy. Avoid rumors. Do not form armed patrols or roadblocks. They are dangerous and often illegal. Work with authorities.

## Conflict and trust
Disagreements will happen. Use a calm, fair process: listen, agree on rules and a person to mediate. Keep group decisions in writing. Keep out people who create trouble.

## Keep it positive
A community that holds block parties, helps with chores and looks after each other is safer, and also happier.

> Laws on self-defense, firearms and force differ by state and country. This course is general safety education, not legal advice. Talk to a local attorney and take a certified class.
