A plan written down and practiced beats a plan in someone's head.

## What the plan contains
1. **Roles:** who calls 911, who gets the children, who shuts off power, who meets responders.
2. **Signals:** a code word for "danger, go to the safe room," and one for "all clear."
3. **Contact list:** police, fire, neighbors, family, doctor, poison control.
4. **Routes and meeting places:** inside and outside the home.
5. **Rules for visitors,** deliveries, and strangers at the door.
6. **Rules for night:** who sleeps where, who locks up, a lamp by the bed.
7. **Rules for leaving** the house: lock, lights, alarm.
8. **Special needs:** a person with limited mobility, pets and medicines.

## Watches and checks
In a long emergency, a family may keep a **watch:** a person awake and on duty in turns. Keep it simple.
- Two- or three-hour shifts, so no one is exhausted.
- A log: time, observations, handover.
- Rules: stay inside, call for help, do not go out alone.
- A radio or phone to alert others.

## Drills
Practice these at least twice a year:
- Fire at night: get out.
- Intruder in the house: get to the safe room and call.
- Strangers at the door: do not open, ask who, call.
- Weather or other event: shelter in place.
Time each drill and discuss it. Keep it calm, short and without fear.

## Include children
Use age-appropriate language. Teach them: do not open the door, go to the safe room, call 911, know the address. Practice dialing a phone, and tell them they will never be in trouble for calling.

## Review
After any drill or real event, hold a short review: what worked, what did not, and what we change. Update the plan.

## Share with trusted neighbors
A neighbor who knows your plan and your code word can be your best backup.

> Laws on self-defense, firearms and force differ by state and country. This course is general safety education, not legal advice. Talk to a local attorney and take a certified class.
