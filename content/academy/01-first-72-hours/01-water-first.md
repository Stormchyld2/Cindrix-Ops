[video] Water comes first

Most people can live about three days without water, but clear thinking fails sooner when you are short of it. In most home emergencies, water is the first thing to secure.

## How much
- Plan on **one gallon per person per day** for drinking and basic hygiene. Half is for drinking and half for cooking and cleaning.
- Add more for children, nursing mothers, sick people and hot weather.
- Count pets too. A dog needs about an ounce of water per pound of body weight each day, and a cat needs less.
- Aim for **at least three days, and two weeks if you can.**

## In the first hour
1. If you know the supply may be cut off, **fill clean bathtubs, pots, bottles and sinks** right away. A bathtub holds many days of water for washing and flushing.
2. **Know your hidden water:** the water heater tank (turn off the power or gas first, and drain from the bottom valve), toilet tanks (not the bowls, and not if you use cleaning tablets), melted ice cubes, and canned vegetables and fruit juices.
3. **Do not drink flood water** or water you cannot trust. It may carry sewage, fuel and chemicals that boiling does not remove.
4. **Listen for a boil-water notice.** A water utility may tell you the tap water is unsafe until it is boiled or treated.

## Making water safe
- **Boiling** is the surest method. Bring it to a **rolling boil for 1 minute** (3 minutes above about 6,500 feet). Let it cool and keep it covered.
- If you cannot boil, plain **unscented household bleach** (5 to 9 percent sodium hypochlorite, with no soap, scent or additives) can disinfect clear water. Follow the label on your bottle or the CDC chart, mix well, wait the full time it says (at least 30 minutes), and the water should smell faintly of chlorine. If it does not, repeat the dose and wait 15 more minutes.
- **Water purification tablets** made for drinking water (chlorine dioxide or iodine) work. Follow the package.
- **Filters** made for camping remove bacteria and protozoa but may not remove viruses or chemicals. Look for a pore size of 0.2 micron or smaller, and read the label.
- **Cloudy water:** let it settle, then pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter before you boil or treat it.

## Storage
- Use **food-grade containers** with tight lids, such as clean plastic bottles or purpose-made water jugs. Do not use containers that held milk or juice (hard to clean) or anything that held chemicals.
- Store in a **cool, dark place,** and replace commercially bottled water by its date. Home-stored tap water is best refreshed about every 6 months.
- **Label** containers with the date and what they hold.

## Rationing
Do not ration drinking water during the first days. Drink what you need, because dehydration makes everything harder. Cut back on washing, and get more water.

> Store treated water in clean, covered containers and label them with the date.

> This is basic awareness, not a substitute for hands-on training. Rules and local conditions vary, so follow official guidance first.

## Plan it before it happens
The best time to fix a water problem is a quiet Saturday. Walk through your home with a notepad and answer four things: where is the main shutoff, how many gallons are stored, what could I treat water with tonight, and who in the house knows where all of this is. Most households find that the answers are "I am not sure", "not many", "bleach, maybe" and "just me". Fix those four and you are ahead of most of your neighbors.

## Count, do not guess
Write the number of people and pets, multiply by one gallon per day, then multiply by the days you want to cover. A family of four with a dog and a cat, for three days, needs about 12 gallons for people plus a little over a gallon for the animals. For two weeks, plan on 56 gallons for the people alone. A single 55 gallon drum or a dozen 5 gallon jugs covers that. Hot weather, illness and hard work raise the numbers, so add a margin.

## Test your treatment method
Treat a bucket of tap water with your filter, your tablets and your boiling pot, one at a time, and taste each. Write down how long each took. Knowing that boiling a gallon takes fifteen minutes and a lot of fuel, or that tablets need thirty minutes, helps you plan the evening instead of discovering it at midnight.

## Common mistakes
- Storing water in thin, cracked or sun-faded containers that leak when moved.
- Forgetting a funnel, a clean scoop, or a clean cup for pouring.
- Assuming a water heater can be drained without turning off its power or gas first, which can ruin the heater.
- Drinking from a flooded tap before the utility says it is safe.
- Rationing drinking water while keeping more for washing.
- Never rotating stored water, so it sits for years.
