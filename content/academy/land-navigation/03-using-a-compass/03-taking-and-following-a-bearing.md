There are two jobs: **read a bearing** to something you can see, and **follow a bearing** to a place you cannot see.

## A. Take a bearing from the ground (to a landmark you can see)
1. Hold the compass flat in front of you at waist height. The direction-of-travel arrow points at the landmark.
2. Turn the housing until the orienting arrow lines up with the needle (red in the shed).
3. Read the bearing at the index line. This is a **magnetic** bearing.

## B. Take a bearing from the map (to a place you will walk to)
1. Lay the compass edge on the map along the line from where you are to where you want to go. The direction-of-travel arrow points toward the target.
2. Turn the housing so the orienting lines run **parallel to the grid lines** (north) on the map. The orienting arrow points north.
3. Read the bearing. This is a **true** bearing.
4. Correct for declination: subtract east, add west (see the earlier lesson).

## Follow the bearing
1. Hold the compass flat. Turn your whole body until the red end of the needle sits inside the orienting arrow.
2. The direction-of-travel arrow now points the way.
3. Pick a clear landmark in that line, such as a tree or rock. Walk to it **without** looking at the compass.
4. At the landmark, check the compass again and pick the next one.

Looking at the compass every few steps wastes time and makes you wander. **Landmarks, not the needle, carry you along the line.**

## No landmark? Use a partner
One person walks ahead until almost out of sight. The other signals left or right until they stand exactly on the bearing. Then the second person walks up to them, and the first goes ahead again. This is called leapfrogging.

## Obstacles
To go around a lake or thicket, turn 90 degrees, count your paces to the side, walk past the obstacle, turn back to the first bearing, and then turn 90 degrees the other way and walk the same number of paces. You are back on line.

> Make a habit: write the bearing and the distance in a notebook before you set off.
