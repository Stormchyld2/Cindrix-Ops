Four ways to put a point on a map using bearings and distances. For all of them you need an oriented map, a compass, and a pencil, and you must convert between magnetic and grid directions (earlier lesson).

## Resection: where am I?
Use when you can see two or three landmarks that are on the map. More landmarks give a better fix.
1. Orient the map.
2. Pick two or three well-defined landmarks that are far apart in direction (not in the same direction), and mark them on the map.
3. Take a compass bearing to the first landmark.
4. Convert it to a grid direction.
5. Work out the **back direction** (add or subtract 180).
6. Draw a line on the map from the landmark back toward you along that back direction.
7. Repeat for the second and third landmark.
8. Where the lines cross is where you are. With three, they often form a small triangle; you are inside it.

Without a compass: orient the map by the land, lay a straight edge (a pencil or ruler) on the map pivoting on the landmark, turn it until it lines up with the real landmark, and draw the line back toward yourself.

## Modified resection: when you are on a road or stream
If you are on a linear feature, one bearing is enough. Take one bearing to one landmark, draw the back-direction line on the map, and you are where that line crosses the road, track or river.

## Intersection: where is that?
Use when you want to locate something you can see but cannot reach: a fire, a damaged building, a smoke plume, a person calling for help.
1. Orient the map and mark where you are.
2. Take a bearing to the unknown object and convert it to a grid direction.
3. Draw a line on the map from your position along that direction.
4. Move to a second known point (or third) and repeat.
5. Where the lines cross is the object.

## Polar plot: go there, or mark it
If you know where you are, the direction and the distance, you can place a point. Start at the known point, draw a line at the direction, measure the distance along it with the map scale, and mark the end.

## The best landmarks
Choose permanent things that show on the map, such as a hilltop, a tower, a bridge or a lake shore. Avoid anything that moves or is easily changed.

## Accuracy
- Use landmarks at roughly 90 degrees apart if you can, and avoid landmarks that are in almost the same direction.
- A one degree error becomes a bigger error at long distance.
- If the lines make a big triangle, check your bearings.

> Resection is your most important recovery tool. If you are unsure of your position, do this before you move.
