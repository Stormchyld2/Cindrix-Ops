A GPS (global positioning system) receiver, whether a handheld unit or a phone, measures your distance from several satellites and works out where you are.

## What it does well
- Gives your position anywhere, day or night, in any weather.
- It needs a view of the sky and at least **four satellites** for a good fix of position and height.
- Handheld units are normally good to within about 10 to 20 meters.
- It can store **waypoints**, then show the direction and distance to them and your speed.
- In navigation mode it tells you how far left or right you have strayed from the line to the waypoint, so you can go round an obstacle without redoing your plan.

## Position formats
A receiver can show your position several ways. Choose the one your map uses.
- **Degrees, minutes, seconds** (DMS): such as 32 degrees 15 minutes 30 seconds north.
- **Degrees and decimal minutes** (DM).
- **UTM** (northing and easting in meters).
- **Military grid (MGRS)**, with grid zone and square letters.
If you set the wrong one, the numbers will not match your map.

## Limits
- It is blocked by dense trees, canyons, buildings and the inside of vehicles, and weak under heavy cover.
- It runs on batteries or a phone battery.
- It shows a straight line to a waypoint, not whether there is a cliff or river in between.
- It can be jammed or switched off, or a device can fail or be lost.

## Rules for using it safely
1. Always carry a paper map and a compass too, and know how to use them.
2. Mark your key places (home, meeting places, water, vehicle) as waypoints **before** you need them, and write the grid references on paper.
3. Keep spare batteries, and turn off what you do not need.
4. Check it against the land and the map. If it disagrees with what you see, believe the land.
5. Check it for a minute or two with a clear sky when you start so it has a good fix.

> GPS tells you where you are. A map tells you what is around. A compass tells you which way to go. Carry all three.
