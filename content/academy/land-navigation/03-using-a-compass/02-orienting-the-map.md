To **orient** a map means to turn it so that the map's north is the same as north on the ground. When it is oriented, everything on the map lies in the same direction as the real thing. A hill on the left of the map is on your left.

## With a compass
1. Lay the compass on the map with the long edge of the baseplate along a north-south grid line, direction-of-travel arrow pointing to the top of the map.
2. Turn the housing so the orienting arrow points to north on the map.
3. Correct for declination. For east declination, turn the housing clockwise by that amount (a 10 degree east declination puts 350 at the index line). For west, turn it counter-clockwise. Or use the compass's own adjustment screw.
4. Hold the compass flat and turn the **map and compass together** until the red end of the needle sits inside the orienting arrow.
5. The map is now oriented. Put a stone on it so it does not move.

## Without a compass
- Match what you see: turn the map until a road, stream or ridge that you can see lies in the same line as it does on the map.
- Use the sun or the stars (see the earlier lesson) to find north, then turn the map's north to that.
- Look at two or three landmarks. Turn the map until they all line up.

## Why do it?
Oriented maps stop the common mistake of turning the wrong way at a junction. They also let you find landmarks quickly: look along the map to a feature, then look up and straight ahead.

## Common mistakes
- Not correcting for declination.
- Orienting near a car, a fence or a power line.
- Turning only the compass and not the map with it.

> When you reach a fork in the path, orient the map first. Then decide.
