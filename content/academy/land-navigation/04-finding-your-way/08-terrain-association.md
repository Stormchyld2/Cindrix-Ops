This is how most people find their way with a map: match what the map says to what you see, and move from feature to feature. It is more forgiving of mistakes than dead reckoning because you always compare the map and the ground, so errors are noticed early.

## How to do it
1. **Orient the map** so north on the map is north on the ground (by compass or by the land).
2. Know roughly where you are before you start.
3. Look at the map and say what you expect to see ahead: a stream, then a rise, then a clearing.
4. Check them off as you reach them.
5. If the ground and the map disagree, stop and work out why before you go on.

## What to match
- **Hills, valleys, ridges, saddles and depressions:** match the shapes from the contour lines (the contour lessons).
- **Slope:** steep or gentle, uphill or downhill, convex or concave.
- **Water:** the shape and size of lakes, which way streams run.
- **Man-made features:** roads, buildings, bridges, fences, power lines, and which direction they run.
- **Vegetation:** forest and clearings. Careful: these change fastest and the map may be old.

## Seasons change the view
- **Winter:** bare trees and snow make the shape of the land clear.
- **Spring and summer:** leaves hide hills and valleys.
- **Autumn:** colors and leaf fall change how things look.
- **Wet season:** streams and ponds grow. **Drought:** water levels fall.
Fire, flood and logging can change things overnight.

## Handrails, catching features and attack points
- **Handrail:** a long feature that runs along your way, such as a road, stream, power line or ridge. Keep it on one side and you need no precise compass work.
- **Catching feature:** a landmark just after your turn-off, or just past your target, that tells you when you've gone too far.
- **Attack point:** an easy-to-find last landmark close to your goal (500 meters or less is ideal). From there, switch to careful compass and pace counting for the final short leg. That's "point navigation".

## Good checkpoints
- Best: long features that cross your route (a river, a paved road, a railway, a ridge, a power line).
- Next: places where the ground rises or falls by at least two contour lines (a hill, spur, draw or depression).
- Least reliable: buildings and vegetation, which change.
- Put a checkpoint wherever you must change direction.

## Choose a sensible route
- Following the valley floor, or travelling just below (not on) a ridge crest, is easy walking with good checkpoints.
- Go with the land. Do not fight it.
- Avoid skylining yourself on a bare crest, and use cover when you need to stay unseen.
- A longer, flatter route can be quicker than a short, steep one.

> The best navigators combine both methods: terrain association for the general route and dead reckoning for the final short leg.

## Check yourself
? What does it mean to orient the map?
+ Turn it so its north matches north on the ground
- Fold it
- Draw a line on it
> Then the map matches what you see.
? What is an attack point?
+ An easy-to-find landmark near the goal, where careful compass work starts
- The goal itself
- A hill you must climb
> From there you do a short precise leg.
? Which checkpoints are most reliable?
+ Long features that cross your route
- Buildings and trees
- Faint tracks
> They are hard to miss and don't change often.
