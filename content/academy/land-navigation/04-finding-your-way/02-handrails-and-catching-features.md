Even a good navigator does not walk on a bearing for miles. Real travel uses the landscape to stay on track and to catch small errors.

## Handrails
A **handrail** is a long line feature that you can keep beside you: a stream, a fence, a power line, a ridge, the edge of a lake, a road. Following a handrail keeps you from wandering and lets you relax your attention. In fog or at night it can be the only safe way to travel.

## Backstops (catching features)
A **backstop** is a large feature across your path that tells you "you have gone too far": a road, a river, a railway. Pick one beyond your goal. If you reach it, turn around.

## Attack points
Do not aim at a small target from far away. First go to a **large, easy-to-find feature** near it (an **attack point**), such as a bridge or a stream junction. Then take a short, careful bearing from there to the small target.

## Aiming off
You are heading for a spot on a long line feature, such as a bridge on a river. If you aim exactly at it, you will arrive on the river and not know whether to turn left or right. **Aim off** instead: aim deliberately to one side, for example 10 degrees to the left. When you hit the river, you know that the bridge is to your right.

## Collecting features
Tick off landmarks as you pass them: "Stream crossing. Hill on the left. Fence. Now the road." If the next feature does not come when you expect it, stop. Do not walk on hoping.

## Travel rules
- Stay on a path or ridge when you can. Cross-country is slow.
- Do not cut across steep ground or a frozen lake only to save time.
- Keep the group together and count heads at every stop.

> If two features do not match the map, you are probably not where you think you are. Stop and check.
