The same skills work everywhere, but each kind of country has its tricks.

## Desert
- Maps may be poor, and contour intervals large, so small features are missed.
- Distant features seem close. In clear air and bright sun, people **underestimate distances by 2 or 3 times**.
- Landmarks seen from far away give a false sense of security. You can be several kilometers out. Confirm with a resection or a close landmark.
- In sandy dunes or in dust storms, travel by dead reckoning.
- Travel at night or at the cool ends of the day, using the stars. The wind often arranges dunes in a pattern that shows direction.
- Water, animals and navigation are linked. Doves and pigeons drink in the evening and fly heavily back from water.
- Carry more water than you think you need.

## Mountains
- The weather changes fast. It is colder and wetter than at the foot, and fog is frequent.
- The contour interval is often large. Small features and dead ground get missed.
- Roads and trails are usually the best routes.
- **Aspect of slope:** take a compass bearing straight down the slope you're on (it crosses the contour lines at about 90 degrees). Match this against the map to help find your place when you can't see.
- An **altimeter** (barometer) lets you fix your position from one direction and one height reading.

## Jungle and thick forest
- Very little terrain to see. Use the compass and an accurate pace count.
- The most common mistake is to **overestimate** distance. As a rough guide, the most you can travel in an hour in daylight is: rain forest up to 1,000 m, deciduous forest 500 m, secondary growth 100 to 500 m, tall grass 500 m, swamp 100 to 300 m, dry rice fields 2,000 m, trails up to 3,000 m.
- Make a **personal pace table**: your pace count per 100 meters in each kind of ground.
- A lost person can follow any stream downstream to eventually reach people, though not everyone found is friendly.
- Swamps: study the map and ground before going in. Dead reckoning is the usual method.

## Snow and cold
- Movement can take up to **five times as long** as in warm weather.
- Without leaves, hills and valleys are easy to read. But on flat snow in bright light, you lose contrast, and the land is hard to read.
- Frozen lakes and swamps can be crossed, but check ice carefully.
- Moonlit snow makes night travel easier.
- It is easy to take shortcuts on a task when very cold. Don't.

## Towns and cities
- Roads, bridges, rail lines and buildings matter more than land shape. The map is probably out of date, so use a recent street map or sketch.
- Write your route as a series of steps: "three blocks north, left on the wide road to the river bridge, then right along the west bank."
- Build a mental picture of the whole area, so you can use other routes if you miss a turn.
- Use resection whenever you can see two known features.

> The four steps and both methods work everywhere. The special conditions change the odds, not the principle.
