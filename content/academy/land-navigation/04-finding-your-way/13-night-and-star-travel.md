The methods are the same at night, but everything is slower and harder.

## Preparing
- Study the map in daylight and decide the terrain, the directions and distances beforehand.
- Pick checkpoints that you can feel or hear: a stream, a road, a fence line, a steep change of slope.
- **Walk the route in daylight first** if you can.
- Use only reliable features. Colors vanish and things become gray silhouettes.

## Moving
- Dead reckoning is the main method. Two people with compasses cross-check each other. One walks a few steps ahead so you can keep them on the line.
- **Your steps are shorter in the dark**, so the pace count is different. Measure your night pace on a 100 meter course.
- Preset the bezel or use the click method.
- Set the first direction while there is still light.

## Light
Let your eyes adapt, and avoid looking at bright lights. Look slightly to the side of a dim object to see it better. Moon and starlight on snow can make it almost like day.

## The stars (Northern Hemisphere)
- The **North Star (Polaris)** is within 1 degree of true north. It is the end star of the handle of the Little Dipper.
- Find it using the two "pointer" stars at the end of the Big Dipper's bowl. Extend the line through them about **five times** the gap between them.
- A star near the northern horizon stays useful about half an hour. Walking south, check every 15 minutes. Walking east or west, change guide stars when it gets too high or sets.
- The farther north you are, the higher the North Star. Above about 70 degrees north, it's too high to be useful.
- In the Southern Hemisphere, use the **Southern Cross** and reverse the north and south rules.

## The moon
A bright moon casts a shadow. Use the **shadow stick method** with the moon instead of the sun.

## When you cannot see
In fog, snow or darkness, move with short legs and tight checks. Or stay put. Travelling lost in bad weather is usually worse than waiting.

> It is far easier to stay found than to find yourself again.
