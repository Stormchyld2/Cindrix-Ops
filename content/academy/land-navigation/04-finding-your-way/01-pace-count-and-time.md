Knowing **how far** you have gone is as important as knowing which way. Without a map scale on the ground, you use your own steps and the clock.

## Pace count
A **pace** here means every time your **same foot** touches the ground (a double step). Most adults take about 60 to 70 of these in 100 meters on flat ground, but **you must measure your own**.

1. Mark out a measured 100 meters (or 100 yards) on a flat path.
2. Walk it in your normal stride and count. Do it three times and take the average.
3. Write the number on your compass or map case.

Your count changes with the ground:
- **Uphill:** more paces for the same distance.
- **Downhill, with a load, in sand, snow, mud or brush:** usually more as well.
Measure your count on different ground and write them down.

## Keeping count
- Use a string of **pace beads**: nine beads on the lower part and four on the upper. Slide one lower bead for every 100 meters. After nine, slide one upper bead for each kilometer and reset the lower ones.
- Or put small stones in a pocket, or make a knot in a cord.
- If you lose count, say the number out loud, or begin again from the last known landmark.

## Time
A common rule (Naismith's rule) for a fit walker with a light pack on a decent path:
- **5 kilometers (3 miles) an hour** on level ground, plus
- **1 extra hour for every 600 meters (2,000 feet) of climbing.**

Add more time for heavy loads, steep or rough ground, snow, a group with children, and rests. Many people do only half this speed with a heavy pack.

## Daylight
Work out when you must stop. Plan to arrive or to be camped well before dark. In a survival setting, **moving less and sheltering early** is often wiser than a long march.

> A rough plan that you follow is better than a fine plan you abandon.
