The principles are the same as on foot. The big difference is speed: a 15-minute drive covers what an hour on foot would.

## A navigator helps
If you can, have one person drive and another navigate. The navigator gathers maps and pencils beforehand, follows the route, records directions and distances, and calls out changes of direction in good time.

## Estimating distance
- **Odometer.** Note the reading at the start and at checkpoints. 0.1 mile is about 160 meters, and 1 mile is about 1.6 km.
- To change kilometers to miles, multiply by 0.62. To change miles to kilometers, divide by 0.62.
- Map distance is flat distance. For travel across country, **add 20 percent** to the map distance.

## Check the slope
A quick rule on a map with a 10 m (about 20 ft) interval: each contour line crossed in 100 meters of map is about a **10 percent slope**. Two lines is 20 percent. If four lines are crossed in 100 meters, find another way. For a different interval, adjust (one line in 100 meters with a 20 m interval is 20 percent).

Side slopes are worse than climbs. A vehicle that tips or slides sideways is hard to recover. If you cross one, go slowly with no turns.

## Weather and ground
Rain, snow and mud ruin off-road travel. After rain, avoid low, wet ground. A stuck vehicle is a bigger problem than a long detour.

## Choosing the route
- Move from feature to feature, like a city driver using junctions and shops.
- Pick checkpoints that you can recognize **at your speed and in your light**. At speed you will miss small changes of height. Look for changes of at least two contour lines.
- Good checkpoints are streams, rivers, paved roads, railways, ridges and valleys.
- In forest, checkpoints about every 1,000 meters. In open country, up to about 5,000 meters.
- Use rough compass directions (north, northeast) for each leg, and note where the sun is.

## Make a route logbook
Break the journey into legs. For each leg write the direction, distance and a note about something easy to identify where you turn. A small notebook on the dashboard works well.

## Compass in a vehicle
A vehicle disturbs the compass. To set a heading:
1. Get out and walk at least **18 meters** away from the vehicle.
2. Set your direction on the compass and pick a steering mark on that line.
3. Get in and drive to the steering mark in as straight a line as you can.
4. At the mark, repeat.

On flat, featureless ground you can find out how much the vehicle shifts the compass. Stand 18 meters in front, face the vehicle and read the direction to it. Reverse it by 180 to get the heading of travel. Get in, drive straight forward, read the compass inside the car, and note the difference. If it reads 8 degrees less than outside, keep your compass reading 8 degrees less than you want.

> Navigating by vehicle has the same four steps. Use terrain association first, and dead reckoning across flat open ground.
