To tell someone exactly where you are, or to find a place from a message, you need a shared way to name a spot. Maps have two.

## Grid references
Many topographic maps have a square grid printed on them. The lines are numbered along the edges.
- Always read **right, then up**. "Along the corridor, then up the stairs."
- A **four-figure** reference gives the grid square. For example, **1234**: east line 12, north line 34. The location is the square whose bottom-left corner is that crossing.
- A **six-figure** reference divides each square into tenths. Read the first two digits along (12), then a third digit for how many tenths further (say 5), and the same for up (34 and 7): **125347**. On many maps the squares are 1 kilometer wide, so six figures locate you to within **100 meters**.

Use a small plastic grid ruler or a piece of paper marked in tenths to read tenths.

## Latitude and longitude
A global system of angles.
- **Latitude** measures north or south of the equator (0 to 90 degrees). Lines run east-west.
- **Longitude** measures east or west of the prime meridian (0 to 180). Lines run north-south.
- Written as degrees, minutes and seconds (1 degree = 60 minutes; 1 minute = 60 seconds) or as decimal degrees.
- One minute of latitude is about one nautical mile (1.85 km).

Say latitude first, then longitude, and give the letters N, S, E and W so there is no confusion.

## Which to use
Use whichever system the map has, and make sure the other person uses the same one. Write the grid system and map name along with the numbers, for example "map sheet name, six-figure grid 125347".

## Saying it over the radio
Read digit by digit, and repeat it back: "grid one two five three four seven, I say again..."

> If you have to leave a note, write the grid reference, the date and time, the direction you went and who is with you.
