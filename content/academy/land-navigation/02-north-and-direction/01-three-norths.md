There are **three norths**, and they are not the same. This is the most common reason for navigation mistakes.

## True north
The direction to the North Pole, where all the lines of longitude meet. The North Star points very close to it.

## Grid north
The direction of the vertical grid lines printed on a map. It is nearly the same as true north, and the difference is small on most maps.

## Magnetic north
The place your compass needle points. It is not at the North Pole. It wanders slowly over the years.

## Declination
**Declination** is the angle between true north and magnetic north where you are.
- **East declination:** magnetic north lies to the east of true north.
- **West declination:** magnetic north lies to the west of true north.

The size depends on where you are. In some places it is near zero. In others it is more than 15 degrees. A 10 degree error will put you about 175 meters (560 feet) off after a kilometer (0.6 mile) of walking.

## Where to find it
The margin of a topographic map shows the declination and the year it was measured. It changes slowly, so an old map is a little off. If you have no map, any recent atlas, weather service source or a stored table for your region will give a good number. Write the number for your home area on the back of your compass with tape.

## Converting between map and compass
Maps are drawn to true (or grid) north. Compasses read magnetic north.
- **Map bearing to compass bearing:** *subtract* an east declination and *add* a west declination.
- **Compass bearing to map bearing:** *add* an east declination and *subtract* a west declination.

Example: the declination is 10 degrees east. A bearing you measure on the map is 90 degrees. To set it on your compass, use 80 degrees.

> Some compasses have a built-in declination adjustment. Set it once for your area and the compass does the work for you.
