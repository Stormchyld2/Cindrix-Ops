An aerial photograph, or a satellite picture on a screen or printed from one, shows the ground as it is now. A map may be years old.

## What a picture does better than a map
- It is current: new roads, cleared land, flooding, new buildings.
- It shows things a map never draws.

## What a map does better
- Ground features are named and drawn with symbols.
- It has a scale, a north arrow and a grid.
- It shows the height and shape of the land.
- A picture is hard to read in poor light and takes more practice.

Use both. The picture tells you what has changed, the map tells you what the land is.

## Straight down or at an angle
- **Vertical** (straight down): distances and directions come close to a map's if the land is flat. You cannot see the height of the ground easily.
- **Oblique** (at an angle): looks more familiar, like the view from a high building, but you cannot measure distances or directions on it because the scale changes across the picture.

## Five clues to identify things
No single clue is enough. Use all five.
1. **Size.** Compare with something you know. Small buildings are homes and big ones are shops or factories.
2. **Shape.** Straight or smoothly curved lines are made by people (roads, rails, canals, buildings). Natural features are irregular.
3. **Shadow.** The shadow gives the side view. A tower from above is a dot, but its shadow shows its shape.
4. **Tone and texture.** Smooth things (pavement) look smooth and even. Plowed fields and marshes look rough and grainy. Water looks light or dark depending on the sun.
5. **Surroundings.** A large building beside railway tracks is probably a warehouse. A school has playing fields.

## Look at it the right way up
Turn the picture so that **shadows point toward you**. Then hills look like hills. Upside down, a quarry looks like a hill.

## Finding the scale of a picture
Pick two points you can find on both the picture and a map, measure both, and divide: **scale = picture distance / ground distance**. Choose points through the middle of the picture. The scale is only approximate and changes with the height of the ground.

## Finding north on a picture
Match it to the map by features, then draw a north line across it. If no map: in the northern half of the world the sun moves about 15 degrees an hour, so shadows turn about 15 degrees an hour. At noon the shadow points north. Before noon, north is to the right of the shadow, and after noon, to the left. Only reliable within three hours of noon.

> Screens can zoom and show a position, but a map and compass still work with no power.

## Check yourself
? Why use an aerial picture with a map?
+ The picture shows current changes and the map shows names and heights
- The picture has a bigger scale
- They always match
> Each covers the other's weakness.
? How should you turn an aerial photo to see the shapes correctly?
+ So shadows point toward you
- So north is down
- So the sun is behind the picture
> Otherwise hills and holes can look reversed.
? Why can't you measure distance on an oblique photo?
+ The scale changes across the picture
- It has no ground in it
- The camera was too close
> Far parts are smaller than near parts.
