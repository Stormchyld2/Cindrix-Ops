**Elevation** is how high a point is above mean sea level (the middle point between high and low tide). **Relief** is the shape of the land.

## Ways maps show relief
- **Contour lines.** The main way on topographic maps.
- **Layer tinting.** Bands of color for ranges of height (check the legend). It gives the range, not the exact height.
- **Shaded relief.** One side of hills and ridges is shaded as if lit from a corner. Darker means steeper.
- **Form lines.** Dashed lines that give only a general idea. They have no numbers.
- **Hachures.** Short broken lines for rocky areas, often on small-scale maps.

## Kinds of contour lines
- **Index lines.** Every fifth line is heavier and numbered with its height.
- **Intermediate lines.** The finer lines between the index lines (normally four between).
- **Supplementary lines.** Dashed lines added on flat land to show changes of at least half the contour interval.

## Contour interval
The **contour interval** (printed in the margin) is the height difference between neighbouring lines. Read the units: feet, meters or yards.

## Find the height of a point
1. Find the contour interval and its unit.
2. Find the nearest numbered index line.
3. Count lines from it to your point. Going uphill, **add** the interval for each line. Going downhill, **subtract**.

Example: index line 500 m, interval 20 m. A point on the second line above it is 540 m. A point on the line just below the 600 m index line is 580 m.

## In-between points
- Less than a quarter of the way to the next line: same as the line below.
- Between a quarter and three quarters: add **half** the interval.
- More than three quarters: count it as the next line.

## Hilltops and holes
- **Top of a hill** (inside the last closed line): add **half** the interval to that last line. A 600 m line and a 20 m interval give about 610 m.
- **Bottom of a depression:** subtract half the interval from the lowest line. Depression lines have **tick marks pointing downhill**.

## Spot heights
- **Bench marks** (a black X with BM and a number) are the most accurate.
- **Spot elevations** appear at road junctions and hilltops. Black numbers are checked, brown are not.

## Three contour rules
1. Every point on one line is at the same height.
2. Every line closes on itself somewhere, even if beyond the map.
3. Lines never cross, except at an overhanging cliff, and they merge on a cliff.

> Lines close together mean steep. Lines far apart mean gentle.
