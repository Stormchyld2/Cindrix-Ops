A tourniquet is a strap that squeezes an arm or leg tight enough to stop blood flowing past it. Used correctly on life-threatening limb bleeding it saves lives. Modern research shows that using one when it is needed causes far less harm than people fear.

## When to use one
- Heavy bleeding from an **arm or leg** that direct pressure does not control.
- A limb that is badly damaged or cut off.
- You cannot stay with the wound, for example you must move the person or help others.

## How to put on a commercial tourniquet
1. Slide it over the limb or wrap it on and put it **two to three finger widths above the wound**, never on a joint. If you cannot tell where, put it **high and tight** high on the limb.
2. Pull the strap tight and fasten it.
3. **Turn the rod (windlass)** until the bleeding **stops**. This will hurt a lot.
4. Lock the rod in its clip.
5. **Write the time** on the strap or the person's forehead.
6. Check that the bleeding has really stopped. If it has not, tighten more, or put a **second tourniquet** just above the first.

## Rules
- Do **not** loosen it. Medical staff will take it off.
- Do not cover it. Everyone should be able to see it.
- Do not use a thin cord or wire.
- Buy a tested make (the military-approved designs). Cheap copies can break.

> A tourniquet that is not tight enough can make bleeding worse. If you can still feel a pulse below it or the wound keeps bleeding, turn it tighter.

## Going deeper: modern thinking on tourniquets
In the past, people feared tourniquets would cost a limb. Evidence from recent wars and civilian shootings shows that **a tourniquet applied properly saves lives, and limbs are rarely lost to a tourniquet applied for a couple of hours.** The Stop the Bleed campaign now teaches using one early for severe limb bleeding.

## Choosing and storing
- Buy **commercial tourniquets** from reputable makers, such as models that are approved by the military's committee on tactical casualty care. Avoid unbranded copies, which can break.
- Keep one on your body or in an easy place, in your bag, car and kit. Practice with a trainer, not your only real one.
- Store out of sun and heat, which weaken plastic parts.

## Using it
1. Place it **2 to 3 inches above the wound** on the limb, not on a joint. If you cannot find the wound, place it as high as you can, "high and tight."
2. Pull the strap tight, then twist the rod (windlass) until the bleeding **stops** and any distal pulse disappears.
3. Lock the rod in its clip, and secure the strap.
4. **Write the time** on the tourniquet or on the person's forehead.
5. Do **not** loosen or remove it. Leave that for medical staff.
6. If bleeding continues, add a second tourniquet just above the first.
7. Expect it to hurt. That is normal, and it means the pressure is working.

## Improvised tourniquets
A wide strip of cloth (at least about 1.5 inches) and a sturdy stick can work, but they are far less reliable. Do not use rope, wire or a thin strap. Tighten until bleeding stops. Improvised ones fail often, so use a real one if you can.

## After it is on
Keep the person warm, watch for shock and get them to care. A tourniquet is meant to be a bridge to hospital care.

+ The time it was put on
- Your name only
- The brand
- The weather
