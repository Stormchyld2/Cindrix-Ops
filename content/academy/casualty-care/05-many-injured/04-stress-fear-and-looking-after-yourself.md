Your body reacts strongly to an emergency: racing heart, shaking hands, narrowed vision, a blank mind. This is normal. You can train yourself to work with it.

## Calm your body
- **Box breathing:** breathe in for four counts, hold for four, out for four, hold for four. Repeat four times.
- Slow your speech down. Lower your voice.
- Do the **next small step**. Do not think about the whole problem.

## Train for it
- Practise the drills until they feel automatic. People under stress do what they have practised.
- Practise with noise and time pressure, safely.

## Afterwards
It is common to feel shaky, tired, sad, angry or numb afterwards. It is also common to replay the event.
- **Talk about it** with someone you trust, soon.
- Eat, drink, rest and move.
- Write down what happened while you remember it clearly.
- If the feelings are strong and do not ease after a few weeks, or you have trouble sleeping, are afraid all the time, or feel hopeless, **speak to a doctor or counsellor**. In the United States you can call or text **988** at any time.

## Care for the team
Check on everyone who helped. Children who watched need extra care and honest simple words.

> Helping in a crisis can leave a mark. Asking for help afterwards is a sign of strength.

## Going deeper: what stress does
In a crisis your heart pounds, hands shake, vision narrows, hearing may fade and thoughts race. This is the body's alarm. In small amounts it sharpens you. In large amounts it freezes you or makes you rush.

## Tools to stay steady
- **Box breathing:** breathe in for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four. Repeat a few times.
- **Slow your speech and your movements.** Others follow your pace.
- **Break the task into one next step.** "Stop the bleeding. Then airway."
- **Use checklists and routines** such as MARCH. A habit carries you when thinking is hard.
- **Self-talk:** a short phrase such as "slow is smooth, smooth is fast."
- **Train under stress,** so your brain has met it before.

## Looking after yourself
Eat and drink, rest when you can, keep warm and tell someone if you feel overwhelmed. Do not drink alcohol to cope. Take breaks between tasks.

## After the event
It is normal to feel shaky, sad, angry, numb, to have trouble sleeping or replay the event. Most of this eases over days or weeks.
- Talk to someone you trust.
- Keep routines: food, sleep, exercise.
- Avoid blaming yourself. You did what you could with what you had.
- If distress lasts more than a few weeks, interferes with life, or you feel hopeless, reach out to a doctor or counselor. In the US you can call or text 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

## Care for the team
Hold a debrief within a day or two. Thank each other, and share the load. Watch each other for signs of struggle.
