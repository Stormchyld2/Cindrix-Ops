Many injured, many bystanders, and everyone shouting. A calm leader changes everything.

## One leader
Pick one person to make the decisions. If you are first on scene, you are the leader until someone better prepared takes over, and then you tell them what you know.

## Set up the scene
1. **Make it safe.** Move people away from danger.
2. **Call for help** with clear facts: where, what happened, how many, what you need.
3. **Make a casualty area.** A safe flat place away from danger. Put red people closest to where help will come.
4. **Use bystanders.** Give each a simple job: hold pressure, fetch blankets, keep a record, guide the ambulance, keep crowds back.
5. **Keep a list.** Names (if known), injuries, treatment and times.

## Keep control
- Say your plan out loud in short sentences.
- Do not let anyone leave without telling the leader.
- Ask everyone to stay calm, and give frightened people a job.

## Hand over
When emergency crews arrive, give a short summary: how many people, who is red, what has been done. Then do what they tell you.

## Look after the helpers
Rotate tasks so no one gets worn out. Make sure helpers drink, eat and rest.

> Do not try to do everything yourself. Delegating is part of the job.

## Going deeper: command and control
A chaotic scene needs one calm leader and a few clear jobs. Emergency services use the Incident Command System (ICS). You can borrow its ideas.

## Set up
- **Command:** the leader. Stand back where you can see everything.
- **Safety:** watches hazards.
- **Triage and treatment:** sort people into colored areas: red, yellow, green.
- **Transport:** plans moves, with vehicles and routes.
- **Communications and records.**
Assign people to jobs, and name a deputy.

## Zones
Set up a safe area upwind and uphill, away from hazards. Create a treatment area, a landing zone for helicopters or ambulances, and a route that stays open. Keep curious onlookers out.

## Control the crowd
Give bystanders jobs: carrying, fetching water, comforting, holding a light. People who are busy are calmer. Send away those who cannot help.

## Communicate
Use short, plain words, no codes. Report to dispatch: location, number of casualties by color, hazards and what you need. Update every so often.

## Handover
When professionals arrive, brief the incoming leader and hand over, then offer help.

## Care for helpers
Watch for exhaustion, stress and injury among helpers. Rotate jobs, give breaks, food, water and warmth. Keep track of who is on the scene.

## Keep records
Log names, injuries, treatment and destination.

## Check yourself
? Who makes decisions on a crowded scene?
+ One leader
- Everyone together
- The loudest person
- The oldest person always
> One clear voice avoids confusion.
? What should you do with a bystander who wants to help?
+ Give them a simple clear job
- Send them away
- Ask them to treat the worst patient
- Ask them to wait
> Simple jobs use their energy and keep them safe.
? What do you tell the crews when they arrive?
+ A short summary: numbers, who is red, what has been done
- Nothing, they will see
- Your whole story
- Only your name
> A short summary lets them act fast.
