Two or three people working together help far more than one. A little planning beforehand makes it smooth.

## Roles
- **Leader.** Looks at the whole scene, makes decisions, keeps people calm. Does not stay on one task.
- **Treater.** Does the hands-on care: stops bleeding, checks the airway.
- **Helper.** Fetches the kit, holds light, writes down times, keeps the casualty warm, calls for help.
- **Watcher.** Keeps an eye on the surroundings for danger.
In a small family, one person may do two jobs. Decide who does what beforehand.

## Clear calls
Short, plain words work best. Repeat back what you hear.
- "Tourniquet on, left leg, 14:32."
- "Airway open, breathing."
- "Need more gauze."

## Handover
When help arrives, give a short report. A handy order is **MIST**:
- **M**echanism: what happened.
- **I**njuries: what you found.
- **S**igns: how they are now, such as breathing and awareness.
- **T**reatment: what you did and when.
Write the same facts on a card or a note and send it with the person.

## Keep records
Note the time of each action and any change. A phone with the clock on screen works.

> Stay calm and speak slowly. A steady voice spreads calm. A panicked voice spreads panic.

## Going deeper: why teams matter
In a crisis, one person cannot call, treat, comfort and keep the scene safe. A small, clear team does better and tires less.

## Roles in a small team
- **Team lead:** looks at the whole scene, makes decisions, does not do the hands-on work, and keeps track of time.
- **Medic/primary care:** treats the most urgent problem.
- **Assistant:** hands over supplies, helps move, holds the head, takes notes.
- **Communicator:** calls for help, meets the ambulance, passes messages, writes the record.
- **Safety:** watches hazards, traffic and the crowd.
Roles change as the situation changes. With two people, split them as lead and treater.

## Closed-loop communication
Give a clear order: "Sam, call 911 now." The person repeats: "Calling 911." Then reports back: "911 called, ambulance on the way, ten minutes." This prevents mistakes and missed jobs.

## Talk about what you see
Name the problem out loud: "Heavy bleeding, left thigh." It helps the team and lets others correct mistakes.

## Handover
When medical staff arrive, hand over using a simple format: who, what happened, injuries found, treatment given with times, current condition and allergies or medicines if known.

## Records
Keep a card with times: when the tourniquet went on, when you checked breathing, changes in the person's state. Give it to the responders.

## Debrief
Afterward, talk through what went well and what to improve, without blame.
