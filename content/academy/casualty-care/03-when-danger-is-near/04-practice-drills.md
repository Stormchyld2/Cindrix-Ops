Reading is not enough. You will do these tasks badly the first time, so do them for the first time in your living room.

## Safety rules for practice
- Use a **training tourniquet** or put a real one on a **padded training limb or a rolled towel**. Never leave a real tourniquet tight on a person for more than a few seconds.
- Never practice wound packing on a real wound.
- Use a **timer** and keep it fun, not scary.

## Drill 1: tourniquet on yourself
Put a tourniquet on your own arm or leg, high and tight, **one-handed**, in under a minute. Repeat with the other limb.

## Drill 2: tourniquet on a partner
Put one on a practice limb or partner's thigh for a few seconds (no longer). Say the time out loud and write it down.

## Drill 3: packing
Make a training wound from a thick towel, with a pocket in it. Pack it with gauze and hold pressure for the full three minutes. Practice with a shirt you can cut.

## Drill 4: the full sequence
One person plays the injured. The other does: safety check, find bleeding, stop it, check airway and breathing, check for shock, cover to keep warm, call for help and write a handover. Time it.

## Repeat
Do the drills every few months, with everyone in the house, including teenagers. Skills fade without practice.

> A drill you can do half-asleep in the dark is a drill you can do under stress. Practice until it feels boring.

## Going deeper: how to train so it sticks
Skills fade quickly. Short, repeated practice does more than one long class.

## Principles
- **Practice the way you would do it:** wearing gloves, in poor light, with noise and a timer.
- **Start slow, then speed up.** Accuracy first.
- **Practice with a partner,** swapping roles.
- **Add stress gradually:** a second person shouting, fake blood, a clock.
- **Repeat every few months.** Put the dates on the calendar.

## Standards to aim for
- A tourniquet on yourself, one-handed, in under a minute.
- A tourniquet on a partner in under 30 seconds.
- Packing a wound and holding pressure for the full time.
- Naming the MARCH steps from memory.

## Equipment
Use a training tourniquet, so your good one is kept for real use. A sponge, a stuffed sleeve or a purpose-made trainer allows packing practice. Training materials are cheap compared with a mistake.

## Safety in drills
Do not tighten a real tourniquet on a person for long, because it hurts and can cause injury. Keep drills short. No real injury or blood. Stop at once if anyone feels unwell.

## Keep a log
Record who practiced what, the times and what needs work. Praise improvement.

## Take a class
Take an in-person Stop the Bleed, American Red Cross or similar first aid course, where an instructor can correct your technique. Self-study cannot replace hands-on teaching.
