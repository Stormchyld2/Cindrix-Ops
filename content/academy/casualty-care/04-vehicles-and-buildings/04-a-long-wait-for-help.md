In a large disaster or far from a road, help can take hours. Your care must go on.

## Keep checking
Every few minutes, then less often as the person becomes stable:
- Is the bleeding still controlled? Look for blood soaking through.
- Check the tourniquet is still tight and the time is written down.
- Is their breathing steady? Their colour? Are they awake and talking sensibly?
- Is the person warm and dry?

## Care while you wait
- Keep them **warm**: insulate from the ground, cover the head, replace damp covers.
- Offer **small sips of water** only if they are fully awake, able to swallow and have **no belly injury**. Otherwise give nothing.
- Keep talking to them. Calm words help.
- Ease pain with position and padding. **Do not give medicines** unless a doctor has told you what to give. Follow the label.

## Keep a record
Write down the time of each check, what you saw and what you did. Hand it to the medical team.

## Help yourself
- Rest when you can, drink, and eat.
- Ask for help and take turns.

## Signalling
Make yourself easy to find: a bright cloth, a light at night, a person at the road to wave down help, and your location written down in simple terms.

> The people who survive a long wait are usually the ones whose helpers keep checking, keep them warm and never stop talking.

## Check yourself
? Who may be given sips of water while waiting for help?
+ A person who is awake, can swallow and has no belly injury
- Anyone who is thirsty
- An unconscious person
- A person with a belly injury
> Fluids are risky if the person might need surgery or cannot swallow safely.
? What should you do about pain?
+ Use position and padding, and do not give medicines unless a doctor has said what to give
- Give whatever is in the cabinet
- Ignore it
- Give alcohol
> Medicines should follow the label and a doctor's advice.
? How often should you re-check the person?
+ Every few minutes at first, then regularly
- Once only
- Only when they ask
- Every day
> Their condition can change quickly.
