[video] Map and compass basics

Phones fail and batteries die. A paper map and a compass work in the rain, in the cold and under trees. This lesson gives the basics. The Land Navigation course goes deeper.

## The map
- **Scale:** how map distance relates to ground distance. On a 1:24,000 map, 1 inch equals 2,000 feet (about 0.38 mile).
- **North:** the top of the map is usually true north. A **declination** diagram shows the difference between **true north** and **magnetic north.** Note it.
- **Contour lines:** each line connects points of equal elevation. **Close together** means steep. **Far apart** means gentle. A closed circle is a hilltop. **V shapes** pointing uphill show valleys and streams.
- **Symbols:** blue is water, green is vegetation, black is roads and buildings, brown is contours. Read the legend.
- **Grid and coordinates:** latitude and longitude, or UTM grid.
- **Date:** check how old the map is.

## The compass
- **Baseplate compass** parts: baseplate, rotating bezel (with degrees), orienting arrow, direction-of-travel arrow, magnetic needle (red end points to magnetic north).
- **Keep it away** from metal, vehicles, power lines, phones and magnets.

## Take a bearing from a map
1. Place the **long edge** of the baseplate along the line from where you are to where you want to go, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing to your destination.
2. **Turn the bezel** until the orienting lines are parallel to the map's north-south grid lines, with the orienting arrow pointing to north on the map.
3. **Correct for declination** when going from the map to the compass (true or grid bearing to magnetic bearing). If your area's declination is **east,** subtract it. If it is **west,** add it. A memory aid is "east is least, west is best." Check the diagram on your map, and practice in a known place.
4. **Read the bearing** at the index line.

## Follow a bearing
1. **Hold the compass** level at waist height, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing away from you.
2. **Turn your body** until the red needle sits inside the orienting arrow ("red in the shed").
3. **Pick a landmark** along the direction of travel, such as a tree or rock.
4. **Walk to it,** then repeat.

## Find where you are
- **Handrails and catching features:** a road, stream or ridge that tells you when you have gone too far.
- **Resection:** take bearings to two or three known landmarks, convert to map bearings and draw the lines back. Where they cross is you.
- **Terrain association:** match what you see (hills, valleys, streams) with the map.

## Pace count
Count how many double-steps (every time your left foot hits) you take over 100 meters. Typical: 60 to 70. Use it to estimate distance, adjusting for slope and brush.

## Without a compass
- **Analog watch:** point the hour hand at the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and 12 points south (northern hemisphere).
- **Shadow stick:** place a stick, mark the tip of the shadow, wait 15 minutes, mark again. The line between marks runs west to east.
- **Stars:** the North Star, Polaris, is found by following the two "pointer" stars of the Big Dipper's bowl. It stays north.

## Keep the map dry
Use a zip bag or a map case. Fold with the area you need showing.

## Practice
Practice in a park or on a familiar trail, in daylight, until you trust the tools.

> This is basic awareness, not a substitute for hands-on training. Practice skills close to home before you depend on them.

## Learn it in a field
You cannot learn navigation from reading. Take a topographic map of a local park, a baseplate compass and a free afternoon. Walk a short route and check your position on the map every few minutes. The mistakes you make in a park cost nothing. The same mistakes in the wilderness cost a night in the woods.

## Declination
Magnetic north is not the same as true north, and the difference, called declination, varies by place. Find the declination on your map's margin and set or adjust your compass, or add or subtract it when you take bearings. Ignoring declination can put you hundreds of yards off after a few miles.

## A practice route
1. **Choose two points** about half a mile apart on your map.
2. **Take a bearing** from one to the other.
3. **Walk it,** using a landmark in line with the bearing, then walk to the landmark.
4. **Count paces** over a known distance, such as 100 meters, to learn your pace count.
5. **Check yourself** with the terrain: ridges, streams and trails.
6. **Return** on the back bearing.

## Handrails, catching features and aiming off
- **Handrails** are linear features such as trails, streams and ridges that guide you.
- **Catching features** are features beyond your target that warn you that you have gone too far, such as a road.
- **Aiming off** means deliberately aiming to one side of a target on a line feature so that you know which way to turn when you reach it.
- Combine them to avoid getting lost.

## Care of gear
Keep the map in a clear bag, the compass away from metal and magnets, and carry a spare. Learn to use a pencil and a notepad to track your route.
