[video] How much water you need

Most people greatly underestimate how much water they use. Run a tap, take a shower, flush a toilet, wash dishes: it adds up. The average person in the United States uses roughly 80 to 100 gallons a day at home. Now imagine that drops to nothing.

This lesson is not about comfort. It is about what you need to stay healthy and functioning when the supply stops.

## Minimum: survival level
**One gallon per person per day** is the usual minimum. It is not comfortable and not sustainable for long, but it covers drinking and a little food preparation, and it keeps a healthy adult alive in a short emergency. Hot weather, hard work, illness, pregnancy and nursing all push the need higher.

## Recommended: realistic level
**Three gallons per person per day** is a far more realistic target. It allows drinking, cooking, basic hygiene such as hand washing and brushing teeth, a quick wash and cleaning dishes. It lets you stay healthy and keep morale up during a long disruption.

## Why the gap matters
The difference between one and three gallons is the difference between surviving and functioning. If you plan for survival only, you will feel the strain within days, especially with children or in a long outage. If you plan for the realistic level, the minimum becomes your margin.

## A worked example
A family of four at the realistic level for one week: 3 gallons x 4 people x 7 days = **84 gallons.**
For two weeks: 3 x 4 x 14 = **168 gallons,** which is about three 55-gallon drums.
You do not need a year of water to begin. You do need enough to get through the first critical days without scrambling.

## The worksheet
Use this to work out your own number.
1. **Count people** in the household, and add guests you might host.
2. **Add pets.** A dog needs about an ounce of water per pound of body weight per day. A cat needs less. Check with your vet for your animals.
3. **Pick a level:** one gallon (survival), three gallons (realistic) or more for hot climates and heavy work.
4. **Choose days:** three days (minimum), 14 days (good) or 30 days (strong).
5. **Multiply:** people times gallons times days. Add pets.
6. **Add a margin** of about 10 percent for spills and error.

## Add the extras
- **Heat and exertion.** In hot weather or hard labor, plan for more.
- **Infants and formula.** Mixed formula uses water.
- **Medical needs.** Some conditions raise water needs, and some medical equipment uses water. Ask your clinician.
- **Cooking.** Rice, pasta and beans need a good deal of water. Count it.
- **Sanitation.** Handwashing and washing dishes need extra water, especially in sickness.
- **Fire.** A little water set aside for putting out a small fire is cheap insurance.

## Stretching what you have
- Use paper plates and cups to reduce washing.
- Use hand sanitizer when hands are not visibly dirty.
- Use treated or rain water for flushing and washing, and keep drinking water for drinking.
- Cook in ways that use less water, such as cooking pasta in less water or eating ready foods.
- Wear clothes more than once and wash only when you must.
- Never cut drinking water to save washing water, as the last lesson explained.

## Where to store it
- **Commercial bottled water,** in cases. It is easy and already sealed. Rotate by date.
- **Food-grade containers,** such as jugs and barrels sold for water, filled with tap water from a safe supply.
- **Stackable water bricks and cubes,** which are easy to move.
- **Barrels of 55 gallons,** which hold a lot but are heavy once full and need a pump or siphon.
- **Your water heater,** which holds 30 to 80 gallons of drinkable water you can drain in an emergency.
- **A bathtub bladder,** which holds around 100 gallons.

Do not use containers that held chemicals, milk jugs that cannot be cleaned or anything that will crack or leak. Keep water out of sunlight and away from fuel and chemicals, and in a cool place. Label it with the date you filled it.

## Ways to move it
Water weighs about 8 pounds a gallon. A five-gallon jug weighs about 40 pounds and a 55-gallon drum more than 450. Plan how you will lift and move your stored water, and what you will carry if you must leave. Collapsible containers and a small wagon or cart can solve a lot.

## Real-world check: the 24-hour test
Turn off the water to your home for a full day and live on stored water. Record how much you used, what surprised you and what you forgot, such as water for the toilet. Adjust the plan. Most families find they use more than they thought.

## Five principles
The calculation is **hazard assessment** and **preparedness.** Storage choices and rotation are **mitigation.** Rationing and stretching water in a crisis is **response.** The test makes you ready for **recovery** from a long outage.
