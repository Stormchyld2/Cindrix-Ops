[video] Your household emergency plan

Supplies keep you alive. A plan keeps you together. Picture this: your partner is at home with the children, you are at work forty minutes away, and the cell network goes down. The freeway is jammed. Nobody has heard from anybody. What does each person do?

If the answer is "I don't know", that is the gap a household plan fills. A written plan records where you will meet, how you will reach each other, who is responsible for what, and how you will keep the household going. It works even when you are not there, because the people who are there can pick it up and follow it.

## Why write it down
- Written plans get followed. Plans in your head are forgotten under stress, and one person's head is not available when that person is stuck elsewhere.
- A plan holds your household together when you are apart. Most families are apart during a typical weekday.
- It lets you act early. Decisions made in advance, calmly, are far better than decisions made at the last minute.
- It makes the people around you more useful. A child, a grandparent or a neighbor can help if the instructions are written and clear.

## The five parts of the plan
Module 3 builds one part at a time. Each part is short, and together they are your household's playbook.
1. **Emergency preparedness binder.** Your documents, contacts and plans in one grab-and-go place.
2. **Family disaster plan.** The bug-in or bug-out decision, with the steps for each.
3. **Group contact plan.** How family and your group check in with each other.
4. **Out-of-area contact plan.** One far-away person who relays news when local calls fail.
5. **Bug-out plan.** Where you would go, how you would get there and what you would take.

## The seven questions every plan answers
Whatever form your plan takes, test it with these.
1. **Who?** Every person and pet in the home, and who is responsible for whom, including the very young, the old and anyone with a disability or medical need.
2. **What could happen?** Your top two or three hazards, such as fire, flood, winter storm, wildfire or earthquake. Ask your local emergency management office what is most likely where you live.
3. **Where?** Meeting places near home, outside the neighborhood and out of town, and the places you would shelter.
4. **How?** The ways you will communicate, with a backup for each way. (This is the PACE idea: Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency.)
5. **When?** The triggers for staying or leaving, written in advance.
6. **With what?** The supplies you own and where they are.
7. **Who helps?** Neighbors, relatives and your group, and what you can do for them.

## Talk through the scenarios
Set aside an hour. Sit down together, pour a drink and walk through each of these. For each one, let every person say what they would do, and write it down.
- A house fire at night. Where is the escape route and the meeting place outside?
- A winter storm with no power for a week.
- A wildfire evacuation order.
- A flood or flash flood.
- Parents at work and children at school when phones stop working.
- A medical emergency when the roads are closed.

You will find disagreements and blind spots. That is the point. Better to find them at the kitchen table than on the road.

## Include children
Children can learn more than we expect. A child as young as four can learn a home phone number and a simple rule such as "stay with the grown-up." Older children can learn the meeting places, the out-of-area contact, and how to call emergency services. Practice makes it calm and a little fun, and calm children are far easier to protect. Never make it frightening. Treat it like a fire drill: ordinary, quick and normal.

## Special needs and pets
Write down the needs of anyone who depends on equipment or help, such as oxygen, mobility aids or hearing devices, and plan for power and transport. If someone takes daily medicines, plan how to keep a safe extra supply and how to carry it, with guidance from their doctor or pharmacist (no lesson here gives medicine amounts). Pets need a carrier or leash, food, water, records and a plan, because many public shelters do not accept animals.

## Keep it short
A short plan that is used beats a long one that sits in a drawer. One page for each topic is plenty. Use plain words. Put copies where people will find them: the binder, the go-bags and the refrigerator door.

## Keep it alive
Review the plan every six months. Review it again after any major change: a move, a new baby, a new job, a new school, a new medical condition or a death in the family. Run a drill once or twice a year. The first drill always finds something broken, and the third is smooth.

## How this fits the five principles of emergency management
A household plan is mostly **preparedness**, but it contains the rest. The hazard questions are **prevention and hazard assessment.** The scenario talks are **response.** Securing heavy furniture and keeping documents in a safe place are **mitigation.** The binder's insurance and records are **recovery.**
