[video] The out-of-area contact plan

In a disaster the first thing that fails for many people is not food or power but contact. Local cell towers overload as everybody calls everybody at once. Calls fail, texts queue and families panic. Yet long-distance calls and texts often still go through, because they use different routes. The answer is to stop everyone trying to reach everyone, and have everyone reach one person instead.

An **out-of-area (OOA) contact** is a person who lives far outside your region, ideally in another state, who acts as a central message point. Family members report to that person, and that person passes news along. You should have one plan for family and one for your group. This lesson covers the family plan.

## Step 1: choose the contact
A good contact has these qualities.
- **Distance.** At least 100 miles away and, ideally, in a different state and weather zone, so they are unlikely to be hit by the same event.
- **Reliability.** A responsible adult who usually answers and keeps a phone charged.
- **Willingness.** Ask them first. Explain the plan and ask them to keep a copy.
- **Backup.** Choose a second contact in a different region in case the first cannot be reached.

Give your contact the list of family members you will report on, with their phone numbers and email addresses and the names of any dependents.

## Step 2: make the contact card
Print a small card for every household member. Put one in each wallet, school bag, go-bag and phone case. Laminate it or use clear tape. The card holds:
- The contact's name and numbers: phone, text and email.
- The backup contact.
- The names and numbers of every family member.
- Your meeting places.
- A reminder: **"Text first. Give STATUS and C.A.N."**

For children, teach them the contact's name and how to show the card to an adult who can help.

## Step 3: agree on the reporting method
In an emergency, every household member reports to the out-of-area contact as soon as they can. The report has two parts.

**STATUS** is one of three choices:
- **Stable.**
- **Need minor assistance.**
- **Life-threatening situation.**

**C.A.N.** is a three-part summary:
- **Conditions:** what is happening around you.
- **Actions:** what you are doing.
- **Needs:** what you need.

An example text: "STATUS: Stable. C: Power out, roads blocked. A: Staying home, have supplies. N: None yet."

The format keeps messages short and useful, and the status tells the contact how urgent each report is.

## The contact's job
- **Collect reports** from everyone and note the time.
- **Pass news along** to other family members who contact them.
- **Keep a log:** who reported, when and what.
- **Relay needs** to people who can help.
- **Stay calm** if someone has not checked in for a few hours. Silence is usually the network, not disaster.

## Practical tips
- **Text first.** A text uses far less network capacity than a voice call and often gets through when calls fail.
- **Be brief.** Short messages go through more easily.
- **Try again at intervals.** If a message fails, wait a few minutes and retry. Avoid repeated redialing, which makes congestion worse.
- **Use a schedule.** Agree to check in on the hour at first, then every four hours, so contact is predictable.
- **Tell the contact if you move,** and where you are going.
- **Share the plan with grandparents and babysitters,** who may be the ones holding the children.
- **Conserve battery.** Switch to low-power mode and keep calls short.
- **Practice once a year.** Pick a Saturday and run a drill: everyone texts a STATUS and C.A.N. report to the contact.

## When the phones fail completely
The out-of-area plan assumes some network. If the networks are down everywhere, fall back on your other layers: pre-agreed meeting places, notes on doors, a hand-crank radio for news, and, for groups, radios. The Communications and Radio Licenses courses go deeper. Always plan for the failure of the plan.

## Five principles
Choosing and briefing the contact is **preparedness.** The STATUS and C.A.N. format is **response.** It supports **recovery** by tracking who is safe. The contact card is a small **mitigation**: it reduces confusion before it begins.
