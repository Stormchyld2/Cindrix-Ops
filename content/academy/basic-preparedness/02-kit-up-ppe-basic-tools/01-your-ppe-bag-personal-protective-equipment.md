[video] Your PPE bag

Your go-bag keeps you alive while you move. Your PPE bag keeps you from being hurt while you work in a damaged, dirty or dangerous place. They are two different jobs, and mixing them makes one bag too heavy to carry and too cluttered to use.

PPE stands for personal protective equipment: the clothing and gear that sits between your body and a hazard. After a disaster, most injuries are not from the event itself. They come afterward, from cleanup and rescue: cuts from glass, nails through boots, falling debris, smoke and dust in the lungs, and contact with floodwater, sewage or mold. Gear that costs a few dozen dollars prevents many of them.

## Why a separate bag
Keep the PPE bag next to the go-bag, but separate. You will not want full PPE in every emergency. Driving away from an approaching storm needs your go-bag, not a hard hat. Walking through ash, debris or wreckage needs both. A separate bag lets you take what the hazard calls for and leave the rest.

Think of it as matching the loadout to the job:
- **Fire or smoke nearby:** mask and eye protection first.
- **Earthquake or storm aftermath:** boots, gloves, helmet and glasses before you walk through debris or broken glass.
- **Flood:** waterproof boots and gloves, and stay out of the water wherever you can.
- **Sickness in the household or community:** gloves, mask and eye protection.

## The core items and what each one does
### Head and eyes
A **hard hat or helmet** protects against falling debris, which is the leading danger during cleanup. A bicycle or climbing helmet is much better than nothing. **Safety glasses or sealed goggles** stop dust, glass and splinters. Goggles with a seal are better in smoke and ash because they close the gap around your eyes.

### Lungs
An **N95 respirator** filters at least 95 percent of small airborne particles such as dust, ash and many germs. It does not protect against chemical gases, fumes or a lack of oxygen, and it does nothing for carbon monoxide. It only works when it seals to your face, so facial hair under the seal breaks it. Pinch the nose band, and check by breathing in and out for leaks. A cloth bandana is a poor substitute and is only better than nothing. If the hazard is chemical, do not rely on a dust mask: leave or shelter in place as officials direct.

### Hands
**Leather or cut-resistant work gloves** for debris. Keep a separate box of **disposable nitrile gloves** for blood, bodily fluids, sewage and chemicals, and replace them rather than washing and reusing them.

### Feet
**Sturdy boots** with a puncture-resistant sole and, ideally, a protected toe. Nails, rebar and glass are common after storms and quakes, and a foot injury ends your ability to move. Choose waterproof rubber boots if floods are your hazard. Keep a pair of spare socks in a sealed bag.

### Body
**Durable long-sleeved shirt and long pants** in canvas, denim or heavy synthetic cloth guard against cuts, scrapes, sunburn and insects. A **reflective vest** makes you visible near roads at night, in dust or in smoke. **Rain gear and a hat** keep you dry and warm, which matters because wet clothes lose heat quickly.

## Match the kit to your local hazards
Look at the hazards where you live: your county's hazard map or your local emergency management office will list them. Then add accordingly.
- **Wildfire:** N95 respirators, sealed goggles, natural-fiber clothing such as cotton or wool, because some synthetics melt onto skin.
- **Flood:** rubber boots, waterproof gloves, and no skin contact with floodwater, which carries sewage and chemicals.
- **Earthquake or tornado:** helmet, boots, gloves and glasses, plus a whistle to call for help if trapped.
- **Illness:** masks, gloves, eye protection, a disposable gown or coverall, and disinfecting wipes.
- **Chemical release:** a basic kit will not protect you. Follow official shelter-in-place or evacuation instructions.

## Fit, test and care
PPE only helps if it fits and you can put it on fast.
1. **Try everything on.** Wear the boots on a walk and the gloves while doing something fiddly.
2. **Practice the order.** Put on mask, glasses and gloves in under a minute without thinking.
3. **Store it clean and dry.** Keep it away from heat. Rubber and elastic perish.
4. **Replace on a schedule.** Check mask straps, glove integrity and boot soles each spring and fall, and replace nitrile gloves and masks about yearly.
5. **Keep one set in the car** if you can, and one by the go-bag at home.

## Safety rules that matter more than gear
- Never enter a damaged building unless it has been cleared. PPE does not make a collapsing structure safe.
- Turn off power and gas only if you know how and it is safe to reach the shutoff.
- Treat downed power lines as live and stay far away.
- Work with a partner, take breaks, and drink water. Fatigue causes mistakes.
- Cover cuts and wash them. Ask a clinician about a tetanus shot if you are not up to date. This lesson does not give medicine amounts.
