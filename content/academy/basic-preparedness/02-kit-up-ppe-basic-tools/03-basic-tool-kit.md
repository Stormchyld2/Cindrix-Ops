[video] Your basic tool kit

When a storm tears shingles off, a pipe bursts or a quake jams a door, the first hours decide whether a bad situation becomes a ruined house. A small, well-chosen tool kit lets you make temporary repairs, shut off hazards and free things that are stuck. It will not make you a contractor. It will keep damage from spreading until professionals can help.

A tool kit is also resilience in a smaller sense: everyday maintenance, from fixing a leaking tap to tightening a loose hinge, costs far less when you do it yourself, and the skills carry straight over to a crisis.

## Build it on a budget
You do not need top-of-the-line tools to start. A good order is cheapest first, then upgrade the tools you use most.
- **Budget:** ask relatives and friends to help you fill the list, since many people own extras. Garage sales, estate sales and thrift stores often have solid used tools for very little.
- **Mid range:** budget hardware chains sell inexpensive tools that are fine for occasional use. Expect them to wear out sooner under heavy work.
- **Best quality:** home-improvement stores and online tool retailers. Ask local builders where they buy theirs. Professional tools last for decades and are safer because they do not slip or break.

A good habit: buy the best you can afford for tools that hold your safety, such as ladders, saws and cutting edges, and the cheapest for tools that are rarely used.

## The core kit
Keep these together in one container you can carry with one hand, such as a toolbox or bucket, and store it where you can reach it.
- **Tape measure (25 feet):** for cutting tarps, plywood and boards to fit.
- **Claw hammer:** nailing and light prying.
- **Pry bar:** pulling boards, opening jammed doors and freeing trapped things.
- **Wood chisel (1 inch):** shaping wood and trimming around hinges.
- **Utility or razor knife:** cutting tarps, plastic sheeting, cord and tape.
- **Pliers:** gripping, bending and cutting wire. Add locking pliers if you can.
- **Adjustable wrench:** plumbing connections and the gas shutoff.
- **Combination screwdriver:** for common screws.
- **Basic socket set:** for bolts on appliances, vehicles and generators.

## Add these as budget allows
- **Hand saw and a folding saw** for branches and boards.
- **Work gloves and safety glasses** to go with every tool.
- **Tapes:** duct tape, painter's tape and electrical tape.
- **Plastic sheeting and heavy-duty staples** for temporary roof and window repairs.
- **Tarps and cord.**
- **Zip ties, wire, screws, nails and bolts** in an assortment.
- **Flashlight** with spare batteries.
- **Utility shutoff tool** for gas, and a **water main key** if you have one.
- **Cordless drill** with spare batteries, or a hand drill.
- **Shovel and a small axe or hatchet,** stored safely.
- **Bolt cutters.**
- **Multimeter and basic electrical tools,** only if you are trained to use them.

## Learn the jobs before you need them
A tool you cannot use is dead weight. Learn these tasks now, in daylight, with time to make mistakes.
1. **Shut off utilities.** Find your water main, your electric panel and your gas meter. Learn exactly how each one shuts off and write down who to call. Do not turn gas back on yourself. The utility must do that.
2. **Patch a roof with a tarp.** Overlap the tarp over the ridge, secure it to boards or battens rather than only nailing through the tarp, and weight or tie the edges.
3. **Board a window.** Cut plywood slightly larger than the opening and fasten it to the frame.
4. **Clear a clogged drain** and fix a leaking pipe temporarily with a clamp or patch.
5. **Cut a fallen branch safely,** away from power lines and with eye protection.
6. **Free a jammed door** or a trapped person's limb with leverage, not force.

A basic home-repair class, a community workshop or a handy friend is the fastest way to learn these.

## Tool safety
- Wear eye protection and gloves whenever you use a tool.
- Keep tools clean and dry and edges sharp. Dull tools slip and cause injuries.
- Cut away from your body and keep your free hand out of the way.
- Never work on live electrical wires. Treat every wire as live, especially after a storm, and stay far from downed lines.
- Use ladders on firm, level ground, keep three points of contact and never overreach.
- Store tools out of reach of children and lock up sharp ones.
- Never run a generator, grill or camp stove indoors or in a garage. Carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly.

## Protect your lungs
When you cut, sand, demolish or clean up, wear an N95 respirator. Buildings made before about 1980 may contain **asbestos,** and buildings made before 1978 may contain **lead paint.** Do not disturb damaged older materials. Ask your local authorities or public health office for guidance before you cut, sand or sweep them.
