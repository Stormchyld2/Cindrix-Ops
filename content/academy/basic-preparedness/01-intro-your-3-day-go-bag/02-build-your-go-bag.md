[video] Build your go-bag

A go-bag has one job: to carry you away from danger and keep you safe, warm, hydrated and in contact for three days. It is not a bunker in a backpack and it is not a hiking kit. It is the short list of things you would be desperate to have if you walked out the door with ten minutes' notice.

Each person in the household gets a bag. Children carry a small one with a few comfort and safety items. Adults carry heavier things and share the load for kids and for anyone who cannot carry weight.

## The five ideas that guide every choice
These five ideas decide what goes in and what stays out. If you remember nothing else from this lesson, remember these.
1. **Done beats perfect.** A half-packed bag by the door is worth more than a perfect bag you plan to finish next month. Start with what you have today.
2. **Keep it carryable.** Aim for no more than 15 to 20 percent of your body weight. A 175 pound person should carry about 35 pounds at most. If you cannot walk a mile with it, trim it.
3. **Essentials only.** Ask of each item: would this make a life-or-death difference in three days? If not, leave it. Comfort items are fine only when they are light.
4. **Two is one, one is none.** Anything critical will fail at the worst time. Carry more than one way to make fire, more than one source of light, more than one way to make water safe.
5. **Check it twice a year.** Food expires, batteries leak, children outgrow clothes. Put a reminder on your calendar to open the bag and refresh it every six months.

## Choosing the bag
Pick a backpack, not a duffel. A pack leaves your hands free for kids, doors and balance. For most adults a pack of 40 to 65 litres is plenty. Bigger is not better, because a big pack invites you to overfill it. Look for padded shoulder straps, a hip belt that moves weight off your shoulders, and a color that is easy to see. Try it on, loaded, before you buy it. If you already own a school or hiking pack, use that for now.

Keep each bag in a place you can reach in the dark: a hall closet, the garage by the door, the trunk of the car. Many households keep one set in the house and a lighter set in each car, because you may not be home when the order comes.

## What goes in the bag
Work through these in order. They follow the rule of threes: shelter and warmth first, then water, food, and the tools that tie it together.

### Warmth and shelter
Cold and wet kill more people than almost anything else, and they kill quickly. Pack **base layers** (top and bottom), an **insulating layer** such as a fleece or puffy jacket, a **rain jacket and rain pants,** a **warm hat,** gloves and **two spare pairs of socks.** Choose wool or synthetic fabric. Cotton holds water and chills you, so skip it. Add a **compact tarp or an emergency bivvy** and about fifty feet of cord. A tarp can be a roof, a windbreak, a ground sheet or a stretcher.

### Water
You can survive about three days without water, but you will think badly long before that. Carry at least **one litre of water** per person in sturdy bottles and a way to make more: a **portable filter** or purifier plus **backup purification tablets.** A filter removes bacteria and protozoa. Tablets and boiling cover viruses as well. Whenever you can, bring water to a rolling boil for one minute (three minutes above 6,500 feet) and let it cool.

### Food
Plan on about **2,000 to 2,500 calories per adult per day** for three days. Choose food that needs no cooking, or only hot water: energy bars, nut butter packets, trail mix, jerky, crackers, instant oatmeal, freeze-dried meals. Pack things you already like to eat, because a stressed person will not eat food they dislike. Include a few hard candies or something sweet for morale. Rotate the food into your normal eating every six months so it never expires.

### Light and fire
Carry **three sources of light:** a headlamp (hands free), a small lantern or flashlight, and a backup such as a second flashlight or chemical light stick. Pack spare batteries in a small plastic bag. For fire carry **three ways to make a flame:** a lighter, waterproof matches, and a ferro rod. Add **tinder** that lights even when damp, such as cotton balls rubbed with petroleum jelly or dryer lint in a small bag. Fire gives warmth, boils water and lifts morale.

### Papers, money and contact
Floods and fires destroy the paper you need to rebuild your life. Keep **copies** of driver's licenses or state IDs, passports, birth certificates, insurance policies and your key phone numbers in a waterproof bag. Also keep an encrypted copy on a USB stick. Pack **emergency cash:** $100 to $200 per adult in small bills such as fives, tens and twenties. When power is out, card readers and ATMs often do not work.

### First aid and medical needs
Carry a compact **first-aid kit** that includes bandages, gauze, tape, antiseptic wipes, a tourniquet, gloves, a space blanket and tweezers. Take a first-aid and bleeding-control class so you know how to use it. Pack **a list of your prescriptions, allergies and doctors,** plus **a spare pair of glasses.** Ask your doctor and pharmacist about keeping an extra supply of any prescription you depend on, and about how to store it safely. This course does not give medicine amounts, so follow the label and your doctor's directions.

### Hygiene
Small things keep you healthy and sane: travel toothbrush and paste, **hand sanitizer,** wet wipes, a travel roll of toilet paper (flattened), soap, feminine hygiene products and a few trash bags. Illness spreads fast when hands cannot be washed.

### Tools, navigation and communication
Pack **three cutting tools:** a multi-tool with a blade, a folding knife and a fixed-blade knife (where legal). Bring a **paper map** of your area with two ways out marked, and a **compass.** Carry a phone charger, a **battery bank,** and a **hand-crank or battery radio** that receives NOAA weather alerts. A small whistle carries farther than a shout. Add duct tape, a notepad and pencil, and work gloves.

### Protection
Self-protection is personal and depends on your laws, your training and your comfort. Think first about **avoidance and awareness,** a loud whistle and a bright flashlight. If you choose to carry anything more, learn the law where you live and get proper training before you carry it. We cover this in its own section later.

## Packing it so it works
Pack the heaviest items close to your back and in the middle. Put the things you need fast, such as the headlamp, first aid and rain jacket, at the top or in outside pockets. Use clear zip bags or colored stuff sacks to group items: warmth, water, food, first aid, tools. Label them. In an emergency you will be tired and stressed, so make the bag easy to understand at a glance. Weigh the finished bag and write the weight on a tag.

## Do a dry run
Carry the bag around the block, then up and down stairs. Put on the rain gear. Light the stove or the fire starter. Make water with the filter. A dry run reveals the zipper that sticks, the lantern with dead batteries and the item you cannot find. Do it once now, and again each time you refresh the bag.

? Why pack more than one way to make fire and more than one light?
- Because the law requires it
+ Because anything critical will fail sooner or later, so you need a backup
- Because they are cheap
- Because they weigh nothing
> Redundancy protects the items that matter most.

- Synthetic

- Fleece
