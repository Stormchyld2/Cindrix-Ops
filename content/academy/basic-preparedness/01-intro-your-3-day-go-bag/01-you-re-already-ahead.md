[video] Before the lights go out

You flip a switch and the light comes on. You turn a tap and water runs. You dial 911 and someone answers. All three feel permanent, so most people never plan for the day one of them stops. That feeling has a name: **normalcy bias,** the habit of believing tomorrow will look like today. It is the main reason people are caught unready, far more than lack of money or lack of gear.

You are already doing the hard part. You decided to look at the problem instead of away from it. This course turns that decision into a household that can take care of itself for at least three days and keep going for much longer, one small step at a time.

## What really happens in a disaster
The first hours of a big storm, flood, wildfire, earthquake or long power failure share a pattern. Roads fill or close. Phone networks jam because everyone calls at once. Stores sell out of water, batteries and bread within hours. Emergency crews go first to the worst cases, and for a day or more that may not include your street. FEMA and the Red Cross both plan around the same number: be able to look after yourself and your family without outside help for **at least 72 hours,** and longer if you live where help is slow to arrive.

Three days is not a prediction that help will fail. It is the gap between the moment a disaster hits and the moment organized help can reach everyone. Your job is to be comfortable and safe inside that gap.

## The rule of threes
This simple rule sets your priorities. In a harsh situation, a healthy adult can generally survive about:
- **Three minutes** without air (or in icy water)
- **Three hours** without shelter in severe weather
- **Three days** without water
- **Three weeks** without food

The numbers are rough, but the order is what matters. Fix the thing that kills fastest first. That is why this course starts with getting out of danger and staying warm and dry, moves to water, and only then to food. A person with a full pantry and no water is in worse trouble than a person with water and a thin pantry.

## Two ways to be ready: bug in and bug out
Most emergencies are solved by staying home. This is called **bugging in** (sheltering in place). A blizzard, a multi-day outage or a boil-water notice is usually best handled from your own kitchen. The rest of this course helps you stock the house for that.

Some emergencies force you to leave quickly: a wildfire line, a flood, a chemical spill, a gas leak, a building damaged by an earthquake. That is **bugging out** (evacuating). You may have minutes, not hours. The tool that makes leaving fast and calm is the **go-bag:** a pack by the door holding three days of essentials. Every person in the household gets one. Next lesson, you will build it.

> A go-bag does not mean you expect the worst. It means that if you must leave in ten minutes, the decisions are already made.

## The five habits behind everything in this course
Emergency managers organize all of their work around five ideas. We use the same five throughout the Academy, and you will see them again and again:
1. **Prevent and assess.** Learn which hazards are real where you live: flood, fire, quake, storm, outage, chemical or rail incidents nearby. You cannot prepare for everything, so find the two or three that are most likely.
2. **Prepare.** Stock supplies, make a plan, and practice it. This is most of what you will do now.
3. **Respond.** Know the first moves when something happens, and who does what.
4. **Mitigate.** Lower the damage in advance: secure heavy furniture, clear brush around the house, move valuables off the floor, keep gutters clean.
5. **Recover.** Get back to normal: documents, insurance, repairs, and the emotional toll for you and your children.

## Your first win today
Most people stall because the job feels big. So begin with something you can finish in ten minutes. The checklist below asks for three small things: name the two disasters you are most likely to face, put one go-bag item where you can reach it fast, and write down one phone number on paper. Finish those and you are already more prepared than most of your neighbors.

- [ ] Name the two disasters most likely where you live | Think flood, wildfire, earthquake, winter storm, hurricane, tornado, long power outage
- [ ] Choose one item you already own for your go-bag and set it where you can grab it | A backpack, a flashlight, a water bottle, a warm jacket
- [ ] Write one emergency phone number on paper and put it in your wallet | A family member who lives outside your area works best
- [ ] Find out how your area sends emergency alerts | Check your county or city emergency page and turn on phone alerts
