[video] The household baseline

Before you buy more gear or learn advanced skills, you need a clear picture of "ready enough." That picture is the household baseline: the point where your family could look after its own basic needs for at least two weeks, without stores, deliveries or help arriving, and could still move safely if you had to leave. Everything else in Tier 3 builds toward it, and everything in Tier 2 and Tier 1 stands on it.

Two weeks is not a random number. After big disasters such as hurricanes, ice storms, earthquakes and floods, emergency managers plan for roads to be blocked, power to be down and shelves to be empty for days, and they ask every household to be able to cope for at least a week or two. Two weeks covers almost every common emergency and gives responders time to reach the people who truly cannot help themselves.

## What the baseline is, and what it is not
It is ordinary readiness: a few weeks of water and food, a way to stay warm and clean, medicines and first aid, a way to get news and reach each other, a safe home, and a plan. Ordinary households can reach it with modest money over a few months.

It is not a bunker, not a buried stockpile and not a closet full of gadgets. Gear you cannot use, food your family will not eat, and a plan nobody has read are all false comfort. A small, tested, understood baseline beats a large, untested one every time.

## The seven needs
Every emergency, from a three-day outage to a long-term disruption, comes back to the same seven needs. Learn them as a set.
1. **Water.** The first number to know is one gallon per person per day for drinking and basic cooking, and about two to three gallons per person per day once you add washing and cooking for a household. Aim for two weeks stored at home, and own a way to treat more.
2. **Food.** Start with a two-week supply of food your family already eats, that needs little or no cooking. Grow toward a three-month pantry that you rotate through normal eating.
3. **Shelter and warmth.** A home that is safe to stay in, a way to keep warm or cool without grid power, and a backup place to go if the house is unsafe.
4. **Health.** First aid supplies, a supply of the prescriptions and medical items you depend on (ask your doctor or pharmacist how to keep a safe extra), and the skills to use them. No lesson here gives medicine amounts. Follow the label and your clinician.
5. **Communication.** A battery or hand-crank radio for news and weather alerts, a way to charge phones, and an agreed way to reach each other when the networks fail.
6. **Sanitation and hygiene.** A way to deal with human waste and trash, and to wash hands. Disease, not hunger, is what hurts people first when sanitation fails.
7. **Safety and security.** Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, fire extinguishers, working locks and lights, knowing how to shut off your utilities, and awareness of the people and hazards around you.

Two supporting layers hold the seven up. **Money** means a little cash in small bills and enough savings to ride out a missed paycheck. **Community** means neighbors, relatives and a group you trust, because no household has every skill or every tool.

## How to check each need: the four questions
Walk through the seven needs, and for each one ask these four questions out loud. Write the answers down.
1. **Do I have a Primary and a backup?** This is the idea behind the PACE plan: Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency. If the first way fails, what is the second? Water from the tap, then stored water, then a filter and a creek, then tablets and a pot.
2. **How long would it last?** Count it. Gallons divided by people divided by days. Cans divided by meals. A feeling of "plenty" is not a number.
3. **Could my family use it without me?** If you are away, hurt or just asleep, can a teenager, a spouse or a grandparent find it, understand it and use it? Label things and show people.
4. **When did I last check it?** Stored items quietly fail: food expires, batteries leak, filters crack, medicines go out of date. Put a twice-a-year reminder on the calendar.

## Reading your results
Most households find that they are strong in two or three needs and nearly empty in two or three others. That is normal and useful. The gaps tell you where your money and weekends should go next. Fix the cheapest, fastest gap first, because momentum matters more than perfection.

A good order for most families is: go-bag, then water, then a two-week food supply, then the plan and binder, then first aid and health, then everything else. The rest of this course follows that order.

## Tier 3 and the way ahead
Tier 3 is the foundation of the three tiers. It asks one thing of you: that your household can meet its basic needs on its own for a while, calmly. Tier 2 builds practitioner skills on top, and Tier 1 is the elite level. You do not skip a floor of a building because you are in a hurry, and you do not skip the baseline.

The five principles of emergency management run through every module: **prevention and hazard assessment** (know what can happen where you live), **preparedness** (build the supplies, plan and skills), **response** (act well when it happens), **mitigation** (reduce the damage before it happens) and **recovery** (get back on your feet afterward). The baseline is mostly preparedness, but you will see the other four in every lesson.
