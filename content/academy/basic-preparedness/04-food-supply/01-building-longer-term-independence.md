[video] The food supply system

What would you do if the shelves were bare for a week? Storms close roads, strikes and breakdowns stop deliveries, and panic buying empties a store in an afternoon. Short disruptions happen in ordinary life, and a household with a food reserve rides them out calmly while others queue.

This lesson introduces the system the next three lessons build: a layered food supply that keeps your family fed through small and medium emergencies. It takes time and a little discipline. Done in steps, it costs far less than people expect, and the confidence it brings is real.

## Why layers
A single pile of food does not fit every emergency. If you must leave, you need food you can carry. If you stay, you need food that needs little fuel. If the disruption goes on, you need bulk staples that last. So the system has three layers that work together.
1. **The two-week portable supply.** Light, compact, ready-to-eat food in a labeled bin next to the go-bags, for evacuating by vehicle or carrying on foot.
2. **The three-month pantry.** The food your family already eats, in larger quantity, rotated through normal use. This is your main buffer.
3. **The one-year long-term supply.** Bulk staples such as rice, beans and oats, sealed for long storage. This is the deep reserve and the base for variety.

The go-bag from Module 1 holds the first three days. Everything here extends it.

## How much is enough?
Plan on **about 2,000 to 2,500 calories per adult per day,** and adjust for children, active people, pregnancy and anyone with medical needs. A rough measure is one pound of dry food per person per day.
- A family of four for three months is about 360 pounds of dry-food equivalent.
- Two weeks for one adult is about 28,000 to 35,000 calories.

The numbers can look daunting. Do not be put off. You never build it all at once.

## The order of building
1. **Three days:** the go-bag.
2. **Two weeks:** portable food.
3. **One month:** regular food, bought a little extra at a time.
4. **Three months:** the pantry.
5. **A year of staples:** a few sealed buckets at a time.
Each step makes the next one easier, and each step gives real protection by itself.

## What to count besides calories
- **Water.** Food needs water to cook and drink. Do not build a food supply without a water supply (Module 5).
- **Cooking fuel.** A way to heat food and the fuel for it. Include a camp stove, fuel and lighters.
- **Nutrition.** Variety, vitamins and protein matter on a long diet. Consider multivitamins for children and adults, with advice from a clinician. This course does not give medicine amounts.
- **Special diets.** Allergies, diabetes, infants, toddlers and older people have specific needs. Plan their food separately and keep it labeled.
- **Pets.** Food and water for animals.
- **Morale.** Coffee, tea, sweets and spices make plain food feel like a meal.

## Eat what you store
The best storage is food your family already eats, because you rotate it naturally. Test every unfamiliar food before you bury it in a bucket. A family that dislikes beans will not eat beans in a crisis, and a stressed child will not eat what he has never tried. Cook from your supply regularly so your cooks know how.

## Safety and rotation
- **First in, first out:** put new food behind old.
- **Mark dates** on cans and containers.
- **Store cool, dry and dark,** off the floor and away from heat.
- **Check each season** for damage, pests, swollen cans or leaks.
- **Do not eat** food from a bulging, leaking or badly dented can, or any food that smells or looks wrong.
- **Protect from pests** in sealed containers.

## Preparing as a group
A prepared neighborhood is calmer. You cannot feed everyone, and a plan that depends on sharing your own supply is a fragile plan. The better approach is to encourage the people around you to build their own reserves, through bulk buys, swapped tips and classes. A group of self-reliant households is stronger than one well-stocked house surrounded by empty ones.

## Five principles
Building the pantry is **preparedness.** Choosing foods for hazards you face is **hazard assessment.** Rotation and safe storage are **mitigation.** Eating from the supply is **response,** and the staples let you **recover** for months, not days.
