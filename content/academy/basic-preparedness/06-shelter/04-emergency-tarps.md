[video] Tarp shelters

When everything else fails, a tarp is one of the most useful and lightest pieces of emergency gear you can own. You are not aiming for comfort. You are aiming to keep rain and wind off you and to hold in body heat. In many places, that is the difference between getting through the night and not.

A tarp takes skill, though. A tarp you have never rigged is a tangle in the dark. Learn three setups now, in daylight, and practice them.

## Types of tarp
### The basic waterproof tarp
A 10 foot by 10 foot waterproof nylon tarp is one of the best single pieces of emergency gear. With rope or paracord, or even improvised tie-offs, it can be a roof, a windbreak, a ground sheet, a rain catcher or a stretcher. A purpose-built camping tarp is lighter and more compact. A basic hardware-store tarp is cheap, heavy and tough, and it works.

Things to know:
- Check the weight, size and grommets.
- Silnylon and poly tarps are light. Plastic tarps are heavy and cheap but tear at the grommets.
- Reinforced corners and edges matter.
- Keep a bundle of cord, ideally 50 feet, and some stakes.
- Reflective or bright colors help rescuers find you. Dark colors hide you. Choose by purpose.

### The poncho shelter
A military-style rain poncho is a multi-use item. It is a rain poncho, a tarp for shelter, and, with a liner, a sleeping cover. With a poncho liner (a quilted, lightweight blanket, nicknamed "woobie"), it adds warmth and can be used as a blanket or sleeping layer.
- **Why it is valuable:** it pulls a lot of weight for its size, and it keeps you dry while moving.
- **Limits:** small as a shelter, less protection for more than one person, and you cannot wear it and use it as a shelter at once.

## Three setups to practice
### 1. A-frame (rain protection)
1. **Tie** a ridge line between two trees at about waist to chest height, or use two poles.
2. **Drape** the tarp over the line, centered.
3. **Stake or weigh down** the four corners at an angle to make two sloped sides.
4. **Pull it tight** so rain runs off.
5. **Close the ends** if you can, by tying the corners or adding another tarp.
*Good for:* rain, and with a ground sheet under you, a complete shelter.

### 2. Lean-to (windbreak and reflector)
1. **Tie** one long edge high between two trees or poles.
2. **Stake** the other edge to the ground on the downwind side.
3. **Face** the open side away from the wind. If you use a fire, and only where it is safe, build a reflector wall on the other side.
*Good for:* wind protection and quick warmth.

### 3. Ground cloth and burrito wrap
1. **Lay** the tarp flat.
2. **Place** a pad and sleeping bag on one half.
3. **Fold** the other half over you.
4. **Tuck** the edges.
*Good for:* cold, wet ground in a pinch.

## Knots you need
You do not need many. Learn these three well:
- **Bowline:** a loop that will not slip.
- **Taut-line hitch:** an adjustable tension knot for guy lines.
- **Clove hitch:** a quick way to tie to a pole or tree.
Also learn a **trucker's hitch** for tightening a ridge line. Practice with your eyes closed and with cold fingers, in gloves.

## Make anchor points
If there are no trees:
- Tie to a vehicle, a fence or rocks.
- Use sticks as stakes, driven at an angle away from the tarp.
- In snow, bury a stick or a stuff sack with the line tied around it, called a deadman anchor.
- Use rocks as weights on the edges.

## Choose the site
- **High and well-drained,** not in a dry creek bed or low spot where water pools.
- **Away from hazards:** dead branches overhead, rock fall, flood channels, game trails and lone trees in a thunderstorm.
- **Out of the wind,** with the back to the weather.
- **On insulation:** under you, use branches, leaves, a pad or a pack. The ground drains heat faster than air.

## Condensation and safety
- Leave a gap for airflow, and keep the shelter small, which is easier to warm.
- **Never cook or run a flame or heater inside a closed shelter.** Carbon monoxide can build up, and a tarp catches fire easily.
- Keep your gear dry, and keep your clothes dry. Dress in layers, and remove them before you sweat.
- Keep a whistle and a signal mirror to call for help, and a bright piece of cloth to be seen.

## Practice
Set up each configuration in your yard at least once, and time yourself. Do it in the rain if you can. Sleep out under a tarp once a year. You will learn about drips, wind and how little warmth a plastic sheet gives.

## Five principles
The tarp is **preparedness** at the Emergency level. Choosing the site is **hazard assessment** and **mitigation.** Rigging it is **response,** and the skill carries over to repairing a damaged roof in **recovery.**
