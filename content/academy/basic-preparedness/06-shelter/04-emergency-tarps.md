When everything else fails, tarp shelters are one of the most versatile and lightweight emergency options you can have.

You are not optimizing for comfort, this is about keeping you dry, blocking wind, and retaining body heat. In many environments, that's the difference between getting through the night and not.

## Types of Tarps

## 10' x 10" Waterproof Nylon Tarp
A simple tarp is one of the most useful pieces of emergency gear you can own. Using rope, paracord, or even improvised tie-offs it can be used in many different configurations to shelter you from rain and wind, such as:

- Lean-to (wind break)
- A-frame (rain protection)
- Ground cover or overhead shelter

It doesn't have to be fancy, you can get a purpose-built camping tarp (lighter, more compact) or just a basic hardware store tarp (cheap, durable, gets the job done).

Things to Know:
- Requires basic setup knowledge (practice beforehand)
- Needs anchor points (trees, poles, vehicle, etc.)

## Military Style Rain Poncho with Liner
A true multi-use survival item that pulls a lot of weight for its size. Its a rain poncho, tarp for shelter, or sleeping bivy sack.

With a liner ("woobie"): Adds insulation and warmth or can be used as a lightweight blanket or sleeping layer.

Why it's valuable:
- Compact and easy to carry
- No complex setup required
- Combines shelter + clothing + warmth in one item

Limitations:
- Smaller coverage area than a full tarp
- Less protection in heavy weather or long-term use

## Tarp systems are your true emergency fallback
They are lightweight, flexible, and reliable when nothing else is available. But they require a bit of skill. Once you know how to use them, they're one of the most dependable shelter options you can carry.

## Checklist
- Obtain a waterproof tarp
- Practice setting up a tarp in at least one configuration
- Obtain cordage or rope (if you don't already have it)
- Add Military style rain Poncho and poncho liner to your supplies

## Going deeper: three tarp setups to practice
### 1. A-frame (rain protection)
1. **Tie** a ridge line between two trees at about waist to chest height, or use two poles.
2. **Drape** the tarp over the ridge line, centered.
3. **Stake or weigh down** the four corners at an angle, creating two sloped sides.
4. **Pull** it tight so rain runs off.
5. **Close** the ends if you can, by tying the corners or adding a second tarp.
Good for: rain, and with a ground sheet under you, a complete shelter.

### 2. Lean-to (wind break and reflector)
1. **Tie** one long edge high between two trees or poles.
2. **Stake** the other edge to the ground, on the downwind side.
3. **Place** the open side facing away from the wind. If you use a fire (only where safe), build a reflector wall on the other side.
Good for: wind protection and quick warmth.

### 3. Ground cloth and burrito wrap
1. **Lay** the tarp flat.
2. **Place** a pad and sleeping bag on one half.
3. **Fold** the other half over you.
4. **Tuck** the edges.
Good for: cold, wet ground in a pinch.

### Knots you need
- **Taut-line hitch** for adjustable guy lines.
- **Bowline** for a fixed loop.
- **Clove hitch** for tying to trees.
- **Trucker's hitch** for tightening.
See the Survival basics course for knots.

### Tarp features to look for
- **Grommets** around the edges and in the middle.
- **Reinforced corners.**
- **Rip-stop nylon** or polyethylene.
- **Size:** 8 x 10 feet is a good solo size. 10 x 12 feet for two.
- **Color:** a bright color for visibility if you need rescue, or a muted one for blending in.

### Make anchor points
If there are no trees: tie to a vehicle, a fence, rocks, or stake with sticks. In snow, bury a stick or a stuff sack as an anchor (a "deadman").

### Condensation
Leave a gap for airflow. Keep the shelter small. Do not cook inside.

### Practice
Set up each configuration in your yard at least once. Time yourself. Do it in the rain if you can.
