[video] Your contingency shelter

If your home and your planned alternates are not available, the Contingency layer is the next fallback: workable but not ideal. These are options you control yourself, and they are meant to keep you protected, not comfortable. This lesson covers the vehicle, tents of several types and bivy sacks.

## Option: sleep in your vehicle
A vehicle can be one of the fastest shelter options, especially when you must leave home with no fixed destination.

**Pros:**
- Already on hand, with seats, doors and a roof.
- Fast to deploy and mobile.
- Protects against wind, rain and, to some extent, people and animals.
- You can charge devices and run heating or cooling for limited periods.

**Cons:**
- Cramped and uncomfortable for more than a night or two.
- Cold in winter and hot in summer. Temperatures inside a car can become dangerous quickly in heat.
- Fuel is limited.
- Parking rules and safety vary, and you may be moved on.
- Carbon monoxide risk.

### Safety warning: carbon monoxide
Carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless and can kill in minutes. Never run the engine in a closed garage. Never run it when the exhaust pipe is blocked by snow or mud, because gas can seep into the cabin. If you run the engine for heat, clear the tailpipe, crack a window on the downwind side, run it only briefly and keep a battery carbon monoxide alarm in the car. Never use a charcoal grill, camp stove or generator inside a car or tent.

### Vehicle shelter, done right
- Park where it is legal and safe, away from hazards such as flood channels, falling trees, roads and power lines.
- Keep a warm blanket, sleeping bags, hat and gloves in the car.
- Use reflective sun shades in summer and crack windows for air.
- In winter, keep the fuel tank at least half full, and keep a shovel, sand or cat litter, and a candle-lantern-free light source.
- Keep water and food in the car.
- Leave a note with a contact, and tell someone where you are.
- Best use: short-term shelter while traveling or evacuating.

## If you are already living in a car or tent
For some people, sleeping in a vehicle is not a preparedness exercise. It is how they are getting by. If that is you, or someone you care about, it is hard and it takes resilience. Help exists. Call **211** (or visit 211.org) for local housing, food and emergency help. Local churches, nonprofits and community groups also help, and many areas have safe parking programs. You are not alone, and nothing in this course is meant to judge anyone's situation.

## Option: tents
Tents are compact and light, but they do not protect against dangerous animals or people, and they give little insulation by themselves. A tent plus a warm sleeping bag and a pad makes a real shelter.

### Family-style tent
- **Pros:** roomy for people and gear; comfortable for families; good for longer stays in one place.
- **Cons:** bulky and heavy; not practical to carry on foot; needs space and time to set up.
- **Best use:** a vehicle-based bug-out or a backyard fallback.

### Backpacking tent
- **Pros:** light and compact, quick to pitch, designed for wind and rain.
- **Cons:** small, more expensive, with less room for gear.
- **Best use:** bugging out on foot.

### Using a tent well
- **Site:** choose high, flat, well-drained ground. Avoid dry stream beds, hilltops in lightning, and dead trees overhead.
- **Ground:** use a groundsheet and an insulating pad. The ground steals more heat than the air.
- **Warmth:** a sleeping bag rated for the lowest likely temperature, plus layers and a hat.
- **Ventilation:** leave vents open to reduce condensation.
- **Fire safety:** never cook or run a heater inside a tent.
- **Practice:** pitch it in the yard first, and know where each pole goes.

## Option: bivouac sack (bivy)
A bivy is a waterproof shell that goes over a sleeping bag. It protects from wind and rain with very little weight.
- **Good-quality breathable bivies** (waterproof, breathable fabric) work well but are expensive.
- **Emergency foil bivies** are cheap and tiny. They can save a life by retaining body heat, but they trap moisture and are not breathable. Used wrongly, you can end up damp and chilled. Use them as a last-resort backup, and dry out when you can.
- **Best use:** an emergency or an ultralight backup.

## Summary
- **Vehicle:** fast and mobile.
- **Tent:** flexible and portable.
- **Bivy:** a last-resort backup.
None of these is about comfort. They are about staying protected when your primary shelter is gone.

## Test it
Do a night in the backyard, a campground or your car once a year. You will learn what you missed: a light, a sleeping pad, a way to cook, or a stove you do not know how to light. Fix the list and repeat.

## Five principles
Choosing the shelter to match the hazard is **hazard assessment.** The kit is **preparedness.** Setting up safely is **response.** Carbon monoxide rules and site choice are **mitigation,** and the yearly test builds the confidence for **recovery.**
