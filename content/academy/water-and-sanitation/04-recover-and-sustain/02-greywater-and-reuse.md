Greywater is used water from sinks, showers and laundry, **not** toilets. It carries soap and some germs, but it can water plants and flush toilets, easing a water shortage.

## First, check the rules
Many states and counties regulate greywater. In an emergency, rules are often relaxed, but you should still keep it away from people, wells and streams.

## What it can do
- **Flush toilets** by pouring from a bucket into the bowl.
- **Water ornamental plants, trees and fruit bushes** (not the edible parts of leafy crops).
- **Wash floors, rinse tools** and clean outdoors.

## Keep it safe
- **Use it quickly,** within a day. Standing greywater smells and grows germs.
- **Do not store** it for long.
- **Do not use** greywater from washing diapers or from anyone ill with a stomach infection.
- **Avoid** putting it on lettuce, root crops or anything eaten raw. Water at the soil level.
- **Use plant-friendly soap:** no boron, chlorine bleach or salt-heavy products in large amounts. Natural soap is best.
- **Keep it away** from wells, streams, and ponds by at least 100 feet, and from the house wall.
- **Spread it out** so it soaks in rather than pooling.

## Collect it simply
- Put a basin in the sink and carry it outside.
- Divert the washing machine outlet into a barrel or a mulch basin.
- A shower bucket catches water while it warms.
- A mulch basin around a tree: dig a shallow ring, fill with wood chips, and pour water on it.

## Blackwater
Water from toilets, kitchen sinks with food waste and diaper washing is **blackwater.** Do not reuse it. Dispose of it with sewage.

## Plan
Greywater can cut household use by a quarter to a half. It helps a garden survive a drought. Combine it with rain catchment and a good filter for a resilient system.

> Local rules on rainwater, wells, greywater and waste differ. Check with your health department before you build anything permanent.
