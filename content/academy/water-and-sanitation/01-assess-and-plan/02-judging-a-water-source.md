In a long emergency you may need water that is not from the tap. Choosing the best source and judging its risk is a skill.

## A ladder of sources, best to worst
1. **Stored, sealed drinking water** and your own treated stock.
2. **Water in your home:** the water heater tank, pipes and melted ice cubes.
3. **Rain** collected from a clean surface.
4. **Springs and wells** that you know and have tested.
5. **Flowing streams and rivers,** high up and far from people, farms and roads.
6. **Lakes and ponds,** where water stands still.
7. **Floodwater, ditches, puddles** and anything near sewage, fuel, dead animals or industry. Avoid these unless there is truly no other choice, and treat them in several steps.
Salt water and urine are never drinkable, and boiling does not fix them.

## What could be in it
- **Germs:** bacteria (such as E. coli), viruses and parasites (such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium). They cause diarrhea, vomiting and dehydration. Boiling, chlorine and a good filter deal with them, each in different ways.
- **Chemicals:** fuel, pesticides, fertilizer, solvents and metals. Boiling does **not** remove them. A carbon filter helps with some, distillation with more. Avoid water that smells of fuel or chemicals or has an oily sheen.
- **Particles:** mud and silt. These make water cloudy and protect germs from treatment.
- **Natural toxins:** blue-green algae blooms make some lakes poisonous. Do not drink from a pond or lake with a scum, a pea-soup color or a bad smell.

## Reading the site
- **Upstream:** what is above this spot? Towns, farms, animals, camps, mines or roads?
- **Flow:** moving water is usually safer than still water.
- **Source:** a spring flowing from the ground is better than water pooled near it.
- **Season:** after heavy rain, runoff makes streams dirty.
- **Signs of life:** dead fish, algae or a smell are warnings.

## Collect with care
Scoop from just below the surface in a moving stream, away from the bank, using a clean container. Let mud settle. Use a cloth or coffee filter to take out particles before treating.

## Test where you can
Private wells should be tested every year for bacteria and nitrate, and for local chemicals as your health department advises. Cheap test strips tell you about chlorine, pH and hardness. Strips and kits do not prove water is safe from germs, so still treat it.

## Make a source map
On a map of your area, mark every water source within walking distance: streams, ponds, springs, hydrants, neighbors' wells. Rank them using the ladder. Note the ones that might fail in a drought or flood.

> Local rules on rainwater, wells, greywater and waste differ. Check with your health department before you build anything permanent.
