You cannot reduce a hazard you have not assessed. Start by understanding how wildfire can reach you.

## How wildfire reaches a home
A wildfire damages a home three ways:
1. **Embers.** Burning pieces of bark, leaves and debris are carried by wind, sometimes a mile or more ahead of the flames. Fire researchers and agencies agree that embers are the main reason homes ignite, and many sources put the share of home ignitions caused by embers and small spot fires very high, around 90 percent. An ember in a dry gutter, an open attic vent or a pile of leaves against the siding can ignite a home before the fire front arrives.
2. **Radiant heat.** Heat from burning trees and buildings nearby can crack windows and ignite siding without flames touching.
3. **Direct flame.** Fire spreading along vegetation, fences, decks and mulch right up to the house.

## Where the risk is highest
- The **wildland-urban interface (WUI),** where homes meet or mix with wild vegetation.
- **Slopes and ridges.** Fire runs uphill fast. A house on a slope, at the top of a canyon or in a narrow draw is at greater risk.
- **Dense, dry, dead vegetation** with no breaks.
- **One-road neighborhoods.** If the only road is blocked, you are trapped.
- **Areas with a fire history,** and areas with long drought.

## What increases the danger on the day
The National Weather Service issues a **Red Flag Warning** when warm temperatures, low humidity and strong winds combine with dry fuels to create high fire danger. A **Fire Weather Watch** means those conditions may develop. On those days:
- Do not burn, do not use equipment that sparks, and do not park on dry grass.
- Check your alerts and your go bags.
- Know your trigger to leave (see the action plan lesson).

## Do a risk check
1. **Look up your address** on the USFA WUI Fire Property Awareness Explorer or your state forestry agency's hazard map.
2. **Ask your local fire department** how fire behaves in your area and where evacuation routes lead.
3. **Walk your property** and list what could burn.
4. **Look at your road:** how many ways out? Can fire trucks reach you? Is your address visible?
5. **Check water:** hydrants, a pool, a pond, a well with a generator. Know what the fire service can use.
6. **Check your insurance** (a later lesson).

## Use the five principles
Throughout this course we use the five-part cycle that emergency managers use:
1. **Prevention and hazard assessment:** find the risks and remove or reduce them (this module).
2. **Preparedness:** roles, plans, training, drills, contacts.
3. **Response:** what you do when it happens (the Set and Go modules).
4. **Mitigation:** active steps to limit damage while it is underway.
5. **Recovery:** restore normal life and learn (the final module).

## Sign up for alerts
You cannot act on a warning you never get.
- Sign up for your county or city **emergency alert** system. Most are free.
- Turn on **Wireless Emergency Alerts** on every phone.
- Buy a **NOAA weather radio** with battery backup.
- Follow your **fire department** and **emergency management** accounts.
- Save your local fire department's non-emergency number.
- Program NOAA weather channels into your handheld radios (see the Communications course).

## Checklist
- Looked up your address's wildfire exposure
- Know how many ways out of your neighborhood there are
- Signed up for your county or city emergency alerts
- Wireless Emergency Alerts turned on
- Saved your local fire department's contact information
- Know what a Red Flag Warning means

> Know the hazard. Everything else follows from that.
