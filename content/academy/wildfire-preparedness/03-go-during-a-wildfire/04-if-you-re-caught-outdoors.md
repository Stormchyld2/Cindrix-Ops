Being caught in the open, away from a vehicle or a structure, is the most dangerous version of this scenario. But there are actions that measurably improve your chances.

## Prevent it
Wildland firefighters use **LCES** to stay alive, and hikers and rural residents can too:
- **L: Lookouts.** Someone watching for the fire.
- **C: Communications.** A way to reach each other and call for help.
- **E: Escape routes.** At least two, known in advance.
- **S: Safety zones.** Places large enough and cleared enough to survive in.

When you are in fire country:
- Check **fire danger and closures** before you go.
- Tell someone your route and return time.
- Carry a **map, a whistle, a phone and a charged power bank.**
- Know where the **burned or bare areas** are.
- **Never camp** where escape routes are poor.
- Keep **watching** the smoke, and move early.

## If the fire is approaching
1. **Move away early,** across the slope or downhill, **toward bare or burned ground,** a road, a stream, a rock outcrop, a lake or a cleared area. Never run uphill. Fire runs uphill faster than you can.
2. **Avoid canyons,** narrow draws, saddles and chimneys, which can funnel flames and wind like a flue.
3. **Move to a wide, clear area** (a meadow, a parking lot or a wide road).
4. **Call 911** if you have a signal and report your location.
5. **Use water if you can reach it.** Any body of water such as a lake, a river, an irrigation ditch, a livestock tank or a swimming pool helps. Get in, stay low, keep your head wet and breathe through a wet cloth. Move to the downwind side of deeper water if smoke is bad, and watch for the heat.

## If you cannot reach a safe place
Last-resort steps:
1. **Stay calm** and move to an area **clear of vegetation,** or a **ditch or depression** on level ground, preferably with little fuel.
2. **Clear fuel** around you if you have time, scraping away leaves, grass and brush down to bare soil.
3. **Lie face down** in the depression, with your feet toward the fire if it approaches from one side.
4. **Cover your body** with soil if possible, and with a coat, wool blanket or backpack.
5. **Cover your head and face** and breathe through a wet cloth, near the ground where the air is cooler and cleaner.
6. **Stay down** until the fire has passed. The front passes in minutes, but the heat and smoke can last longer.
7. **Keep your skin covered,** wear natural fibers and avoid synthetics.

## After the front passes
- **Move onto burned ground** where there is no fuel left, if safe.
- **Watch for falling trees** and branches (snags), rolling rocks and burning stumps.
- **Treat burns** with cool (not ice cold) running water and cover them with a clean, dry dressing. Get medical help.
- **Call for help** and stay put if you cannot move safely.

## Protective clothing
Wear what fire experts advise: long pants, a long-sleeve shirt, heavy shoes or boots, a cap, a dry bandanna for your face, and goggles or glasses. Natural fibers like cotton and wool are better. Synthetic fabrics can melt against skin.

## Gear you might need
- An N95 mask
- Wool or cotton layers (not synthetic, which can melt)
- A bandanna and water bottle

## Checklist
- Knows to seek a depression with sparse fuel and clear nearby fuel if there is time
- Knows to lie face down and cover exposed skin until the fire passes
- Knows to avoid canyons, natural chimneys and saddles in terrain
- Knows to use any body of water if reachable
- Knows LCES

> Never run uphill from a fire. Go to bare ground or water.
