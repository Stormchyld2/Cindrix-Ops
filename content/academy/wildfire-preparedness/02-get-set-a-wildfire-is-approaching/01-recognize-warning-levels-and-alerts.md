Not every alert means the same thing, and confusing "be ready" with "leave now" is dangerous. Most areas use a tiered system, such as an advisory, then a warning, then a formal evacuation order. Learn your local terms before you rely on them under stress.

## Typical levels (terms vary by place)
- **Watch or advisory:** be aware. A fire may be a threat. Get ready, check bags, stay tuned.
- **Evacuation warning (sometimes "voluntary" or "get set"):** there is a significant threat. **Be ready to leave at a moment's notice,** and leave now if you have children, pets, livestock, medical needs or limited mobility.
- **Evacuation order (sometimes "mandatory" or "go"):** there is an immediate threat to life. **Leave now.**
- **Shelter-in-place:** in rare cases, officials tell you to stay inside because roads are unsafe or the hazard is a smoke or hazardous-material event. Follow instructions.

Fire officials may use "voluntary," "mandatory," "precautionary" or "immediate threat." **Act on any of them at once.** If you are unsure, leave.

## Know the other weather terms
- **Red Flag Warning:** critical fire weather in effect.
- **Fire Weather Watch:** conditions may develop within the next one to three days.
- **Air Quality Alert:** smoke is affecting the air. See the health lesson.

## Where alerts come from
- **Wireless Emergency Alerts** on your phone.
- **County and city notification systems** (sign up).
- **NOAA Weather Radio.**
- **Local radio and TV.**
- **Fire department and emergency management** websites and social media.
- **Door-to-door notification** by officers or firefighters.
- **Sirens** or loudspeakers in some communities.
- **Neighbors, your group** and your radio network.

## Do not rely on one source
Cell towers burn and networks jam. Have a **radio that does not need a signal.**
- **Battery or hand-crank radio** with NOAA weather band.
- **A scanner or handheld radio** to monitor local fire and emergency frequencies where allowed.
- **A shortwave or VHF/UHF radio** for listening on amateur radio frequencies to gather information. If you are a licensed operator, you can also transmit and ask questions. See the Communications and Radio Licenses courses.

## Monitor the fire
- **Official maps and updates** from your fire agency, the InciWeb site or state dashboards.
- **Air quality maps** such as AirNow's Fire and Smoke Map.
- **Satellite fire detection maps.**
- **Wind direction and forecasts.**
- Check at **set intervals,** not constantly. Assign one person to monitor and share.

## Decide using your triggers
Use the triggers in your action plan, not your emotions. If the fire reaches a trigger, **act.** The earlier you move, the safer you are, and you keep the roads clear for firefighters.

## Checklist
- Know the difference between an evacuation warning and an evacuation order
- Have a way to receive alerts that does not depend solely on cell signal
- Know to monitor local news or radio once fire activity is nearby
- Assigned one person to watch updates
- Know your local terms and levels

> "Be ready" is not "leave now." Know which one you have heard.
