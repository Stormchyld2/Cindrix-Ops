Opioids include prescription pain medicines, heroin and fentanyl. An overdose slows or stops breathing. **Naloxone (brand name Narcan)** can reverse an opioid overdose and is safe for a bystander to use. It does nothing harmful if the person has not taken opioids. Many states let anyone carry it, and many pharmacies and health departments give it out.

## Signs of an opioid overdose
- **Unresponsive** or cannot be woken.
- **Slow or shallow breathing,** or none. Choking, gurgling or snoring sounds while they cannot be awakened.
- **Blue or purple lips and fingernails.**
- **Tiny (pinpoint) pupils.**
- Limp body, cold and clammy skin.

## The five essential steps (SAMHSA)
1. **Evaluate for signs of overdose.** Try to wake the person by shouting their name, then by rubbing your knuckles hard on the breastbone or upper lip.
2. **Call 911.** Say the person is unresponsive and not breathing, and give the exact location. Follow the dispatcher's instructions.
3. **Give naloxone** to anyone showing signs of opioid overdose, or if you suspect one. Use the nasal spray as the package directs. You do not need to be sure. The package instructions explain how to use it. **Additional naloxone may be needed** if there is no response, and fentanyl-involved overdoses may need more than one device. Follow the instructions and the dispatcher.
4. **Support breathing.** If the person is not breathing, give **rescue breaths,** or full CPR if there is no pulse.
5. **Monitor the response.** Stay with the person and watch them for **at least four hours after the last naloxone use,** because naloxone wears off and breathing can slow again. Get them to medical care even if they seem to recover.

## Rescue breathing (adult)
1. Make sure the airway is clear.
2. **Tilt the head back, lift the chin and pinch the nose.**
3. Cover their mouth with yours (a barrier mask helps) and give **two slow breaths,** confirming the chest rises.
4. Continue with **one breath every 5 seconds** until they breathe or help arrives. If there is no pulse, do CPR.

## Recovery position
If the person is breathing but unresponsive, or if you must leave them, **roll them onto their side** with the top knee bent and the head tilted slightly back, so that vomit drains out and the airway stays open.

## What to expect after naloxone
- The person may wake up suddenly, confused or agitated, because naloxone can cause **withdrawal symptoms** (sweating, vomiting, shaking, anger). **Stay calm,** reassure them, and explain what happened.
- They may want to use more drugs. Explain that this can cause overdose again when naloxone wears off, and that they need medical care.
- **Never** leave them alone.

## Limits
- Naloxone has **no effect on non-opioid overdoses,** such as cocaine, alcohol or benzodiazepines. Still call 911 and provide breathing support.
- It cannot revive someone who has been without oxygen too long. **Do not delay** calling 911 or starting CPR.

## Good Samaritan laws
Many states have **Good Samaritan laws** that protect people who call 911 for an overdose from certain drug charges. Check your state's rule.

## Keep naloxone on hand
- Get naloxone from a pharmacy (many sell it over the counter), a health department or a community program, and **put it in your first aid kit, car and go bag.**
- **Check expiration dates** and store at room temperature.
- **Know where it is** and show family members how to use it.
- **Take a short training** from your health department. Many offer them for free.

## Prevention in the home
- Lock up prescription opioids and other medicines.
- Dispose of unused medicine at take-back sites.
- Talk with your family about the risks of fake pills, which may contain fentanyl.
- Do not mix opioids with alcohol or sedatives.

## Checklist
- Know the signs of an opioid overdose
- Know the five essential steps
- Naloxone in the kit, car and go bag
- Know rescue breathing and the recovery position
- Know to stay at least four hours
