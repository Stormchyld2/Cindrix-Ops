The hands-on abilities that can mean the difference between life and death.

The ability to perform basic first aid can mean the difference between life and death. Even treating a minor cut correctly can mean the difference between quick healing and a dangerous infection.

In high-stress emergencies, people do not rise to the occasion. They fall back on their level of training. Repetition and familiarity are critical.

## To be prepared, you must learn
- How to perform **CPR** (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)
- How to use an **AED** (automated external defibrillator)
- Basic **wound care**
- **Bleeding control**
- **Chest seals**
- Applying a **tourniquet**
- **Splinting**
- Responding to an opioid overdose with **naloxone** (Narcan)
- Responding to a severe allergic reaction with **epinephrine**
- Choking, shock, burns, and heat and cold illness

## Medical skills are perishable
Reading about them helps. True competence comes from **hands-on training, repetition and practice under stress.**

### Where to learn
**CPR and AED training.** Most classes finish in a single day. Certifications are typically valid for two years.
- American Red Cross
- American Heart Association
- Local fire departments
- Community colleges
- Hospitals
- Employers and workplace safety programs

**Basic first aid courses.** Topics typically include wound care, bleeding control, burns, fractures and sprains, allergic reactions, medical emergencies and patient assessment.
- American Red Cross
- American Heart Association
- CERT programs (Community Emergency Response Team)
- Outdoor schools
- Wilderness medicine organizations
- Community preparedness groups

**Other resources**
- **Stop the Bleed** (stopthebleed.org), a free or low-cost course of about an hour.
- Your local health department, which may run naloxone training.
- Reputable books and videos from the organizations above (use them to refresh, not to replace practice).

## How skills build
1. **Learn** the steps (read, watch).
2. **Practice** on a manikin or with a partner, with real gear.
3. **Practice under stress:** timed, in the dark, with noise.
4. **Repeat** every few months.
5. **Teach** someone else. Teaching locks it in.

## Make it a group habit
Hold a medical skills night. Run scenarios. Check each other's kits. Track who is certified and when it expires. Use the Academy sign-off page to record training.

## Checklist
- Attend and complete a basic first aid class
- Attend and complete a CPR and AED class
- Attend a Stop the Bleed course
- Put recertification dates on the calendar
- Hold a medical practice session with your group every quarter

> The average citizen does not need to become a doctor to make a difference. A person with basic training, a calm mind and simple equipment can save lives before professionals arrive.

> This lesson is first aid education, not medical advice or a substitute for hands-on training from the American Red Cross, American Heart Association or a Stop the Bleed class. Call 911 for any life-threatening emergency.
