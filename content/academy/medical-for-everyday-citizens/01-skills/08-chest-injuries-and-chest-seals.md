Wounds to the chest can interfere with breathing. They need quick recognition and a call to 911. This lesson covers what a trained citizen can do.

## Why chest wounds are dangerous
Breathing works by pressure changes in the chest. A hole in the chest wall (an **open chest wound,** sometimes called a sucking chest wound) can let air in and out, so the lung cannot expand. Air can also build up around the lung and press on it and on the heart (**tension pneumothorax**), which can kill quickly.

## Signs
- A wound in the chest or upper back, from a stab, a gunshot, a fall or a crash.
- Trouble breathing, fast or shallow breathing.
- A **sucking or hissing sound** when the person breathes.
- Bubbling blood at the wound.
- Blue lips, anxiety, pain with breathing.
- Uneven chest movement.
- Signs of shock.

## What to do
1. **Make sure the scene is safe** and **call 911.**
2. **Expose the chest** and look at the front and **the back** and sides, including under the armpits. Wounds are easy to miss.
3. **Control severe bleeding first** with pressure or packing, as taught in the bleeding lesson.
4. **Seal the wound** with a **vented chest seal** if you have one (see below). If there is an exit wound, seal that too.
5. **Position the patient** sitting up or on the injured side if they are more comfortable, whichever helps them breathe. Do not force them flat.
6. **Watch their breathing.** If it gets worse after a seal is placed (growing difficulty breathing, anxiety, blue lips), the seal may be trapping air. **Lift a corner of the seal** or remove it for a moment to let the trapped air out, then press it back on, and call for help. This is where hands-on training matters.
7. **Treat for shock.** Keep them warm.
8. **Do not remove** impaled objects. Pad around them.

## How to apply a chest seal
1. **Wipe blood** and moisture from the skin around the wound, so the seal can stick.
2. **Ask the patient to breathe out** (if able), and **apply the seal** over the wound at the end of exhaling.
3. **Press and smooth** the seal to make good contact on all sides.
4. **Use a vented seal** (it lets air out but not in). A **non-vented** seal can trap air. With no vented seal, a clean plastic bag or foil taped on **three sides** (leaving one side open as a valve) works as an emergency alternative.
5. **Carry two seals** in an IFAK, one for each side or for an exit wound.
6. **Check** the seal often.

## Other chest problems
- **Rib fractures:** pain with breathing and tenderness. Support the area gently, for example hold a pillow against the chest. Do not wrap tightly. Seek care.
- **Flail chest:** a segment of ribs moving opposite to the rest. Seek urgent care.
- **Chest pain without injury** may be a heart attack. **Call 911,** have the person rest, and give aspirin only if a medical professional or the dispatcher advises and the person is not allergic (follow the label). See the CPR course.

## Practice
Practice applying seals on a training chest or a partner (with a practice pad), and on both front and back.

## Checklist
- Can recognize a chest wound
- Can apply a chest seal quickly
- Two vented chest seals in the IFAK
- Know the signs of worsening breathing
- Know to look at the back as well as the front

> This lesson is first aid education, not medical advice or a substitute for hands-on training from the American Red Cross, American Heart Association or a Stop the Bleed class. Call 911 for any life-threatening emergency.
