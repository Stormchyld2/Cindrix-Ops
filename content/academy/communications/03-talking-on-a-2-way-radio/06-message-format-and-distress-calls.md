In an emergency your message has to be clear, complete and short. This lesson gives you two skills: writing a message that can be sent without questions, and calling for help when a life is at risk.

## Write it before you send it
When you can, write the message first. It takes ten seconds and makes the call shorter and clearer.

A good message has:

1. **Who it is for.** The station or person you are calling.
2. **Who it is from.** You, with your callsign or name.
3. **What happened.** The facts only.
4. **Where.** The location in words someone else can find.
5. **What you need.** A clear request.
6. **Time.** When it was written or when it happened.

## A simple format: WHO, WHAT, WHERE, NEED
"Base, this is Fox. Two people. One has a broken leg. We are at the trail marker at the north fork, half a mile past the bridge. We need help walking out and a way to move the injured. Over."

## Keep to facts
- Say what you know. Label guesses and rumors as such: "unconfirmed," "I believe."
- Do not name people on an open channel if you can avoid it. Use code words or numbers.
- Leave out opinions and jokes.
- Give numbers digit by digit.

## Calling for help: the steps
If a life is in danger, speak up quickly and clearly. These steps work on almost any radio and any channel:

1. **Say the call.** "**Mayday, Mayday, Mayday**" if a life is in immediate danger. "**Pan-pan, pan-pan, pan-pan**" for an urgent problem that is not life-threatening.
2. **Say who you are and where.** "This is [your name or callsign], at [location]." Give a landmark, road, cross street, GPS position or grid reference.
3. **Say what is wrong.** "One person unconscious, not breathing." Short and factual.
4. **Say what you need.** "Need an ambulance." "Need a rescue team."
5. **Say how many people.** "Four people, two hurt."
6. **End with "over,"** then **listen** for an answer.
7. **Repeat the call every minute** or so, or until someone answers.

## What the rules say
Rules allow people to use whatever communication they have to protect life and property when normal systems are not available. That covers real emergencies, in which a person's life or property is in immediate danger. It does not cover routine tests and practice on channels you are not allowed to use. Plan for emergencies with the right license and radio, and keep the emergency exception for when it is really needed.

## Other ways to call for help
- **Phone:** If you have any signal at all, call the local emergency number. A text to emergency services may work in some places where a call does not.
- **Signals:** Three of anything is the traditional distress signal: three whistle blasts, three flashes of a light, three shots, three fires in a triangle.
- **A satellite messenger:** A personal locator beacon or satellite messenger can send a call for help from nearly anywhere with a clear sky.

## When someone else calls for help
- **Stop transmitting.** Listen.
- **Write down** what you hear, including the location.
- **Answer** only if you can help and nobody else has answered.
- **Relay** the call to emergency services if the caller cannot reach them.

## Practice
- Write three sample messages and read them aloud.
- Practice the words out loud with the radio switched off, or with the transmit button NOT pressed. Never send a practice Mayday on a live channel. A false distress call is against the rules and can send real rescuers away from real people.
- If you drill with real radios, practice the WHO, WHAT, WHERE, NEED message on your group's own channel with everyone told in advance that it is a drill.

## Checklist
- Practice writing the WHO, WHAT, WHERE, NEED message.
- Learn the Mayday and Pan-pan steps.
- Learn your own location description.
- Add whistles and signal mirrors to your kit.
- Run a message drill on your own group channel every quarter.

> If a life is at risk, speak clearly, say where you are, say what is wrong and keep calling until someone answers.
