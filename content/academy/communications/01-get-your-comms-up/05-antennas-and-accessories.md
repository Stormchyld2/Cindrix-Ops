The antenna is the most important part of your radio's performance, and it is the cheapest upgrade you will make. The antenna that comes on most handhelds is made for convenience, not range.

## Why the stock antenna is weak
A short "rubber duck" antenna is compact and tough, but it gives up some of your signal. Held at face level, a typical rubber duck radiates only a fraction of what a good full-size antenna would. Clip the radio to your belt and the signal drops again, because your body soaks up some of it. In plain terms, a 5-watt radio with a rubber duck on your belt can put out only a small part of its rated power.

## Better antennas, in order of cost
1. **A longer flexible antenna.** A replacement that is longer than the rubber duck gives a clear gain with no fuss. Choose a model for your band (GMRS is UHF).
2. **A telescoping half-wave antenna.** Extends when you need range and collapses for travel. In the open it can extend your range from about a mile to three miles or more, depending on the ground. Use it when you are standing still. Do not leave it extended in a pack or in heavy brush.
3. **A "Slim Jim" or J-pole.** A simple antenna made of twin-lead wire or metal tube. It is light and compact. You hang it from a tree branch or a window frame, and the extra height makes a big difference.
4. **A magnetic-mount antenna for a vehicle.** Place it in the center of the metal roof. The roof acts as a ground plane. A radio in a car without an outside antenna loses most of its signal to the metal body.

## Make the most of any antenna
- **Get high.** Height beats power. Move to a rooftop, hillside or open field before you transmit.
- **Keep it vertical.** Handheld antennas are built to be vertical. A tilted antenna sends less signal in some directions.
- **Hold the radio away from your body.** Hold it out in front of your face, not against your chest or in a pocket.
- **Mind the obstacles.** Metal buildings, thick walls and dense forest all block signals. Step out into the open.
- **Add a ground plane.** On a vehicle roof, a metal sheet or a large flat metal object helps a small antenna perform better.

## Connectors and adapters
Radios use different antenna connectors, and the wrong one is the number one reason an antenna will not fit.

- **SMA** is the small screw connector on many handhelds (male or female, so check which).
- **BNC** is the quarter-turn connector on many antennas and cables.
- **UHF (PL-259)** is the large connector on many vehicle and base antennas.
- Carry an **adapter** for each connector type you own. Common ones are SMA to BNC and BNC to UHF.
- Do not transmit without an antenna connected. It can damage the radio.

## Cables and coax
- A longer cable loses more signal. Keep the cable as short as you can.
- Use quality coax and keep the connectors dry and tight.
- Protect the cable from sharp bends and from foot traffic.

## Replace and test
- Flexible antennas wear out. Replace them yearly, or sooner if they kink or crack.
- Replace a telescoping antenna if it wobbles or will not stay extended.
- Test any antenna before you rely on it. Transmit to a friend a known distance away and compare.

## About radios that are not certified for GMRS
Inexpensive imported handhelds, such as the popular Baofeng models, are not FCC-certified for GMRS. The FCC does not allow uncertified radios to transmit on GMRS or FRS frequencies. A licensed ham radio operator may use these radios legally on the amateur bands. If you use one for GMRS, you are outside the rules. The safe course is to buy a certified GMRS radio for GMRS and to keep any uncertified radio for **listening** or, if you are a ham, for amateur frequencies.

## Checklist
- Replace the stock antenna with a longer flexible or telescoping model suited to your band.
- Carry a spare antenna and the right adapters.
- Add a magnetic-mount antenna for your vehicle.
- Practice transmitting from a high place and from a low one and compare the range.
- Replace flexible antennas once a year.

> Never key the radio with no antenna attached.
