A radio is only as good as the power behind it. Most radio failures in an emergency are not the radio. They are dead batteries.

## Know your battery type
- **Lithium-ion (Li-ion) packs.** Most modern handhelds use them. They hold a lot of charge and are light, but a Li-ion radio is of little use after a power outage unless you have another way to charge it or a spare pack.
- **Nickel-metal-hydride (NiMH) and older nickel-cadmium (NiCd) packs.** These need to be cycled (fully discharged and recharged) from time to time. Write the date you last charged them on a piece of tape on the pack.
- **AA cases.** Many radios accept a case that holds AA batteries. AA cells are available almost everywhere, and they are your best fallback.

## Plan power for the whole event
Count the hours you expect to use the radio, not the minutes. A radio that is only listening draws very little. A radio that is transmitting draws a lot. Follow the pattern: listen most of the time, talk briefly.

1. **Count radios.** How many radios will you run at the same time?
2. **Estimate hours.** A realistic plan for a three-day event is at least 72 hours with the radio turned on and mostly listening.
3. **Carry spares.** One spare battery for every radio is the minimum. Two is better.
4. **Plan charging.** Decide how each battery will be recharged: car, solar, power bank or power station.

## Ways to charge without the grid
- **Car charger.** A cable from the lighter socket. Cigarette-lighter plugs are often unreliable, so also keep a direct battery lead with a fuse.
- **Solar panel.** A folding panel with a charge controller. A small panel is slow, but a steady trickle beats nothing.
- **Power bank.** Some radios charge by USB. A power bank holds many full recharges.
- **Small power station.** A battery with outlets, which runs a base station and charges everything else.
- **Hand-crank charger.** A last resort. It gives only a little power, but it works.

## Fuse every power lead
If you wire a radio to a car battery or a power supply, put an **in-line fuse** in the positive wire, close to the battery. A short can start a fire in seconds.

- Use a small fuse (around 2 amps) for a handheld.
- Use a larger fuse (around 10 amps) for a mobile radio rated up to 40 watts. Check the radio's manual for the correct value.
- Use thick enough wire. Thin wire gets hot.
- Remember the polarity: red is positive and black is negative. Match them every time.
- Use a different connector size or color for each voltage so you cannot plug the wrong cord into a radio.

## Care and storage
- Keep batteries at room temperature when you can. In the cold, keep a spare battery in an inside pocket close to your body.
- Charge your packs to about half before storing them for months, and top them up every few months.
- Never charge a swollen, hot or damaged lithium-ion battery. Take it out of service and dispose of it properly.
- Never leave charging batteries unattended on a bed or sofa.
- Label every battery with the date you last charged it.

## Save power while operating
- Use the lowest power setting that reaches the person you are calling.
- Turn the screen brightness down.
- Turn the radio off when you are not on a schedule to listen.
- Keep transmissions short. A long conversation burns your battery.

## Checklist
- Know what battery type each radio uses.
- Carry one spare battery per radio, two if you can.
- Pack an AA battery case for any radio that supports one.
- Choose two ways to charge without the grid and test them.
- Fuse every power lead you build.
- Label each battery with its last-charged date.
- Recharge and rotate batteries every few months.

> Plan the power first. A great radio with a dead battery is just a weight in your bag.

- Keep the screen at full brightness
- Always use the highest power
- Leave the radio transmitting
> Transmitting uses the most power, so use only what you need.

? What should you do with a swollen lithium-ion battery?
+ Stop using it and dispose of it properly
- Keep using it as a spare
- Charge it more slowly
- Store it in your pocket
> A swollen battery can overheat or catch fire. Take it out of service.
