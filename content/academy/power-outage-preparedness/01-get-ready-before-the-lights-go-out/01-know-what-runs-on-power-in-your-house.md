The best time to deal with a power outage is on a sunny Tuesday when nothing is wrong. Everything in this section can be done in a weekend, and most of it costs very little.

## Why start with a hazard walk-through
Emergency managers begin with **prevention and hazard assessment:** find what can go wrong, then reduce or remove it before it happens. For a power outage, the hazard is not the dark. It is everything in your life that quietly depends on electricity.

Walk through your home and ask one question in every room: **what stops working when the power does?** Some answers are obvious, like lights and the refrigerator. Others catch people off guard.

## The usual surprises
- **Water.** If you are on a well, your pump is electric. No power means no water and no flushing toilets. City water usually keeps flowing for a while, but treatment and pumping can fail in long outages.
- **Heat.** Many furnaces need electricity to run the blower and the controls, even if they burn gas or oil. Heat pumps and electric baseboard heaters need power outright.
- **Cooling.** Air conditioners, fans and sump pumps.
- **Garage door opener.** It will not open the door your car is parked behind unless you know the manual release.
- **Cordless home phones and the internet router.** Cell service may be up, but anything that relied on home internet is gone.
- **Electric stove and oven.** No hot food unless you have another way to cook.
- **Medical equipment.** Oxygen concentrators, CPAP machines, powered wheelchairs and beds, nebulizers, and refrigerated medicines.
- **Security.** Alarm systems, electric door locks, driveway gates and cameras.
- **Sump pump.** A dead sump pump in a storm outage can flood a basement.
- **Fuel pumps and ATMs.** Gas stations and cash machines need power. Have cash and fuel in the vehicle.

## Make the list
Make a simple table on paper or in the program: **item, what it needs, what it does if power fails, and the backup.** Mark each item as:
1. **Life-safety** (medical devices, heat in deep cold, cooling in extreme heat).
2. **Health and property** (refrigerator, freezer, sump pump, water pump).
3. **Comfort and convenience** (TV, internet, lights).

Your plan covers the first two groups first.

## Know your panel and your shutoffs
- Find the **main electrical panel** and learn how to flip the main breaker. Label every breaker.
- Find the **gas shutoff** and keep the right wrench near it. Do not turn gas off unless you smell gas or are told to.
- Find the **water shutoff** in case pipes freeze or break.
- Practice the **manual release** on the garage door.
- Keep a flashlight at each of these places.

## Tell people who need to know
Both the **electric utility** and your **local fire department** should know if someone in your home depends on a powered medical device. Many utilities keep a medical or priority list and may warn you before planned shutoffs. Ask yours how to sign up. This does not guarantee power, so you still need a backup plan.

## Checklist
- Walked the house and listed everything that needs power
- Know whether your water depends on an electric pump
- Know whether your heating system works without power
- Found and practiced the garage door manual release
- Know where the main electrical panel is and how to flip the main breaker
- Know where the gas and water shutoffs are
- Told the utility and fire department about any medical device that needs power

> A written list of what depends on power is the start of every outage plan.
