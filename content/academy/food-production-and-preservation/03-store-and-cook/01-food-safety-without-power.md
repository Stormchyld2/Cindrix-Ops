When systems fail, food poisoning is a real danger. A few rules prevent most of it.

## The danger zone
Bacteria multiply fastest between **40 and 140 °F.** Do not leave perishable food in that range for more than **2 hours** (1 hour above 90 °F).

## When the power goes out
- Keep fridge and freezer doors closed. A refrigerator keeps food safe about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours.
- Use a thermometer. Throw out meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, soft cheese and leftovers that have been above 40 °F for more than 2 hours.
- Cook or can frozen food that is still partly frozen with ice crystals, if you can.
- **Never taste** food to test it.

## After a flood
Throw away any food that touched floodwater, including canned goods whose seals are damaged. Cans that are intact can be saved if you remove labels, wash them with soapy water, rinse and sanitize in a bleach solution. Discard food in cardboard, cartons and screw-top jars that touched floodwater.

## Cooking safely
- Wash hands, surfaces and tools.
- Use separate cutting boards for raw meat and ready foods.
- Cook to safe internal temperatures: poultry and leftovers 165 °F, ground meat 160 °F, whole cuts of beef, pork and fish 145 °F, with rest time as advised. Use a food thermometer.
- Cool and store leftovers fast.
- Reheat to 165 °F.
- Boil or treat water you use for food, drinks and washing.

## Washing dishes with little water
Use three basins: wash with hot, soapy water; rinse; and sanitize with a teaspoon of plain bleach per gallon of water. Air-dry.

## Special risks
Babies, older people, pregnant women and anyone with a weak immune system are at higher risk from food poisoning. Take extra care.

## Pests and spoilage
Check dry goods and cans regularly for signs of damage. Throw away anything that is moldy, bulging, rusted through or smells wrong.

## Illness
Vomiting and diarrhea can dehydrate quickly. Give fluids, rest, and get help for severe or bloody symptoms, high fever, or signs of dehydration, especially in children and older adults.

> Always use tested recipes and current guidance for preserving food. Local laws govern livestock, hunting, fishing and selling food.
