In an emergency you will not have all the facts. You will have to decide anyway. The good news is that decision-making is a skill that you can train.

## What stress does
Stress narrows your attention, speeds your heart, and pushes you to act, or to freeze. Fine motor skills drop, hearing and sight may narrow, and memory of the event may be patchy. This is normal. Training, rehearsal and simple procedures protect you.

## The OODA loop
John Boyd, a US Air Force pilot, described decisions as a loop:
- **Observe:** take in information.
- **Orient:** make sense of it, using your experience, training and context.
- **Decide:** choose an action.
- **Act:** do it, then observe the result.
Faster, better-informed loops beat slower ones. The key is **orientation:** the model in your head. Training and drills improve it.

## Recognition-primed decisions
Experts in fields such as firefighting seldom compare many options. They recognize a situation as similar to one they know, pick a course that worked, mentally run it, and act. If it will not work, they pick another. This is why practice with many scenarios matters.

## A simple decision aid
When time allows, use a short routine:
1. **What is happening?** (facts, not guesses)
2. **What is the goal?** (life safety first)
3. **What are my options?** (at least two)
4. **What are the risks of each?**
5. **Decide, announce, act.**
6. **Check:** is it working? If not, change.

## Tips
- **Decide at the right level.** The person closest to the problem often knows best.
- **Say your decision aloud** so others know and can correct you.
- **Set triggers in advance** (when X happens, we do Y).
- **Do not wait for certainty.** An 80 percent plan today beats a perfect plan tomorrow.
- **Do not stick to a bad plan.** Watch for signs that the plan is failing.
- **Use checklists** for critical steps.
- **Ask for a second opinion** if time allows.

## Biases that cause errors
- **Normalcy bias:** assuming things will go on as usual. Signs are ignored.
- **Confirmation bias:** seeing only what supports your belief.
- **Sunk cost:** staying with a plan because of what you have invested.
- **Group-think:** everyone agrees to avoid conflict.
- **Fixation:** locking onto one problem and missing others.
Use a "devil's advocate" to challenge plans.

## Fatigue and food
Tired, hungry people decide poorly. Rotate leaders, eat, drink, and rest.

## Afterward
Review decisions without blame. Ask: what did we know? What did we do? Why? What would we do next time?
