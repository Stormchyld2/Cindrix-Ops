You cannot prepare for everything, so you rank. Professionals do this with a Threat and Hazard Identification and Risk Assessment (THIRA). You can do a household version in an evening.

## Step 1: list the hazards
Include all three groups:
- **Natural:** storms, flood, wildfire, earthquake, winter weather, heat, drought, landslide, disease outbreak.
- **Technological and accidental:** long power cut, water or gas failure, fire, chemical release, transport crash, dam failure, communications failure.
- **Human-caused:** crime, civil unrest, cyber attack, supply disruption, economic shock, targeted violence.
Ask neighbors, the county emergency manager and your hazard mitigation plan (many counties publish one). Look up flood maps, wildfire risk maps and fault maps.

## Step 2: describe each one
For the top hazards, write a short, realistic scenario: "A winter ice storm takes out power for ten days." Describe **where, how long, how bad** and what it affects.

## Step 3: rate likelihood and impact
Use a simple five-by-five grid, or just low, medium, high.
- **Likelihood:** how often has it happened, and how likely is it in the next ten years?
- **Impact:** harm to people, to your home, to your supplies, to your income and to your time.
- **Speed:** warning time. Hurricanes come with days of warning. Earthquakes come with none.
- **Duration:** a few hours, days, weeks?
A rare event with severe impact (such as a major earthquake) can rank above a common event with mild impact.

## Step 4: assess exposure and vulnerability
- **Exposure:** what is in the path? Your home, your road, your workplace, your water source.
- **Vulnerability:** how likely is it to be damaged or fail? An old roof, a low-lying basement, a single road out, a household member who depends on electricity for medical equipment.
- **Capacity:** what you already have to cope: supplies, skills, neighbors, insurance.

## Step 5: rank and decide
Make a table: hazard, likelihood, impact, exposure, top consequences, current capacity, gap. Pick the **top five.** These drive your plan.

## Step 6: set target capabilities
For each top hazard, write what you must be able to do: "Keep four people warm and fed for 14 days with no power," "Evacuate in 15 minutes with pets," "Treat a bleeding injury with no ambulance for an hour."
These targets are your standards for the next steps.

## Update
Review each year, after each event, and when your life or area changes.

## Common errors
- Planning only for the last disaster.
- Ignoring the quiet, slow hazards such as job loss, illness and aging.
- Treating the plan as a one-time job.
- Not asking neighbors and officials.
