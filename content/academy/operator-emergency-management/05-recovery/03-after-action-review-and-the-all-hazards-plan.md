The cycle closes when you learn from what happened and put that into the next plan.

## The after-action review (AAR)
The US Army's AAR asks four questions:
1. **What did we plan to happen?**
2. **What actually happened?**
3. **Why was there a difference?**
4. **What will we do next time (sustain and improve)?**
Rules:
- **Do it soon** while memories are fresh.
- **Everyone speaks,** from the youngest participant to the leader.
- **Focus on the system and the task,** not on blame.
- **Leaders speak last** so they do not influence.
- **Write it down,** with who will do what by when.

## An improvement plan
Turn findings into actions:
- **What:** a specific change (buy a second filter, rewrite the call tree, drill the evacuation).
- **Who:** one owner.
- **When:** a date.
- **Check:** how you will know it is done.
Track until done.

## Update the hazard assessment
New events show new hazards or change likelihoods. Update the list and the rankings.

## Build your all-hazards plan
Bring the course together in a binder or a digital folder. Contents:
1. **Cover page:** purpose, date, people, version.
2. **Hazard assessment:** top five hazards and target capabilities.
3. **Base plan:** roles, decision rules, communications, meeting places.
4. **Functional annexes:** water, food, power, heat, sanitation, medical, security, communications, transport, pets, finance and papers, morale.
5. **Hazard annexes:** one per top hazard.
6. **Checklists and SOGs:** one-page cards.
7. **Inventory and resources.**
8. **Contacts and maps.**
9. **Training and exercise schedule.**
10. **After-action reports and improvement plan.**
Keep a paper copy, a copy in the go-bag, a copy with a trusted person and a protected digital copy.

## The final check
- Can any adult in the household follow the plan with no help from you?
- Has it been exercised in the last year?
- Is every gap on the improvement plan?
- Does each person know their role?

## Maintain
Review the plan every year, after each event and when your life changes. Make it a habit.

> A prepared household is not one that has everything. It is one that knows its risks, has practiced its response and learns from every event.

> This course is for people who have finished Tiers 3 and 2. It builds on them. Nothing here replaces the instructions of emergency services.
