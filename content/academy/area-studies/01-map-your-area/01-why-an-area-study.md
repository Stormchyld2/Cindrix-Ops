An **area study** is your pre-research: a written, organized picture of the place where you live and work, built from active information gathering, analysis and sharing. At Tier 3 it is the foundation for every other plan you make.

## What are you really preparing for?
Not the event itself. You are preparing for the **follow-on effects** of the event.

An earthquake lasts a few minutes. The next days are about no power, a broken water main, closed roads, jammed phone lines, closed stores and hospitals under strain. A winter storm lasts a day. What follows is cold, blocked roads and no heat. These effects are what hurt people, and many of them depend on your location. A bridge, a river, a hill, the age of your pipes and the distance to a hospital all shape what happens to you.

An area study tells you what those effects will probably be, where you stand, and what you can do about it.

## The military idea, in plain words
The military uses a method called **Intelligence Preparation of the Battlefield**. Before planners decide what to do, they study the ground, the weather, the people and the problem. It has four simple steps:

1. **Define the environment.** Mark the edges of the area you care about and decide which features matter.
2. **Describe the effects.** Work out how terrain, weather and the local population help or hinder you.
3. **Evaluate the threats.** Work out what could go wrong and how it would act.
4. **Decide courses of action.** Choose what you will do, and what you will do instead if that fails.

The method is the same for a soldier or a family. Only the questions change. Instead of an enemy force, a civilian's "threats" are things like floods, fires, storms, power cuts, crime and sickness.

## You cannot plan without facts
You cannot answer "what should we do" without knowing "what can we do" and "what needs to be done." The area study gives you those facts in one place, before you are under stress.

## What a good area study gives you
- A map that shows the edges of your home area and the wider area you depend on.
- A list of hazards, ranked by how likely and how serious they are.
- A list of the resources near you: water, food, medical help, fuel, shelter and tools.
- Routes in and out, with alternatives.
- Contact details for the people and offices that matter.
- A set of written plans: Plan A, Plan B, Plan C.
- A schedule to keep it up to date.

## The work is ongoing
An area study is never finished. Roads change, shops close, neighbors move. Plan to review it every quarter and after any big event.

## What this course will not ask you to do
This course is about **places, hazards, services and shared resources**. You will not be asked to build files about private individuals, track your neighbors, or judge people by their religion, politics or background. Doing that is unfair, can be unlawful, and gives you no useful plan. Know what the community offers and needs, and leave people's private lives alone.
