You cannot prepare for everything at once. Start by learning what is most likely to happen **where you live**, and how bad it would be. That tells you where to spend your time and money.

## Step 1: List the hazards
Use your own area's history and public maps. Common hazards include:

- Flood, flash flood, storm surge, dam failure
- Wildfire and smoke
- Earthquake, landslide, volcano
- Hurricane, tornado, severe storm, hail
- Winter storm, ice storm, extreme cold
- Extreme heat and drought
- Power outage and grid failure
- Water contamination or loss of pressure
- Chemical or hazardous materials spill; pipeline or rail accident
- House fire
- Disease outbreak
- Crime and civil unrest
- Cyber or communications outage

## Step 2: Look up public maps and data
All of these are free:

1. **FEMA flood maps.** Search for the FEMA Flood Map Service Center and enter your address to see your flood zone.
2. **FEMA National Risk Index.** An interactive map that scores each county and census tract for natural hazards, based on expected annual loss, social vulnerability and community resilience.
3. **USGS hazard maps.** Earthquake shaking and landslide maps.
4. **NOAA and the National Weather Service.** Storm history, winter weather and heat data for your area.
5. **State and county hazard mitigation plans.** Most counties publish one. It lists the local hazards and past events.
6. **Local wildfire maps.** State forestry agencies publish fire hazard zones.
7. **Chemical sites.** Your local emergency planning committee has information about nearby facilities that store hazardous chemicals.
8. **Your utility.** Ask for your area's outage history.

Also ask people who have lived in the area a long time. They remember floods, fires and storms that the maps may not show.

## Step 3: Rate each hazard
For each hazard, give a score from 1 (low) to 5 (high) for two things:

- **Likelihood:** How likely is it in the next 5 years?
- **Impact:** How bad would it be for us if it happened?

Multiply the two. A rare hazard with a terrible impact (a big earthquake in a fault zone) and a common one with a moderate impact (a multi-day power outage) can both earn a high score.

- Multi-day power outage: likelihood 4, impact 3, score 12
- Winter storm: likelihood 4, impact 3, score 12
- Flood: likelihood 2, impact 5, score 10
- Chemical release: likelihood 1, impact 4, score 4

Rank them from top to bottom. The top three are your **priority hazards.**

## Step 4: Think about the knock-on effects
Each hazard causes other problems. Write them next to each one:

- Winter storm: no heat, road closures, frozen pipes, no stores.
- Flood: drinking water unsafe, mold, evacuation, contaminated food.
- Wildfire: smoke, evacuation orders, closed roads.

These follow-on problems drive what you need to store, learn and plan for.

## Step 5: Look at your vulnerabilities
Add what makes **you** more vulnerable: an elderly parent, a baby, a person on dialysis or oxygen, pets, a house in a flood zone or a dry forest, a long commute, a single road out.

## Use this to decide
Your top three hazards decide your first purchases and training. A family in a flood zone starts with sandbags, a go-bag and a plan to move to high ground. A family on a fault line starts with strapping furniture and a water supply.

## Checklist
- Write a list of 10 or more hazards for your area.
- Look up your flood zone and your area's risk index.
- Read your county's hazard mitigation plan.
- Score likelihood and impact for each hazard.
- Identify your top three hazards.
- Write down the knock-on effects of each.
- List your family's special vulnerabilities.

> Plan first for what is most likely and most damaging where you live, not for what is in the news.
