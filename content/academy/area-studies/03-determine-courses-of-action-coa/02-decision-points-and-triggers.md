The hardest part of any emergency is deciding **when** to act. Many people stay too long because they hope the problem will go away. A **trigger** is a clear condition decided ahead of time that tells you to act. A **decision point** is the place or time where you must choose.

## Why triggers help
- Stress makes you slow and unsure. Pre-decided triggers reduce the amount of thinking you must do.
- A group agrees on the trigger in advance, so nobody argues in the moment.
- Triggers tie your actions to **observable facts**, not feelings.

## What makes a good trigger
1. **Observable.** Anyone can see it: an alert, a measurement, a time, a sign.
2. **Specific.** Not "when it gets bad," but "when the creek reaches the second fence post."
3. **Early enough.** It must give you time to act before it is too late.
4. **Linked to an action.** "When X happens, we do Y."
5. **Agreed.** Everyone in the group knows it.

## Examples
**Evacuation triggers**
- An evacuation order for your area from officials. Leave at once.
- A wildfire reported within [X] miles of your home, or smoke you can see in your valley.
- A flood watch upgraded to a warning, and the creek above a marked level.
- A chemical release warning, with instructions to shelter in place or leave.

**Stay-put triggers**
- A tornado warning: go to your shelter.
- A winter storm with a road closure order: stay home.
- A shelter-in-place order for a chemical or air hazard.

**Escalation triggers**
- No power after 24 hours: switch from Plan A to Plan B.
- No contact with family on the Primary channel after two attempts: switch to Alternate.
- Water pressure drops or the advisory says boil water: switch to stored water.

## Know the alert words
The National Weather Service uses terms you can build triggers on:

- **Watch.** Conditions are favorable for the hazard. **Get ready.**
- **Warning.** The hazard is happening or about to happen. **Act now.**
- **Advisory.** Less serious, but it could still cause inconvenience or danger.

## Leave early
For hazards like wildfire, flood and hurricane, leaving **early** is almost always safer than waiting. Roads clog quickly, and smoke and water make them dangerous. If an official orders an evacuation, go. If you are told to shelter in place, do so.

## Make a trigger card
For each priority hazard, make a card with:

- **Hazard:** (for example, wildfire)
- **Trigger:** (for example, fire within 5 miles, or evacuation warning)
- **Action:** (go-bags in the car, family at the rally point, leave in 30 minutes)
- **Who does what.**
- **Where we go.**
- **Who we tell.**

Keep the cards in the binder and the go-bag.

## Review the triggers
- After every drill and every real event, ask whether the trigger came early enough.
- Adjust it if people were late.

## Checklist
- Write a trigger card for each of your top three hazards.
- Add the NWS watch and warning meanings to each card.
- Agree on the triggers as a family or group.
- Put a copy in every go-bag.
- Review the triggers after every drill.

> A good trigger is something you can see, decided ahead of time, early enough to act.
