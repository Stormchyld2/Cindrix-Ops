# CINDRIXOPS
# COMMUNICATIONS & WATCH OPERATIONS PLAN

**Document:** COPS-COM-001  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Classification:** Household Operational Use  
**Operational Areas:** Home, Temporary Campsite, Evacuation Destination  
**Personnel:** Four-person primary team; expandable to five  
**Communications Service:** Family Radio Service (FRS)  
**Primary Equipment:** Kaito Voyager Ranger handheld transceivers

---

## 1. MISSION AND PURPOSE

Establish reliable, disciplined, and sustainable communications for family security, campsite operations, perimeter inspections, emergency coordination, and personnel accountability.

This plan is designed to function without cellular service, internet access, or commercial electrical power.

**Operational priorities:**

1. Maintain the ability to receive emergency communications at all times.
2. Account for personnel performing watches, inspections, and remote tasks.
3. Conserve battery power without compromising safety.
4. Provide clear procedures for communications failures.
5. Maintain continuity during shift changes.
6. Support four-person and five-person staffing configurations.
7. Document significant events without creating unnecessary administrative work.

**Operating principle:** Communication reliability takes priority over radio silence, rigid schedules, and battery conservation.

## 3. FRS CHANNEL ALLOCATION PLAN

### 3.1 Channel assignments

| Channel | Privacy code | Assignment | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH 01 | OFF / 00 | PRIMARY NET | Routine communications, watch reports, emergencies |
| CH 04 | OFF / 00 | ALTERNATE NET | Backup when the primary channel becomes unusable |
| CH 05 | OFF / 00 | OPTIONAL WORK NET | Nonurgent logistics when a separate primary-net monitor remains available |

**Primary channel: CH 01**

This is the default channel for every active operator.

All personnel begin their shifts on the primary channel unless another assignment has been acknowledged.

**Alternate channel: CH 04**

Used when the primary channel experiences sustained interference or cannot support intelligible communication.

The change must be communicated and acknowledged whenever possible.

**Optional work channel: CH 05**

May be used for extended supply coordination, maintenance discussions, or other nonurgent traffic.

It must not isolate someone who needs to receive emergency calls.

### 3.2 Channel selection rules

- Conduct an interference and coverage survey before adopting the assignments.
- Use only channels supported by the actual radios.
- Confirm that displayed channel numbers correspond to the intended FRS frequencies.
- If a selected channel is persistently occupied, choose another supported FRS channel and update the written channel card.
- Do not assume a channel is private or reserved.
- Do not use tones as an encryption system.
- Keep privacy-code filtering disabled on the primary emergency net.
- Never interfere with other legitimate radio users.

FRS is a shared radio service. Conversations can be monitored by anyone with suitable receiving equipment.

### 3.3 Channel change procedure

When a channel becomes unusable:

1. The watch coordinator announces a proposed move to the alternate channel, if communication is still possible.
2. All reachable operators acknowledge.
3. Operators switch to the assigned alternate.
4. Base verifies contact with each operator.
5. The change is entered in the communications log.
6. Operators who cannot be reached follow the lost-communications procedure.

Operators must not independently move between channels without an agreed reason or procedure.

## 4. COMMUNICATIONS ROLES

### 4.1 Watch coordinator

The person currently responsible for base communications.

Responsibilities:

- Monitor the primary FRS channel.
- Receive and acknowledge reports.
- Maintain the communications log.
- Track personnel operating away from base.
- Coordinate shift turnover.
- Initiate missed-check-in procedures.
- Monitor weather warnings using separate receiving equipment when practical.
- Maintain access to emergency contacts and site information.

The watch coordinator does not need to remain physically seated at a desk for the entire shift but must maintain reliable access to communications.

### 4.2 Mobile operator

Any person performing an inspection, patrol, supply trip, or remote task.

Responsibilities:

- Carry and test assigned communications equipment.
- State destination and expected return.
- Maintain the agreed communications plan.
- Report significant changes in conditions.
- Notify base of delays whenever possible.
- Request assistance promptly when necessary.

### 4.3 Relief operator

A person temporarily assuming watch or communications responsibilities so another operator can eat, rest, use the restroom, or address an urgent matter.

Responsibility must be explicitly transferred.

**An unattended radio does not constitute an active watch.**

## 5. SIX-HOUR WATCH SCHEDULE

### 5.1 Four-person configuration

| Shift | Hours | Assigned operator |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 0000–0600 | Operator A |
| Bravo | 0600–1200 | Operator B |
| Charlie | 1200–1800 | Operator C |
| Delta | 1800–0000 | Operator D |

This provides one designated watch operator at all times.

The operator's responsibilities include communications monitoring, general site observation, and coordination of safety checks.

A watch operator should not be assigned a remote patrol that leaves the communications base unattended unless another person explicitly takes over base monitoring.

### 5.2 Rotation of personnel

Avoid permanently assigning the same individual to the overnight shift.

Use a rotating watchbill that distributes night duty and provides predictable recovery time.

For example, personnel can move forward by one shift after a suitable rest period or rotation cycle, rather than changing shifts every day without regard to sleep.

The next watchbill must be published before the current one expires.

### 5.3 Five-person configuration

When a fifth person is available, use that additional capacity to improve safety, relief coverage, and sleep opportunities.

Recommended roles:

- Four scheduled six-hour watch positions.
- One designated relief/support position per daily cycle.
- Rotate relief responsibilities among all five people.

The relief operator may cover meals, breaks, appointments, unexpected fatigue, and short periods when the primary watch operator needs assistance.

This configuration is preferable to creating additional unnecessary patrols merely because another person is available.

### 5.4 Shift turnover checklist

Incoming and outgoing operators complete the following:

- [ ] Confirm incoming operator is present and fit for duty.
- [ ] Identify personnel currently away from base.
- [ ] Confirm primary and alternate radio channels.
- [ ] Perform a short radio check.
- [ ] Check battery condition and charging equipment.
- [ ] Transfer current weather and hazard information.
- [ ] Identify unresolved incidents or maintenance problems.
- [ ] Review scheduled returns and pending check-ins.
- [ ] Sign or initial the watch log.

The outgoing watch operator remains responsible until the incoming operator explicitly accepts the handover.

## 6. PERSONNEL CHECK-IN PROCEDURES

### 6.1 Types of check-ins

**Shift accountability**

Completed at every six-hour watch turnover.

Confirms who has responsibility, what equipment is available, and whether any issues remain unresolved.

**Remote-personnel accountability**

Used whenever someone leaves the immediate base area for an activity that requires communications coverage.

**Task completion**

Confirms an inspection, maintenance activity, supply collection, or other assignment has finished.

**Emergency communications**

May occur at any time and overrides all routine reporting.

### 6.2 Remote check-in frequency

| Situation | Recommended approach |
|---|---|
| Operator at base | Continuous availability; verbal handover as required |
| Short task within immediate sight/hearing | Departure and return notification |
| Routine remote inspection | Agree on expected return and periodic contact, initially around every 30–60 minutes |
| Extended remote work | Agree on contact interval appropriate to terrain and risk, generally no longer than two hours without another reliable accountability method |
| Hazardous or unfamiliar conditions | More frequent check-ins or a buddy system |
| Emergency | Immediate communication whenever possible |

The watch coordinator records the expected return and next contact time.

A missed check-in is evaluated in context rather than treated automatically as a security incident.

### 6.3 Standard departure report

**Example:**

“Base, this is Patrol One. Departing for the south boundary inspection. Two personnel, radios operational. Expected return 1430. Over.”

**Base acknowledgement:**

“Patrol One, Base. Two personnel, south boundary, expected return 1430. Copy. Over.”

### 6.4 Standard status report

“Base, Patrol One. Inspection complete. No new hazards observed. Returning to camp. Over.”

### 6.5 Standard arrival report

“Base, Patrol One. Returned to camp. Both personnel accounted for. Out.”

Use short, unambiguous messages. Do not broadcast unnecessary personal details, financial information, or descriptions of sensitive supplies.

## 7. RADIO OPERATING PROCEDURES

### 7.1 Voice communications

1. Listen before transmitting.
2. Press push-to-talk.
3. Pause briefly before speaking.
4. Identify the station being called.
5. Identify yourself.
6. State the message clearly.
7. Release push-to-talk to receive a reply.
8. Confirm critical information.

**Example:**

“Base, this is Logistics One. Request assistance moving water containers. Over.”

“Logistics One, Base. Assistance available in ten minutes. Over.”

“Base, Logistics One. Copy. Out.”

### 7.2 Standard terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| This is | Identifies the transmitting station |
| Over | Message complete; reply expected |
| Out | Conversation complete; no reply expected |
| Copy | Message received |
| Say again | Repeat the last message |
| Stand by | Wait for a response |
| Correction | Previous statement was incorrect |
| Affirmative | Yes |
| Negative | No |
| Emergency | Immediate priority communications |
| Radio check | Request for a reception assessment |

Avoid relying on elaborate jargon or secret code words when plain language communicates the situation more accurately.

### 7.3 Prohibited operating practices

- No intentionally false emergency messages.
- No unnecessary long transmissions.
- No deliberate interference with other radio users.
- No assumption that communications are encrypted.
- No deliberate withholding of urgent safety information.
- No unattended monitoring arrangement presented as continuous human supervision.
- No transmission of sensitive personal information unless necessary.

## 8. EMERGENCY COMMUNICATIONS

### 8.1 Incident classifications

**RED — Immediate emergency**

Examples:

- Serious injury or medical emergency.
- Fire threatening people or structures.
- Immediate life-threatening hazard.
- Missing person with urgent safety concerns.

Response:

1. Announce “Emergency” clearly.
2. Identify the location.
3. Describe the hazard.
4. State the assistance required.
5. Keep the channel available for emergency communication.
6. Contact public emergency services when available.
7. Document the event once immediate needs are addressed.

**AMBER — Significant problem**

Examples:

- Failed radio.
- Low remaining power.
- Delayed return.
- Equipment malfunction.
- New safety hazard requiring action.

Response:

Report the problem and requested assistance. The watch coordinator determines whether additional action is needed.

**GREEN — Routine status**

Indicates an assigned check or task has been completed without an identified issue.

Green does not mean that the entire property is verified secure.

### 8.2 Emergency message format

Transmit the following information:

1. **Emergency announcement**
2. **Who is calling**
3. **Location**
4. **Nature of emergency**
5. **Number of people affected**
6. **Assistance required**

**Example:**

“Emergency, emergency. Base, this is Patrol One. Medical emergency near the south trail entrance. One injured adult, conscious and breathing. Request first-aid assistance. Over.”

The receiving operator repeats critical details and coordinates the appropriate response.

### 8.3 Communications priority

| Priority | Traffic |
|---|---|
| 1 | Immediate danger to life |
| 2 | Urgent medical or safety information |
| 3 | Lost communications and overdue-personnel coordination |
| 4 | Weather warnings and developing hazards |
| 5 | Routine patrol or task reports |
| 6 | Logistics and nonurgent conversation |

A lower-priority transmission should stop when emergency traffic is announced.

## 9. MISSED CHECK-IN AND LOST COMMUNICATIONS

### 9.1 Missed check-in

When a remote operator misses an agreed contact time:

**Step 1 — Initial contact**

Call the operator on the primary channel.

**Step 2 — Repeat contact**

Attempt another call after a short interval appropriate to the situation.

**Step 3 — Alternate communications**

Try the agreed alternate FRS channel, phone service if available, or another established method.

**Step 4 — Evaluate circumstances**

Review the operator's destination, expected return, terrain, weather, last communication, and any known hazards.

**Step 5 — Escalate**

Notify the responsible coordinator and initiate a welfare check or request emergency assistance if circumstances warrant.

Never automatically assume an attack or hostile activity merely because a check-in was missed.

### 9.2 Lost radio communications

If an operator cannot reach base:

1. Check volume, battery, channel and privacy-code settings.
2. Retry from a safe position with better reception.
3. Attempt the alternate channel.
4. Use another available means of communication.
5. Return to the previously agreed meeting point or base when safe.
6. Avoid unnecessary travel in dangerous weather, darkness, or hazardous terrain simply to restore radio contact.

If the operator is injured or stranded, safety and appropriate emergency signaling take priority over the routine return procedure.

### 9.3 Radio blackout at base

If the primary base radio fails:

- Switch to a functioning spare if available.
- Reestablish the primary listening position.
- Check charging and power connections.
- Notify reachable operators.
- Document the failure.
- Use an agreed alternative accountability method until communications are restored.

## 10. POWER AND BATTERY MANAGEMENT

### 10.1 Power priorities

**Primary:** Fully charged internal batteries and normal USB charging.

**Secondary:** Stored electrical power from solar equipment or a power bank.

**Emergency:** Hand-crank generator.

Do not assume a specific number of minutes of operation per minute of cranking without testing the actual equipment.

### 10.2 Battery management rules

- Begin each watch with adequate battery reserve.
- Check radio power at every shift turnover.
- Maintain a charging location protected from rain and excessive heat.
- Keep charging equipment accessible.
- Recharge idle radios when power is available.
- Test backup charging methods periodically.
- Maintain at least one communications-ready radio at base.
- Prioritize emergency receiving capability over unnecessary radio use.

### 10.3 Low-battery procedure

When a radio reaches its low-battery indication:

1. Inform base when practical.
2. Return for charging or exchange equipment when safe.
3. Avoid unnecessary transmission.
4. Use emergency charging if no other option exists.
5. Record recurring battery or charging failures.

### 10.4 Power-conservation policy

Battery conservation does not mean switching off the only available receiving radio during an active remote assignment.

Conserve power through:

- Low-power settings where supported and adequate for coverage.
- Short transmissions.
- Reduced display brightness.
- Disabled unnecessary alert tones and lights.
- Charging during available solar generation.
- Avoiding unnecessary AM/FM listening on communications-critical radios.

## 11. NOAA WEATHER MONITORING

Weather monitoring is separate from the FRS channel allocation.

### 11.1 West Virginia reference stations

| Station | Frequency | Transmitter |
|---|---|---|
| WXJ85 | 162.550 MHz | Clarksburg |
| KWN35 | 162.475 MHz | Morgantown |
| KWN36 | 162.500 MHz | Greggs Knob |
| WXM74 | 162.450 MHz | Sutton |

Reception varies by terrain. Select the station that can be received reliably at the specific operating site.

A separate NOAA weather-alert receiver is preferred at base so routine weather monitoring does not interrupt the primary FRS communications watch.

### 11.2 Weather monitoring schedule

| Period | Activity |
|---|---|
| Morning turnover | Review forecast and active alerts |
| Midday | Update forecast if conditions warrant |
| Evening turnover | Review overnight forecast |
| Before extended outdoor work | Check current weather |
| During active warnings | Monitor updates as conditions require |

Weather monitoring must increase during severe weather. It must not be restricted to one five-minute daily window.

### 11.3 Severe weather reporting

Record:

- Date and time.
- Receiving station.
- Warning type.
- Affected location.
- Warning expiration.
- Expected hazards.
- Required protective actions.

**Tennessee destination:** Program and verify the locally appropriate NOAA transmitter after identifying the actual deployment location. Do not assume West Virginia stations provide coverage there.

## 12. RADIO COVERAGE TESTING

Before relying on FRS communications at a location, complete a site survey.

### 12.1 Required test locations

- Base operations area.
- Sleeping area.
- Food preparation and supply area.
- Vehicle parking.
- Property entrances.
- Boundary inspection points.
- Planned garden and work locations.
- Low-ground locations.
- Tree-covered areas.
- Any remote area where personnel may work alone.

### 12.2 Signal quality scale

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Clear and fully understandable |
| 4 | Understandable with minor noise |
| 3 | Understandable with occasional repetition |
| 2 | Unreliable, frequent repetition required |
| 1 | Barely understandable |
| 0 | No usable communication |

Record the rating in both directions, where possible.

A location with unreliable radio coverage must be marked on the site's operational map.

Where communication cannot be established, modify the personnel accountability method instead of assuming the radio will work in an emergency.

## 15. TRAINING REQUIREMENTS

Each operator must demonstrate the ability to:

1. Turn the assigned radio on and off.
2. Adjust volume and channel.
3. Disable privacy-code filtering.
4. Make and acknowledge a routine radio call.
5. Report an emergency in plain language.
6. Change to the alternate channel.
7. Recognize a low-battery indication.
8. Recharge the radio using USB.
9. Use the hand-crank backup.
10. Receive a NOAA weather broadcast.
11. Follow the lost-communications procedure.
12. Complete a basic communications log entry.

Conduct periodic practice sessions and record any problems identified.

Training should include realistic failures such as a discharged battery, an occupied channel, and a location with poor reception.

## 17. FINAL OPERATIONAL RULES

1. Someone is responsible for receiving emergency communications whenever personnel are operating remotely.
2. One primary channel is used for normal operations.
3. An alternate channel is established and tested.
4. FRS privacy codes are not encryption.
5. Emergency traffic takes priority over routine traffic.
6. Remote personnel have an agreed accountability plan.
7. Watch responsibility is explicitly transferred.
8. No communications procedure depends on internet connectivity.
9. Weather monitoring does not interrupt emergency receiving without backup coverage.
10. Battery conservation never takes priority over immediate life safety.
11. All personnel know the communications failure procedures.
12. Radio coverage is tested rather than assumed.
13. Equipment shortcomings are reported and documented.
14. Safety-related communications use clear, ordinary language.
15. Original records and equipment inventories are maintained without unnecessary duplication.
